Intelligent Construction Systems (ICS) are high-speed nondestructive quality assurance methods that evaluate pavement properties continuously during construction. By sensing concrete temperature, moisture, thickness, dowel alignment, curing effectiveness, support conditions, workability, air content, density, smoothness, texture, and noise in real time, immediate adjustments can be made to ensure the highest quality finished product meeting performance specifications. [1]

CP Road Map Overview

CP Road Map Track 3 is dedicated to developing 12 specific ICS technologies and methods, plus quality assurance tests, specifications, and implementation workshops. Many problem statements relate to both Track 1 (Materials and Mixes) and Track 2 (Design Guide). [1]

Integrated Intelligent Paving Systems

Integrated intelligent paving systems can predict future pavement performance by incorporating sensors that monitor concrete temperature, workability, air properties, mix constituent proportions, strength, slab thickness, dowel bar alignment, curing effectiveness, pavement support, smoothness, texture, and noise potential [1]. By enabling automatic adjustments to the paving process, these systems can achieve intended design lives of 30, 40, or 50 years [1].

Additionally, maturity methods relate concrete temperature and moisture history to strength, which allows contractors to assess the timing for opening pavement to traffic [1]. However, precise guidelines are still needed regarding maturity measurement intervals, accuracy, monitoring locations, and sensor installation constraints such as minimum cover requirements [1].

Concrete Thickness Sensing Technologies

Concrete thickness sensing aims to replace time-consuming core sampling with nondestructive techniques, such as physical probes, impact-echo, or ground-penetrating radar, to measure slab thickness at intervals as short as 1 ft [1]. This continuous real-time measurement capability allows for increased sample density while decreasing testing costs [1]. Various technologies facilitate these measurements, including laser scanning systems that can determine pavement thickness by comparing pre-paving base scans with post-paving surface scans to generate virtual core measurements with low variability [2].

Anticipated vision of laser scanning concrete thickness profiler [2]

Additionally, electromagnetic sensing methods utilize embedded metal plates in the subbase or rebar cages to enable real-time thickness measurements via paver-mounted sensors [2]. Similarly, eddy current testing is largely insensitive to concrete composition, though it requires daily calibration against manual depth measurements to maintain accuracy [2].

Eddy current sensor for concrete thickness measurement [2]

Dowel Bar Alignment and Installation

To address the need to detect misalignment caused by displacement during paving operations, the MIT-SCAN-2 device provides a method for obtaining position information efficiently for dowel bar alignment sensing. Typical allowable tolerances vary among agencies, with some specifying limits of 0.25 inches per 18 inches or 3 inches per 8 feet both horizontally and vertically [1]. Furthermore, dowel bar ripple manifests as disturbances covering approximately 5 feet of pavement that repeat every 20 feet, with peak-to-trough elevation differences typically around 0.25 inches [3].

Dowel bar ripple [3]

Vibration from dowel-bar inserter operation can also cause discrepancies between real-time profiles (approximately 100 in/mi) and hardened quality control profiles (approximately 65 in/mi) [4].

Surface Texture and Noise Management

For tined pavements, texture depth and groove width are critical parameters that directly influence noise generation levels [5]. To achieve quieter surfaces, artificial turf drag texturing should be completed as soon as possible after finishing but before the concrete attains its initial set [6], and transversely tined surfaces meeting noise targets typically have nominal tine spacings of 12.5 mm (0.5 in.) or less [6]. To manage these levels, tire-pavement noise sensing equipment can be mounted to the paving train immediately behind finishing and texturing operations to predict noise potential in real time from surface texture measurements on fresh concrete; this allows for immediate correction of in-place plastic concrete or automatic adjustments to the texturing process to meet stringent noise restrictions [1].

Influence of surface characteristics on vehicle performance. [5]

A target A-weighted tire-pavement noise level of approximately 101 to 102 dB (ref 1pW/m²) measured using OBSI at 60 mph serves as a reasonable threshold for new concrete pavements [6]. OBSI testing utilizes 528-ft test segments to evaluate tire-pavement noise, with gaps permitted to avoid structures or steep grades that invalidate measurements [6]. Furthermore, diamond grinding equipment must weigh at least 35,000 pounds with a minimum grinding width of 4 feet to effectively reduce noise without causing spalling or aggregate fractures [6].

Normalized distributions of OBSI noise levels for conventional concrete pavement textures [6]

Pavement Smoothness and Profile Measurement

Theoretical models predict that a stringline spacing of 25 ft provides better protection against survey errors than 50 ft spacing, though field validation is required to confirm these cost-benefit relationships [5]. During paving operations, profiling devices can be referenced to longitudinal stationing to identify and modify surface imperfections in both plastic and hardened concrete states [3], and these devices can also detect string line disturbances, which have been identified as a factor that degrades pavement smoothness [3]. Furthermore, the GSI device includes extraneous measurement content with a wavelength near three feet, which influences IRI values due to its sensitivity to roughness in that range [3].

Reduction in roughness by hand finishing [3]

Real-time smoothness (RTS) systems utilizing devices such as the GOMACO GSI or Ames RTP can detect short-wavelength surface irregularities imparted by oscillating correcting beams, which are subsequently removed during auto-float and hand finishing operations [4]. Additionally, field trials indicate that real-time IRI measurements are significantly influenced by joint spacing (inserted dowels) and concrete load spacing in addition to long-wavelength roughness from stringless total station swaps [4].

Sensor Protection and Field Implementation

Sensors should be shrouded when employed in the slip-form paving train to prevent concrete and curing material splatter from damaging equipment or adversely impacting profile measurements. [3]

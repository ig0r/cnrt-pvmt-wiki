Noise Zones

Part of: Pavement Noise is-classification-system-of
Noise Zones is a classification system of Pavement Noise.
classificationmeasurementnoisepavement
Also known as: OBSI noise zones, tire-pavement noise zones
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Noise zones are categorical classifications used to group environments or surfaces based on their acoustic characteristics, serving as a framework for evaluating and managing sound levels. In the context of pavement engineering, these zones categorize tire-pavement interaction noise into low, mid, and high ranges to guide design targets and mitigation strategies [1] [3]. Physically, such zones are defined by regions where ambient sound levels are reduced because surrounding materials absorb, scatter, or attenuate acoustic energy through mechanisms like internal damping and phase cancellation [5].

Key findings

  • Diamond grinding and controlled drag textures reliably achieve noise levels within the mid-noise quality zone, whereas transverse tining often produces higher noise levels that fall into the avoid category [1] [2] [6].
  • Concrete pavement noise levels are categorized into three distinct zones based on Overall Sound Intensity (OBSI): a low-noise ‘innovation’ zone below approximately 99–100 dBA, a mid-noise ‘quality’ zone between 99–105 dBA, and a high-noise ‘avoid’ zone above 104–105 dBA [1] [2].
  • Joint treatment and surface condition critically influence noise performance, with deteriorated joints driving pavements into the high-noise avoid category even if the texture itself is acceptable [1] [2].
  • Diamond grinding serves as the most reliable method for reducing noise in existing high-noise pavements and achieving low-noise targets in new construction while maintaining friction and ride quality [1] [6].
  • Pervious concrete overlays produce significantly lower noise levels (96–98 dBA) compared to conventional dense concrete surfaces (100–110 dBA), placing them in the low-noise innovation zone [3].

Noise Zone Characteristics and Porous Surface Performance

Noise zones are categorical classifications used to group pavement sound levels into broad performance categories, typically based on observed sound pressure measurements [1]. These zones provide a standardized framework for evaluating tire-pavement interaction noise and determining appropriate design targets or mitigation strategies [1] [3]. Porous surfaces reduce acoustic energy within these zones by utilizing air-filled voids to scatter sound waves and convert mechanical energy into heat through internal damping [5].

The following figure illustrates how these acoustic interactions manifest as the reflection of sound waves resulting from moving vehicles.

Reflection of sound waves resulting from moving vehicles [5]

The figure illustrates the difference in acoustic behavior between a dense pavement surface and a porous one. In the first scenario, sound waves reflect off a solid surface to reach an observer, whereas in the second, the open structure of the porous pavement causes a difference in arrival time between direct and reflected sound waves. This mechanism decreases noise level intensity, allowing porous pavements to absorb sound energy within specific acoustic zones.

Research problem — Conventional concrete pavements routinely generate excessive tire-pavement noise, creating high-noise zones that degrade community livability and fail to meet regulatory expectations for quieter environments [4] [5] [6]. Existing concrete textures predominantly fall into mid- or high-noise categories, with a lack of viable solutions in the lowest noise zone and excessive variability preventing reliable achievement of low-noise targets [1] [2]. There is a critical need to develop and validate quieter pavement technologies, such as porous or pervious concrete overlays, that can effectively reduce sound intensity while maintaining necessary durability and safety standards [3] [5].

State of the art — The current state of practice for concrete pavement noise management is organized around a three-zone framework that categorizes pavements by on-board sound intensity (OBSI) levels, with designers targeting the mid-range ‘Quality’ band for new construction [1] [2]. While conventional textures are the standard for maintaining noise within acceptable limits, innovative porous surfaces like pervious concrete and exposed-aggregate systems show promise for reaching lower-noise zones but remain largely experimental pending broader specification and cost-effectiveness analysis [1] [3] [5].

Findings from the reports

  • Concrete pavement noise levels are categorized into three distinct zones based on Overall Sound Intensity (OBSI): Zone 1 (low noise, below ~99-100 dBA), Zone 2 (mid/quality noise, ~99-105 dBA), and Zone 3 (high noise, above ~104-105 dBA) [1] [2].
  • No conventional dense concrete textures consistently achieve the low-noise levels of Zone 1; this zone is currently reserved for innovative solutions such as pervious concrete [1] [2].
  • Zone 2 represents the practical quality range where most existing dense concrete textures reside, and new pavements can consistently achieve levels below 103 dBA through diligent quality control [1] [2].
  • Diamond grinding and drag textures reliably produce the lowest noise levels within Zone 2 while maintaining necessary friction and smoothness requirements [2] [6].
  • Zone 3 pavements exhibit high noise levels due to aggressive textures or deteriorated joints and are identified as candidates for immediate rehabilitation or replacement [1] [2].
  • Transverse tining textures generally produce higher noise levels and are less likely to meet low-noise targets compared to longitudinal tining, grinding, or drag methods [2] [6].
  • Diamond grinding serves as an effective mitigation strategy that significantly reduces source noise levels in high-noise pavements [1].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a three-zone classification framework for concrete pavement noise, categorizing performance into low, mid, and high levels to guide specification development and policy decisions [1] [2]. This approach uniquely couples on-board sound intensity measurements with laser-based texture scanning to create a data-driven tool for evaluating existing pavements and directing future research toward quieter surface designs [2]. Reproducing the analysis requires constructing representative test sections using specific texture methods and mix designs, then applying standardized noise measurement protocols with environmental corrections to consistently assign results to the defined zones [1] [3] [6].

Across the reviewed reports, concrete pavement noise is consistently categorized into three distinct zones based on Overall Sound Intensity (OBSI), with Zone 2 (~99–105 dBA) representing the practical “quality” range where conventional textures like diamond grinding and dragging can reliably achieve target levels under strict quality control. While Zone 3 (>104–105 dBA) identifies high-noise pavements characterized by aggressive textures and joint deterioration that require immediate remediation, Zone 1 (<99–100 dBA) remains an innovation target currently unattainable with conventional dense concrete but achievable through porous surfaces like pervious concrete overlays. Synthesizing field data reveals that while diamond grinding and controlled textures are the most reliable methods for achieving low-noise performance in conventional pavements, porous pervious concrete offers a distinct pathway to significantly lower noise levels (96–98 dBA) compared to traditional concrete (100–110 dBA). [1] [2] [3] [6]

Implications — Agencies should designate mid-range noise levels as the realistic performance envelope for conventional concrete, mandating tighter construction tolerances and texture controls to keep new pavements within this acceptable range [1] [2]. High-noise zones must be treated as prohibited areas, requiring the removal or retrofitting of existing sections through diamond grinding, overlay, or joint repair to bring them into compliance [1] [2]. Specifiers should prioritize high-porosity materials like pervious concrete in noise-sensitive areas, as their uniform void structure provides significant acoustic benefits when mix designs and placement practices ensure consistent quality [3]. Future specifications can leverage prescriptive or end-result controls to select textures with higher quiet-performance probabilities, ensuring that pavements meet target noise levels while supporting durability and friction requirements [6].

This classification system serves as a framework for evaluating acoustic characteristics to guide design targets and mitigation strategies, directly illustrating the concept of noise zones in pavement engineering.

Limitations — The noise zone boundaries remain approximate guides rather than definitive limits, as they overlap due to measurement variability and are currently based on data from a single tire type that may shift with future conversions [1] [2]. Current concrete solutions for the lowest noise zone are limited to experimental pervious pavements, and high variability in texture measurements makes simple percent-within-limits specifications risky for payment or compliance purposes [1]. Observed noise performance is heavily influenced by construction consistency and surface condition rather than material properties alone, while the reliance on emerging testing protocols requires strict quality control to ensure reliable results across varying pavement ages [3] [6].

Internal Curing

Internal curing is a concrete technology that provides reservoirs of water within the concrete matrix to promote continued hydration without increasing the initial water-to-cement ratio. This technique improves concrete durability and reduces shrinkage cracking risk.

Introduction and Fundamental Concept

Internal curing works by:

  1. Incorporating saturated lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) particles into the concrete mix
  2. These particles act as internal water reservoirs
  3. As the concrete dries and hydration progresses, water is released from the reservoirs
  4. This water helps continue hydration throughout the concrete volume
  5. Unlike surface curing, internal curing benefits the entire concrete volume

The use of highly absorptive aggregates, such as slag or porous limestone, can provide internal curing benefits by releasing stored water to promote cement hydration beyond the normal curing period, thereby increasing strength and reducing permeability. [1]

(b). Core-determined concrete pavement condition showing type of coarse aggregate [1]

Internal curing represents a significant advancement in concrete technology, enabling more durable and sustainable concrete structures through improved hydration management.

Internal Curing Agents and Mechanisms

The use of lightweight fine aggregates as internal curing agents can be considered a technique for shrinkage reduction in materials exhibiting high early-age shrinkage, such as latex-modified concrete with very early strength gain [2]. Similarly, superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) can serve as internal curing agents by absorbing and holding additional water within the cement paste, which provides sacrificial water for evaporation and improves hydration [3]. To provide the necessary water absorption and retention capabilities for internal curing applications, SAPs must maintain a three-dimensional network structure [4], and effective SAPs must be capable of desorbing stored water when the relative humidity in the surrounding concrete matrix drops below approximately 94% [4]. One specific SAP formulation, a crushed crystalline partial sodium salt of cross-linked polypromancic acid rated at 2000 times absorption for pure water, has been used in pervious concrete mixtures to eliminate the need for plastic curing [3].

Classification of SAP based on different characteristics [4]

Technical Basis and Material Science

First discussed by Philleo (1991) and validated through extensive research, internal curing technology is most beneficial in low water-to-cement ratio mixtures [5], particularly those with a water-to-cement ratio lower than 0.42 where the risk of desiccation is high and external water cannot easily penetrate into the concrete [5]. While dry or unsaturated absorptive aggregates can negatively impact concrete by depriving nearby cement particles of water needed for extensive hydration, which compromises both strength and porosity [1], internal curing provides distributed curing throughout the concrete volume that complements conventional surface curing methods and enables more sustainable concrete mixtures. This process mitigates autogenous shrinkage by replenishing pore water consumed during hydration reactions through the release of stored water from porous aggregates distributed throughout the concrete bulk [6]. When lightweight aggregates are used as internal curing agents, their fully rigid behavior allows for the displacement of stored water under negative capillary pressure without deforming, unlike early-age concrete which is a weak solid [6].

High water-cement ratio and cracking left half of the cold joint [1]

As measured by thermal gravimetric analysis, internal curing can increase the calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) content by approximately 20% at 28 days compared to conventional concrete [7]. This method improves the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) by enhancing the degree of hydration in that critical region [5], and the reduced modulus of elasticity in internally cured concrete decreases stresses under shrinkage strains, contributing to improved cracking resistance [7]. Furthermore, internal curing alters the moisture gradient distribution within concrete slabs compared to control mixes due to its unique response to self-drying and water absorption processes [8]. Specifically, research indicates that internally cured concrete containing pre-wetted lightweight fine aggregates affects moisture gradient distribution and subsequent deformation of pavement slabs [8], as the presence of internal curing agents can mitigate upward slab curvature caused by negative moisture gradients where the bottom of the slab retains higher moisture content than the top surface [8].

Concrete slab warping and curling [5]

Key Benefits: Material Properties and Durability

Internal curing provides various material property improvements, including reduced autogenous shrinkage, reduced stiffness, increased strength, lower permeability, and improved durability. These enhancements lead to more efficient hydration of cementitious systems, enhanced performance of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), reduced transport properties (diffusion, sorptivity), and an increased service life of concrete structures. Specifically, internal curing reduces plastic shrinkage cracking in concrete by addressing early-age volume changes that occur before the concrete has fully set [9]. The incorporation of SAPs can further reduce plastic shrinkage cracks by replacing water lost at the surface with evaporation from the polymer reservoirs [4]. Additionally, internal curing provides additional benefits such as improved freeze-thaw resistance, reduced curling/warping, and lower shrinkage. Internal curing can also mitigate damage associated with alkali-silica reaction (ASR) by providing space for ASR gel expansion and altering the pore solution composition through dilution, all while maintaining comparable strength and freeze-thaw performance to conventional concrete [9].

Drying shrinkage and autogenous versus concrete age in conventional concrete (left) and high-performance concrete (right) after internal curing [4]

Furthermore, internally cured concrete typically exhibits a lower coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE), which helps mitigate the negative effects of increasing joint spacing on pavement distress performance [7]. The use of lightweight fine aggregates (LWFAs) for internal curing can reduce the weight/density of concrete beams by approximately 5% compared to conventional concrete, while simultaneously increasing compressive strength [10]. For instance, high-performance concrete with LWFAs has demonstrated a 28-day average compressive strength of 6,870 psi against a required strength of 4,000 psi [10]. Such concrete can also exhibit improved durability indicators, such as a surface resistivity of 13.90 kΩ·cm, which falls within the moderate range for chloride ion penetration resistance [10]. Regarding SAPs, they can decrease the permeability of healed cracks up to 100 μm wide by swelling and filling these voids when water penetrates the system [4], though they can also act as physical air voids after desorption, which may result in higher spacing between voids or voids filled with hydration products [4].

Implementation: Materials and Construction Requirements

Internal curing agents can include porous lightweight aggregates, superabsorbent polymers (SAPs), natural pozzolans such as rice husk ash and natural zeolite [6], or pre-wetted wood fibers in addition to lightweight aggregates and superabsorbent polymers [5]. Mixture proportioning for internal curing typically involves replacing approximately 30% of the fine aggregate volume with prewetted lightweight fine aggregate (typically 20-30% replacement of fine aggregate) to provide an internal water volume equivalent to the chemical shrinkage of the cementitious materials [9]. The required amount of internal curing water is generally estimated at 7 pounds per 100 pounds of cementitious materials, which is then used to calculate the necessary mass and volume of lightweight aggregate based on its absorption capacity [9]. The release of internal curing water stops the buildup of excessive negative pressure in capillaries and prevents air from infiltrating the pore system, thereby mitigating plastic shrinkage cracking [6].

Conceptual illustration of volumetric components in a plain and internally cured mixture [9]

The use of lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) requires adjusted mix proportions to account for LWFA properties, as well as proper soaking and drainage before mixing; specifically, LWFA requires 48-hour soaking and overnight drainage [9]. Construction considerations include quality control measures for material preparation and potential adjustments to mixing and placement procedures. However, LWFA preconditioning has been identified as the biggest logistical challenge for larger projects [5]. Furthermore, absorptive aggregates can interfere with air entrainment systems by drawing water toward them during mixing, which may carry non-vinsol resin-based air-entraining admixtures to the aggregate surface where they accumulate and cause bubble coalescence [1]. To mitigate this, proper preconditioning of slag aggregates through diligent wetting of stockpiles is critical to prevent them from entering an absorptive state during construction, which ensures that concrete strength, low permeability, and effective air systems are maintained [1].

Air bubble coalescence in Core 6 [1]

Performance Evidence: Laboratory and Field Testing

Laboratory Testing

  • Improved compressive and tensile strength development
  • Reduced permeability (rapid chloride permeability testing)
  • Lower stress rates in restrained shrinkage testing
  • Enhanced surface resistivity

Field Applications

  • Bridge deck construction (Buchanan County, Iowa example)
  • Multiple successful implementations nationwide
  • Long-term performance monitoring

Pavement Applications and Economic Viability

Lifecycle cost analysis demonstrates economic viability for jointed plain concrete pavements, as it reduces maintenance requirements over pavement service life and enables a thickness reduction (1 inch) while maintaining performance. Although lifecycle cost analysis indicates that internally cured pavements may have initial construction costs approximately 3.2% higher than conventionally cured pavements of the same thickness [7], sensitivity analysis shows NPV savings robust to variations in construction cost, discount rate, and maintenance cost.

Cost stream over the lifecycle of a pavement [7]

The use of internal curing allows for a reduction in minimum pavement thickness from 8 inches to 7 inches and from 10 inches to 9 inches while maintaining required performance limits over a 30-year design life [7]. Furthermore, internally cured concrete can compensate for the performance effects of increasing joint spacing from 15 feet to 20 feet, mitigating increases in the International Roughness Index (IRI) value over the design life [7].

IRI of conventionally cured pavements over design life [7]

Case Study: Iowa Field Implementation

Iowa Field Implementation (IHRB TR-746, 2021)

  • Field demonstration at two Iowa overlay sites: Washington County and Winneshiek County
  • IC sections showed reduced warping and curling: 0.9 mm vs. 2.2 mm maximum winter movement (Washington County)
  • Enhanced hydration for ~1 month after placement: IC sections maintained higher temperatures than CC sections
  • Improved permeability: surface resistivity 12-19% higher in IC sections at 90 days
  • Enhanced mechanical properties: compressive strength increased 14-20% at 90 days; modulus of elasticity reduced (desirable for cracking resistance)
  • Life-cycle cost savings: EAA showed ~$1,100/mile annual savings despite 2-3% initial cost increase

Research Directions and Future Applications

Internal curing is particularly valuable for low water-to-cement ratio mixtures, structures at high risk of cracking, applications requiring enhanced durability, and bridge decks exposed to aggressive environments. Beyond bridge decks, internal curing is being evaluated for continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP), whitetopping, and ultra-thin whitetopping applications to reduce built-in stress caused by restrained shrinkage and moisture gradients [9].

Adiabatic Calorimeter

Part of: Calorimetry
calorimetryhydrationtesting
Also known as: adiabatic calorimetry
Created 2026-05-20 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

A Calorimetry device that measures Heat of Hydration under near-zero heat loss conditions (temperature loss <0.02 K/h). Heat loss is prevented by controlling the temperature of the surrounding environment using water, air, or heated containers. [1]

Overview and Core Characteristics

  • Adiabatic curves are the most suitable starting points for temperature calculation in hardening structures

Semi-Adiabatic Calorimeter Performance

Semi-adiabatic calorimeters permit a maximum heat loss of less than 100 J/(h⋅K) and typically yield mean temperature rises that are 2–3% lower than adiabatic test results [2]. These devices can predict adiabatic temperature rise by measuring and accounting for the specific heat loss during testing [2]. A specific example is the AdiaCal semi-adiabatic calorimeter, which is used to measure the heat of hydration in concrete with test results typically recorded from initial setup until peak temperature is reached; for instance, tests have shown peak temperatures reaching approximately 105°F at 14.6 hours or up to 115°F depending on the mix and conditions [3]. Such methods are most convenient for producing continuous heat of hydration curves under conditions close to mass curing and can determine heat evolution of an actual concrete mixture.

AdiaCal calorimetry test equipment for heat of hydration of concrete [3]

Standardization and Testing Protocols

The development of nonproprietary test standards for adiabatic calorimetry aims to create procedures suitable for AASHTO adoption that are practical, cost-effective, and sensitive enough to characterize concrete mixtures [4]. Adiabatic calorimetry is standardized under ASTM C 1679, which governs the testing procedures for heat of hydration in concrete mixtures [3], and the RILEM Round-Robin test program found that specimen size and curing temperature do not significantly affect the adiabatic temperature rise results [1]; specifically, the RILEM Round-Robin showed 50% of adiabatic temperature rise variations within a narrow 2K range.

Temperature increase of mass concrete under adiabatic conditions (adapted from Mindess and Young 1981) [1]

Impact of Mix Components and Admixtures

Adiabatic calorimetry can detect cement-admixture compatibility issues by monitoring heat evolution over time [1], and semi-adiabatic calorimetry serves as an effective method for assessing potential incompatibilities between concrete mixture ingredients, allowing for regular material testing to flag changes in composition or behavior before batch plant operations [3]. Through these methods, it is observed that the addition of fly ash to concrete delays the peak rate of adiabatic temperature rise by approximately 1.5 hours compared to plain cement mixes, whereas slag replacement does not extend the dormant period and may slightly accelerate initial set time [5]. Furthermore, adiabatic calorimetry reveals that supplementary cementitious materials like slag can decrease maximum concrete temperatures without postponing the hydration dormant period, unlike fly ash which extends this phase [5].

Total heat of blended cement with slag at different temperatures (adapted from Ma et al. 1994) [1]

The behavior of various admixtures also significantly influences thermal properties; for instance, superplasticizers mildly retard concrete setting, which reduces early-age adiabatic temperature generation without affecting total heat output [1]. Conversely, accelerating admixtures increase the rate of heat evolution during the setting stage and may also increase heat liberation during the hardening stage depending on their chemical composition [1]. Finally, retarders reduce the early-age heat of hydration and temperature rise, with values typically equalizing to non-retarded mixes after 3-7 days [1].

Effect of superplasticizer on cement hydration (adapted from Jolicoeur et al. 1998) [1]

Fly Ash Hydration Detection

While simple isothermal calorimetry tests can clearly identify a second heat evolution peak associated with fly ash hydration in concrete mixes—a feature that is generally not observed when using AdiaCal or IQ Drum semi-adiabatic calorimeters [6]—other materials exhibit different thermal behaviors. In rapid-set calcium sulfoaluminate (CSA) cement, calorimetry reveals a sharp heat generation peak without a dormant period between hours 5 and 10, indicating extremely fast initial hydration that can lead to thermal cracking if not managed [7].

Isothermal calorimetry results for US 71 (Atlantic, IA) field concrete samples [6]

The addition of citric acid to rapid-set cement extends the dormant period and shifts the peak rate of heat evolution later, whereas latex admixtures do not significantly alter the heat signature or cumulative heat output [7]. Specifically, during the 5-to-10-hour window after mixing, pastes containing citric acid exhibit a rapid increase in cumulative heat from approximately 25 J/g to 180 J/g, which is roughly double the rate observed in conventional pavement cement pastes [7].

Rate of heat generation [7]

Various types of calorimetry are employed depending on the specific testing requirements, such as the Adiabatic Calorimeter, which provides a near-zero heat loss environment (<0.02 K/h) and is best for mass concrete simulation; in these applications, adiabatic calorimetry implicitly measures curing temperature effects on hydration rates, requiring activation energy calculations to convert results to isothermal reference temperatures [1].

Semi-adiabatic calorimeter–IQ drum (www.quadreliservice.com) [1]

Field Applications and Measurement Techniques

In field calorimetry applications, mortar samples sieved from concrete are recommended over raw concrete samples because they exhibit significantly less variation during simple isothermal testing [6]. When using semi-adiabatic test data to predict pavement temperatures via models like HIPERPAV II, higher accuracy is achieved compared to using isothermal test parameters because the increased temperature conditions better mimic in-situ pavements and the tests are performed on concrete rather than mortar [6]. The commercial semi-adiabatic device used in field testing is designed to measure the heat of hydration directly at construction sites, often positioned next to the pavement during paving [8]. Calorimetric data collection in field settings can be conducted using commercial semi-adiabatic devices alongside UPV measurements to correlate heat evolution with set times [9], and calorimetry results from both laboratory and field samples can be directly compared to evaluate the consistency of heat evolution data across different testing environments [10].

Predicted pavement temperatures versus measurements (pavement top, Alma Center, WI ) [6]

The area underneath the heat signature curve generated by semi-adiabatic calorimeters is used to indicate one-day strength gain in cementitious materials [3]. Furthermore, the thermal profile generated by calorimetry can be utilized to assess the setting times of mixtures through “thermal setting times,” which are calculated using specific fractions (such as 20% and 50%) of the temperature rise curve [8]. Complementary to this, Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) measurements using p-wave propagation can determine initial set times in fresh concrete by monitoring sound impulse travel through a sample maintained at ambient slab temperatures [9]. Commercial UPV devices utilize transducers placed at the bottom of a mold and on a floating polyethylene disk at the surface to record sound impulse travel times every minute [9].

Limitations and Challenges

Isothermal calorimeters are limited to a seven-day duration due to signal sensitivity constraints that make it difficult to distinguish hydration signals from background noise [1]. Furthermore, conventional test takes up to a week, utilizes expensive and complex equipment, is not suitable for routine field application, and produces results affected by assumptions of thermal properties of materials.

Semi-adiabatic calorimeters such as the AdiaCal device are highly sensitive to concrete placement temperature, causing significant variation in temperature curves even for samples tested on the same day [6]. This sensitivity makes them suitable for predicting set time but less desirable for precise quality control compared to other methods [6]. Neither standard isothermal nor AdiaCal semi-adiabatic calorimeters effectively identify changes in the water-to-cement (w/c) ratio of field concrete, necessitating supplementary methods like microwave testing for such identification [6].

AdiaCal calorimeter [6]

Mitigation Strategies

Water insulation is the most popular method for preventing heat loss by controlling the surrounding environment’s temperature [1]. To mitigate thermal cracking in overlays with high early-age heat generation, calorimetry data supports adjusting mix proportions to reduce rapid hydration or implementing cooling measures such as pre-cooled aggregates and chilled mixing water [7]. Consequently, research initiatives have identified a need to develop a field-ready version of the adiabatic calorimeter that is rugged, inexpensive, and capable of reproducing laboratory data for routine quality control [4].

Lafarge basket test setup [1]

Temperature Profiles in Ternary Mixes

AdiaCal calorimetry tests for ternary mixtures can record peak temperatures reaching approximately 105°F to 115°F within roughly 14.6 hours, depending on specific mixture proportions and initial ambient conditions [3].

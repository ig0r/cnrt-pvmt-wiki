Deflection Ratio

Part of: Curling and Warping

Also known as: normalized deflection

A new indicator developed in the Phase II study (IHRB TR-749) that measures the degree of curling and warping while minimizing the effect of transverse joint spacing. The deflection ratio is calculated as the slab deflection divided by the slab length, providing a normalized measure of curling intensity that allows comparison across slabs of different sizes. [1]

Multiple linear regression modelss prediction results for county roads and city streets: (a) curvature IRI, (b) deflection, (c) deflection ratio, (d) degree of curvature [1]

Introduction and Significance

To provide a more consistent indicator of curling/warping severity than raw deflection alone, the ratio normalization removes the bias introduced by different joint spacings (12 ft, 15 ft, 20 ft) across Iowa’s pavements.

Computational Methodology

The deflection ratio is computed using a second-order polynomial fitting model to identify the slab’s neutral axis, as this quadratic equation physically represents the pavement shape and allows for the calculation of curvature from high-speed profiler data [1]. This ratio is derived from a second-order polynomial fitting model applied to high-speed profiler data, which identifies the slab’s neutral axis and allows curvature calculation from the distance between a diagonal line connecting the slab edges and the point of maximum deflection [2].

Fitted profile to characterize concrete slab curling/warping [2]

The deflection ratio is calculated by normalizing the distance between a diagonal line drawn across the slab edges and the point of maximum deflection in the fitted curvature profile by the slab length. [2]

Schematic of the deflection basin under an FWD load plate [2]

Data Collection and Sensor Configuration

Deflection ratio values are calculated using all three sensors mounted on the front bumper over the left wheel path, right wheel path, and the center of the vehicle in a truck-mounted high-speed inertial profiler system. [2]

Truck-mounted high-speed profiler system [2]

Phase II Key Findings: Seasonal and Diurnal Effects

In the Phase II study, deflection ratio values ranged from 1.92 to 16.46 across all sites, with trendlines showing the deflection ratio approaching zero when the total equivalent temperature difference was near 0°F [1]. Winter measurements are typically excluded from deflection ratio analysis due to the difficulty of performing profiling in winter conditions and because prior research found minimal insight into smoothness or curling/warping behavior during those seasons [2]. Within LTPP sections, a seasonal effect was observed where the fall avg. deflection ratio = 6.85 and spring = 5.71, while a diurnal effect was noted with morning avg. deflection ratio = 6.44 vs. noon = 6.01.

Seasonal effects on non-LTPP sections: (a) curvature IRI, (b) deflection, (c) deflection ratio, (d) degree of curvature (14 sites, 42 data points for each season) [1]

Phase II Key Findings: Geometric and Material Factors

The study of curling and warping effects on concrete pavement reveals several significant variables, including width, shoulder configuration, and mix design. Regarding the width effect, a 12 ft width resulted in an average deflection ratio of 6.40 compared to 5.98 for a 14 ft width. The shoulder effect was also evident, where tied PCC shoulders produced an average deflection ratio of 3.71 versus 5.53 for untied shoulders. Additionally, the mix design effect showed that QMC mixes had an average deflection ratio of 4.82 compared to 5.98 for pre-QMC mixes [1].

Comparative Analysis of Section Types

In non-LTPP sections, the deflection ratio exhibits a wider variability range (1.92 to 16.46) compared to LTPP sections (2.15 to 13.99), with winter showing the lowest average values for these specific sites [1]. For county roads and city streets, deflection ratio values are generally lower than highway sections, ranging from 2.29 to 10.84, with fall exhibiting the highest average curling intensity among these local road types [1].

3D Concrete Printing

Part of: Additive Manufacturing for Concrete is-instance-of
3D Concrete Printing is an instance of Additive Manufacturing for Concrete.
3d-concrete-printingadditive-manufacturingconstruction-technology
Also known as: 3DCP, Concrete 3D Printing
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-20

The technology integrates mixing, placement, consolidation and finishing into one automated step, eliminating the need for traditional formwork and enabling complex geometries with reduced labor and time [1]. The process relies on a pump-extrusion system that deposits the material according to the sliced geometry; each deposited strand must retain sufficient stiffness to support subsequent layers while remaining workable enough for continuous placement [1]. Printable mixes are distinguished from conventional cast concrete by a set of performance attributes including flowability, extrudability, printability, buildability and open time that define the usable workability window [1].

Feasibility Study of 3D Printing of Concrete for Transportation Infrastructure (TR-756) (2020)

Key findings

  • Printable concrete performance was found to be dictated by mix design and material selection, with an optimal blend providing smooth extrusion, consistent layer placement, and adequate structural stability [1].
  • Printed elements were observed to be anisotropic, exhibiting a marked drop in compressive capacity when loaded parallel to the filament direction while exceeding conventional cast specimens in flexural strength due to extrusion densification [1].
  • Typical printing defects such as air-bubble pop outs, discontinuities, slumping, and plastic-shrinkage cracking were traced to fresh-mix rheology and could be mitigated by prompt curing and refined printing parameters [1].
  • Achieving a balance among flowability, extrudability, buildability, and open time was demonstrated to be essential for successful printing, with these requirements shifting according to printer design and operating settings [1].

Printable Mix Design And Mechanical Anisotropy

Printable mix design requires balancing conflicting rheological properties, specifically ensuring sufficient flowability for pumping while maintaining the stiffness necessary to support subsequent layers during deposition [1]. This balance is achieved by formulating cementitious materials with low water-to-binder ratios and incorporating chemical admixtures that control both extrudability and the rate of stiffness development [1].

The figure below illustrates how varying the water-to-binder ratio impacts these critical printing qualities.

Printed cylindrical object demonstrating the effects of water-to-binder ratio on printing quality. [1]

The image displays a gray, cementitious cylinder resting inside a container with visible residue on the walls. The object exhibits significant deformation and slump at its base rather than maintaining a sharp vertical profile.

Research problem — Developing a printable concrete mix that simultaneously satisfies flowability for smooth extrusion and buildability to prevent shape loss or defects under subsequent layers remains a critical challenge [1]. Deficiencies in these rheological properties lead to layer failures, reduced structural performance, and safety concerns that hinder the scalable deployment of 3D concrete printing for infrastructure applications [1].

The following figure illustrates common issues encountered during the concrete printing process.

Plastic shrinkage cracking on a printed concrete filament. [1]

The image displays a close-up of a single, thick strand of greenish-grey material deposited in a spiral pattern, exhibiting extensive surface fissures and cracks. These defects are identified as plastic shrinkage cracks resulting from the mixture’s low water-to-binder ratio designed to improve buildability.

State of the art — Mechanical anisotropy in printed elements is a defining characteristic, with compressive strength varying significantly based on loading direction relative to the filament orientation due to weaker interlayer bonding compared to monolithic cast concrete [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Identified a specific mix formulation (Portland cement with 2.5% silica fume, 20% rapid-set grout, and w/b=0.32) as the most printable blend due to its reliable extrusion and shape retention [1].
  • Demonstrated that adding silica fume or viscosity-modifying agents reduced flowability but improved buildability, whereas superplasticizers increased flowability at the expense of shape stability [1].
  • Measured a significant reduction in compressive strength for printed elements compared to conventional cast prisms, confirming pronounced anisotropy with lower strength when loaded parallel or perpendicular to filament direction [1].
  • Observed that flexural strengths were higher for printed parts than for mold-cast samples regardless of loading direction, attributed to material densification during extrusion [1].
  • Documented typical printing defects such as air-bubble pop-outs, filament discontinuities, slumping, and cracking, which were traced to fresh-mix rheology and exacerbated by increased printing speeds [1].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a simplified mix-design strategy that replaces traditional cement with a rapid-set grout to simultaneously achieve extrudability and buildability without relying on multiple chemical admixtures [1]. This approach is supported by a rigorous, staged mixing protocol and a combined visual-quantitative quality assessment framework that ensures reproducible geometric fidelity in printed elements [1].

The following figure illustrates the flexural strength development of the rapid-set grout used in this study.

Flexural strength development of grout used for 3D printing at various ages. [1]

A small amount of additional VMA and superplasticizer were also used to adjust the segregation resistance and flowability of the 3D printing mortar. The data demonstrates that the material gains significant structural integrity over time, which is a critical requirement for ensuring buildability in automated concrete deposition processes.

Implications — Structural designs must explicitly account for directional strength variations, recognizing that compressive capacity is reduced when loads act parallel to the printed filaments [1]. Construction practice requires implementing prompt and controlled curing regimes to mitigate shrinkage-induced cracking and dimensional drift inherent in anisotropic prints [1]. Quality assurance protocols should employ a dual approach combining visual ranking with quantitative three-dimensional scanning to detect defects such as slumping or air-bubble pop-outs before service [1].

The following figure illustrates the failure modes observed when samples are subjected to compressive loads parallel to the printed filaments.

Failure modes of samples under compression tested at 28 days, specifically showing a sample loaded in the direction parallel to printed filaments. [1]

The image displays a rectangular concrete specimen exhibiting multiple vertical cracks that run along its length. These vertical cracks are larger at the edges and finer at the center of the tested sample, indicating separation between adjacent filaments caused by tensile stresses from bending deformation under compressive load.

Limitations — Mechanical performance is compromised by weak interlayer bonds that result in significant anisotropy and lower compressive strength compared to conventional cast parts [1]. Achieving a uniform printable mix is difficult because minor variations in flowability or stiffening dramatically alter print quality, requiring tighter control of mixture consistency [1]. Unexplained phenomena regarding directional strength and the complex interplay of variables necessitate substantially more research before reliable full-scale deployment can be realized [1].

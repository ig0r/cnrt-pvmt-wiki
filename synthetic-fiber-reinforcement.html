Synthetic Fiber Reinforcement

Part of: Fiber-Reinforced Concrete
concretematerialsreinforcement
Also known as: concrete fibers, fiber reinforcement
Created 2026-05-20 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-29 02:56:42+00:00

Synthetic fibers added to concrete to improve structural performance of thin PCC overlays. Three types were tested on the Iowa Highway 13 project: polypropylene fibrillated (3 lb/c.y.), polypropylene monofilament (1 lb/c.y.), and W.R. Grace structural fibers (3 lb/c.y.). Fibers were blown into the concrete mixing drum using a hopper system. Construction challenges included fiber clumping (resolved by increasing blower suction) and surface fibers standing up after tining (worn away after a few days of traffic). [1]

Overview of Phase III Performance Findings

The cable-2006-composite-pavement-unbonded-overlays-phase3 study over four years found that adding fibers had no significant impact on faulting, joint movement, or load transfer values. However, fibers provided insurance against loss of materials when individual slabs cracked or lost support. Structural fibers (Type C) enabled larger slab sizes (9 × 9 ft.) without loss of load transfer or increased cracking rates in overlays ≤4.5 in. Fibers controlled section loss when concrete cracked in three isolated fractured slab areas. [2]

Fiber Material Specifications and Dosages

The Iowa Highway 13 project utilized three distinct synthetic fiber types—polypropylene fibrillated, polypropylene monofilament, and W.R. Grace structural fibers—at dosages of 3 lb/cu yd, 1 lb/cu yd, and 3 lb/cu yd respectively [1]. To mitigate finishing difficulties caused by the fibers, fiber inclusion was discontinued in header joints at the beginning and end of each day [1].

Typical Cross Section for the Iowa Highway 13 Project [1]

While they do not prevent cracking and only retard material loss around cracks, fibers are recommended as an insurance policy against surface loss in PCC overlays less than 4 inches thick [3].

On this project, three varieties of synthetic fiber reinforcement were used—polypropylene fibrillated, polypropylene monofilament, and structural fibers [2]—and all fibers on this project were provided by W. R. Grace [2].

These discrete additions are often introduced via hopper systems that blow the fibers directly into the concrete mixing drum during transport [1]; specifically, fibers have been added at rates of one pound of monofilament per cubic yard of concrete, three pounds of fibrillated polypropylene fibers per cubic yard of concrete, and three pounds of structural fibers per cubic yard of concrete [2] using a hopper system supplied by W. R. Grace to blow them into the concrete mixing drum [2].

Fibers being blown into the concrete mixing drum [2]

Impact on Crack Control and Reflective Cracking

Fibers assist in bonding and hold cracks tight but will not stop crack formation; consequently, research is ongoing to evaluate their use for allowing larger joint patterns that match underlying PCC layers [3]. While fibers have no impact on the stress required to initiate cracking, they help control crack widths and keep joints tight, providing benefits to joint performance [4].

6-foot joint spacing concrete overlays using BCOA-ME design predicted thickness versus maximum allowable percent of cracked slabs [4]

Furthermore, fiber reinforcement significantly reduces reflective cracking in concrete overlays; when cracks do form in fiber-reinforced sections, they are noticeably thinner than those in unreinforced control sections [4]. In sections with excessive joint spacing (30 ft and 40 ft), fiber reinforcement significantly reduced mid-slab transverse cracking, with only one crack observed compared to nearly 100% of slabs in unreinforced sections [5].

Comparison of reflective cracks developed in test sections without fibers (left) to those with fiber reinforcement (right) [4]

Joint Spacing Guidelines With Fibers

Joint spacing in feet should generally not exceed twice the depth in inches unless fibers are used, which allows for larger joint patterns that can match underlying pavement layouts [3]. For instance, fibers allow for larger joint spacing patterns, such as longitudinal joints spaced 59 inches apart and transverse joints at 48-inch intervals in a three- to four-inch overlay [6].

Deflection comparison for pavement with 3 . 5 whitetopping and 9 ft joint spacing [3]

The addition of 4 lb/yd³ of synthetic macro-fibers does not significantly affect joint activation rates due to their low modulus of elasticity, which requires considerable strain before they carry load [4]. However, fibers effectively limit crack widths over time and reduce the severity of reflective cracking compared to unreinforced sections [4]. Additionally, fibers enhance aggregate interlock across joints, which can improve load transfer efficiency and reduce faulting potential in thin overlays where dowel or tie bars cannot be placed [5].

Percentage of joints activated for different joint spacing of 5-inch thick concrete overlays [4]

Structural Benefits to Fatigue and Fracture Toughness

The inclusion of structural macrofibers increases fatigue capacity and fracture toughness while reducing overlay design thickness. Fibers also help control differential slab movement caused by curling and warping. [7]

Brick Street Whitetopping Applications

In the cable-2006-unbonded-ultrathin-whitetopping-brick-streets study, polypropylene fibers were used at 1.5 lb/cu yd (Oskaloosa northbound lane) and 2.5 lb/cu yd (Des Moines), while the southbound lane in Oskaloosa had no fibers as a control [6]. After five years, cracking was insignificant in both lanes and could not be correlated to the presence or absence of fibers; consequently, for overlay depths >3.5 in., fibers were recommended as optional, whereas for depths <3.5 in., fibers were recommended to increase performance reliability [6]. In brick street whitetopping applications, polypropylene fibers are incorporated at a dosage rate of 2.5 pounds per cubic yard and must be mixed into fresh concrete until uniformly distributed to prevent clumping [6].

Southbound lane overlay paving [6]

Other evaluations have examined synthetic fiber reinforcement in conjunction with various other factors. The Leawood, Kansas project evaluated synthetic fiber reinforcement alongside joint spacing, joint sealing, and urban application suitability [1], while Iowa Highway 21 testing included synthetic fiber reinforcement as a variable alongside ACC surface preparation, PCC thickness, joint spacing, and joint sealing [1].

Construction Challenges and Field Observations

The use of fibers caused concerns for construction workers finishing the slab, especially when using structural fibers at the end-of-day joints [2].

Longitudinal joint–forming knife [2]

To allow for smoother headers and easier finishing, fiber inclusion was stopped both in and out of the headers at the conclusion and beginning of each day; if paving was stopped in a test section that included fibers, the last two truckloads of the day were mixed without fibers, as were the first two truckloads mixed in the morning [2].

During the second day of paving, large concentrations of fiber were seen in the concrete due to a lack of suction in the blower that usually distributes fibers evenly throughout the mixing drum [2]; this was resolved by holding a small cardboard piece over the blower opening after adding fibers, which caused all subsequent clumps to disappear [2].

Adjustment to blower for better fiber dispersion [2]

The tining process caused fibers to stand up, making the surface look “hairy,” though all surface fibers were worn away after a few days of highway traffic [2].

Scarified pavement surface [2]

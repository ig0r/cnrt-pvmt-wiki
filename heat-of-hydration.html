Heat of Hydration

Part of: Cement Hydration is-thermal-effect-of
Heat of Hydration is the thermal energy release caused by Cement Hydration.
calorimetryconcretehydrationmaterialsthermal
Also known as: adiabatic temperature rise, heat signature, hydration heat
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Heat of hydration is the exothermic thermal energy released when cementitious materials chemically react with water to form hydration products and harden. This reaction proceeds through distinct kinetic stages, typically characterized by an initial rapid heat evolution, a dormant period of low activity, and an acceleration phase where the majority of thermal energy is generated as strength develops [1] [2] [9] [19]. The magnitude and rate of this heat release are governed by the cement’s chemical composition, particle fineness, water-to-cement ratio, curing temperature, and the presence of supplementary materials or admixtures [1] [2] [4] [7]. The resulting temperature rise can induce thermal expansion and subsequent contraction upon cooling, which may lead to thermal cracking in large concrete elements if not properly managed [9] [12]. Calorimetric techniques are employed to monitor this heat evolution profile, providing critical insights into setting times, early-age strength development, and the overall degree of hydration [4] [7] [17].

Key findings

  • Incorporating supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash or slag consistently lowered the peak temperature rise during early hydration and postponed the time at which that peak occurred [1] [5].
  • Replacing three to five percent of the binder with silica fume had essentially no impact on the overall heat evolution curve, allowing such a replacement without altering hydration temperature or causing adverse thermal effects [5] [12].
  • Fly ash lengthened the dormant period and delayed the time to reach the maximum rate of temperature rise, whereas slag replacement did not postpone the dormant period and left the time to peak adiabatic temperature rise unchanged or slightly decreased [1].
  • Raising the test or curing temperature increased both the peak rate and total heat released while shortening the time to reach those peaks, causing initial and final thermal set times to drop dramatically [4].
  • Variations in water-reducer dosage or fly-ash replacement mainly shifted the entire heat-evolution curve without altering its shape or peak magnitude, indicating no incompatibility [4].
  • Heat indexes derived from isothermal calorimetry curves enabled the prediction of both set time and early-age strength, providing a practical assessment of thermal-cracking risk for field pavements [7].
  • The heat released during cementitious material hydration in semi-flowable self-consolidating concrete was comparable to or lower than that of conventional pavement concrete, an effect attributed to the inclusion of fly ash [10].
  • Type K self-consolidating concrete generated essentially the same heat of hydration as ordinary Portland-cement concrete, allowing its incorporation in high volumes without introducing additional thermal stress [11].

Heat Generation Measurement, Control, and Thermal Effects

The heat of hydration is the exothermic thermal energy released when cementitious materials chemically react with water, a process that drives the hardening and strength development of concrete [1] [2] [4] [5]. This reaction follows a characteristic evolution curve featuring an initial rapid phase, a dormant period, and an acceleration stage where the majority of thermal energy is generated [1] [2] [9] [19]. The magnitude and rate of heat release are governed by cement chemistry, fineness, mix proportions, curing temperature, and the inclusion of supplementary materials or admixtures [1] [2] [4] [7] [9]. Calorimetric techniques in adiabatic, semi-adiabatic, or isothermal modes are employed to monitor this thermal response, providing critical data for assessing setting times, early-age properties, and potential thermal cracking risks [2] [4] [7] [15] [19].

Cumulative heat of hydration for cement pastes over time. [19]

The distinct kinetic stages are visible in the plot, characterized by an initial rapid rise followed by a slower rate of heat generation.

Research problem — Excessive heat generated during cement hydration creates internal temperature gradients that induce thermal stresses, leading to early-age cracking, curling, and warping in concrete pavements [1] [12] [13] [18]. There is a lack of practical, standardized field methods for quantifying the thermal signature of concrete mixes, leaving engineers without reliable tools to predict early-age temperature gradients and manage quality control [2] [4] [15]. The difficulty in accurately predicting heat evolution and setting times hinders the optimization of mix designs for varying climatic conditions and fast-track construction schedules [3] [7] [12] [16].

The following figure illustrates how different cement types influence this critical thermal property.

Rate of heat generation over time for different cement types. [7]

The figure displays the rate of heat generation in mW/g versus time in hours, showing distinct curves for Type I, Type III, and ISM cements. Type III cement exhibits a significantly higher peak rate of heat evolution compared to Type I cement due to its much higher fineness. The ISM blended cement shows an initial reduction in the early rate of hydration followed by a delayed, sharp peak compared to Type I cement.

State of the art — The field relies on calorimetric testing—primarily semi-adiabatic and isothermal methods—to generate heat-evolution curves that serve as fingerprints for assessing early-age hydration kinetics, predicting setting times, and estimating strength development [1] [2] [7] [10] [16]. Industry practice has shifted from expensive laboratory instruments to low-cost, portable devices such as AdiaCal and IQ-Drum systems, which enable rapid on-site quality control and integration with predictive software like HIPERPAV [2] [7] [10] [18]. Thermal management is achieved through the strategic use of supplementary cementitious materials to attenuate peak temperatures and extend dormant periods, while field monitoring via maturity sensors guides decisions on traffic opening and joint sawing [1] [5] [9] [11] [15].

Findings from the reports

  • Incorporating supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash or ground-granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) systematically lowers the peak temperature and total heat released during hydration, thereby reducing the risk of thermal cracking [1] [2] [5] [10] [18].
  • Incorporating supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash or ground-granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) systematically lowers the peak temperature and total heat released during hydration, thereby reducing the risk of thermal cracking [1] [2].
  • Finer GGBFS grades generate higher peak temperatures than coarser grades due to increased reactivity, yet both still produce lower overall heat signatures compared with plain Portland cement [5] [12].
  • Silica fume increases the heat signature and accelerates the time to reach peak temperature due to strong pozzolanic activity, but varying its dosage between 3% and 5% has a negligible effect on the overall thermal profile [5] [12].
  • Testing or curing temperature is the dominant factor governing heat-of-hydration parameters; higher temperatures increase peak rates and total heat while shortening the time to reach peaks, whereas lower temperatures delay set times and reduce heat release [4] [16].
  • Heat-evolution curves derived from calorimetry can reliably predict early-age setting times and strength development, with specific heat indexes correlating strongly to conventional ASTM C403 set-time measurements [2] [7].
  • Semi-adiabatic calorimetry data provide pavement temperature predictions that align more closely with actual field measurements than those derived from isothermal parameters [4].
  • Rapid-setting cementitious pastes can generate heat at twice the rate of conventional pavement cements, creating a significant risk of thermal cracking if active cooling measures are not employed during early curing [9].
  • Incorporating super-absorbent polymers (SAPs) elevates the primary hydration peak and modestly raises total heat evolved by releasing additional water that increases the degree of cement hydration in the early stages [19].
  • Semi-adiabatic temperature rise tests demonstrated a clear linear correlation with mortar compressive strength at early ages (approximately three days), although this relationship diminished by seven days as slag content began to dominate strength development [3].
  • Found that Type K self-consolidating concrete generates essentially the same heat of hydration as ordinary Portland cement concrete, allowing for high-volume incorporation in large structures without introducing additional thermal stress [11].

Novelty & rigor — Research has advanced the measurement and control of heat generation by developing portable, low-cost calorimetry systems that enable rapid on-site monitoring and real-time process feedback for concrete pavements [2] [4] [7] [18]. Novelty in thermal effect analysis is demonstrated through the creation of quantitative heat indexes and predictive models that link specific supplementary cementitious material chemistries to early-age strength and cracking risk [3] [5] [7] [12]. The field integration of hydration heat monitoring into pavement smoothness studies provides a unique framework for correlating exothermic reactions with curling and warping outcomes during early-age performance [13].

Across the reviewed reports, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash and ground-granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) generally reduce peak temperatures and total heat evolution compared to plain Portland cement, although the specific thermal impact depends on SCM type, fineness, and dosage. While fly ash consistently extends the dormant period and delays the time to peak heat generation, GGBFS effects are more variable; finer slag grades can increase early heat release rates compared to coarser grades, yet both typically lower the overall thermal signature relative to ordinary cement. In contrast to SCMs that mitigate heat, silica fume and certain rapid-setting or specialized binders significantly increase early-age heat signatures and peak temperatures due to accelerated pozzolanic reactions. Measurement methodologies vary in sensitivity and application; isothermal calorimetry provides high-resolution data on reaction kinetics and set-time prediction, whereas semi-adiabatic tests are widely used for field monitoring but may show weaker correlations with actual setting times depending on the mix design. The resulting heat-evolution profiles directly influence early-age performance indicators, including thermal cracking risk, set time, and strength development, with lower total heat evolution generally correlating to reduced internal stresses in mass concrete applications. Implications — Engineers should utilize supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash and slag, to lower peak hydration temperatures and mitigate thermal cracking risks in large pours or hot-weather placements, while adjusting schedules to account for delayed setting times associated with these blends [1] [5] [10] [16]. Routine quality control and performance prediction should rely on simple isothermal calorimetry for mix verification, while semi-adiabatic testing provides critical data for modeling pavement temperature gradients and forecasting field operations like sawing [2] [7] [10] [16]. Mix designs involving rapid-setting overlays or super-absorbent polymers require strict temperature management and proportion adjustments to prevent excessive early-age heat generation and subsequent thermal distress [9] [19].

Limitations — Calorimetric methods often fail to capture the initial temperature spike occurring within minutes of mixing, and results can be skewed by sampling errors or significant variability between concrete and mortar specimens [1] [4] [7]. Predicting setting times from heat evolution data is unreliable due to methodological differences in defining set points, low statistical correlation with actual field performance, and high sensitivity to ambient and starting temperatures [7] [10] [16] [17]. Existing models are restricted to specific material ranges and laboratory conditions, while practical adoption is hindered by the high cost of equipment, long test durations, and economic constraints on certain supplementary cementitious materials [2] [12] [18].

Non-Destructive Testing for Setting Time Prediction

Research problem — Current methods for predicting concrete setting time using semi-adiabatic calorimetry are unreliable because the temperature signal does not uniquely correspond to the setting process and may fail to reflect actual field conditions [17]. There is a critical need for accurate initial set predictions to optimize saw-cutting schedules, as timing errors can lead to pavement defects such as raveling or random cracking, resulting in increased construction costs and disputes [17].

State of the art — While semi-adiabatic calorimetry has traditionally been used to estimate thermal setting times based on temperature rise fractions, its poor correlation with actual field set conditions limits its reliability [17]. Ultrasonic pulse-velocity monitoring of p-waves is now the de-facto technique for predicting setting time because the onset of velocity increase directly marks initial set and supports practical scheduling for sawing operations [17].

The relationship between conventional sawing times and initial set determined by ultrasonic pulse velocity is illustrated in the following figure.

(b). Conventional sawing time versus initial set from UPV measurements [17]

The figure plots a positive linear correlation between the ultrasonic pulse-velocity (UPV) measured initial set and the subsequent conventional sawing time for various concrete mixtures.

Findings from the reports

  • Ultrasonic pulse velocity reliably indicated initial set time, enabling accurate scheduling for early-entry sawing approximately 220 minutes after the determined set point [17].
  • The temperature-rise method correlated poorly with ultrasonic pulse velocity for predicting setting times and did not predict setting as accurately as the UPV method [17].
  • All concrete mixtures displayed a similar temperature increase once hydration began, despite differences in peak timing [17].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a field-based approach that directly compares semi-adiabatic calorimetry with ultrasonic pulse velocity measurements to predict concrete setting time, challenging the reliability of heat evolution data for practical scheduling [17]. By coupling these two non-destructive techniques, the study establishes a more robust on-site protocol for estimating initial set and calibrating saw-cut schedules, particularly for mixtures with slower hydration kinetics [16].

Implications — Specifiers and contractors should avoid relying on semi-adiabatic calorimetry for predicting concrete setting times due to its poor correlation with actual set conditions [17]. Practice should shift toward ultrasonic pulse velocity monitoring as a more reliable method for determining the optimal timing for early-entry and conventional sawing operations [17].

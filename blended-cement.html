Blended Cement

Part of: Cementitious Materials & Chemistry is-instance-of
Blended Cement is an instance of Cementitious Materials & Chemistry.
cementmaterialsscm
Also known as: slag-blended cement
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-19T16:58:09Z

Blended cement is a hydraulic binder produced by intergrinding ordinary Portland cement clinker with one or more supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash, slag, silica fume, or natural pozzolans. These supplementary components possess pozzolanic or latent hydraulic properties that react with calcium hydroxide released during hydration to form additional calcium-silicate-hydrate gel, thereby enhancing the binder’s long-term strength and durability [1] [2] [3] [4] [8] [9] [11] [12] [13] [16]. The inclusion of these materials modifies the fresh-state characteristics of the binder by altering setting times, workability, and heat evolution profiles compared to plain Portland cement [1] [3] [4] [8] [9] [11] [12] [13]. By partially replacing clinker with these supplementary materials, blended cements reduce the overall carbon footprint of production while improving resistance to chemical attacks such as sulfate attack and alkali-silica reaction [1] [2] [3] [8] [9] [11] [12] [13]. The specific performance characteristics of the resulting binder are determined by the type and proportion of supplementary materials incorporated, leading to various standardized classifications [1] [3] [9] [11] [13] [16].

Key findings

  • Blended cements reduce the peak heat of hydration and delay its occurrence compared with plain Portland cement [1] [6] [7] [11] [13].
  • Incorporating supplementary cementitious materials consistently lengthens both initial and final setting times while increasing fresh-state workability [6] [7] [11] [13].
  • Ternary blends containing slag, fly ash, and silica fume provide enhanced durability and strength performance that outperforms plain Portland cement [2] [6] [13].
  • Class F fly ash effectively mitigates sulfate expansion and alkali-silica reaction (ASR), whereas Class C fly ash can cause excessive expansion [5] [11] [13].
  • Field performance of slag-containing concretes is more strongly influenced by construction practices than by the dosage of slag or scaling test results [8] [12] [14].
  • Early-age strength development is often lower for blended cements, but the strength gap narrows with age and many blends achieve high long-term compressive strengths [7] [11].
  • Blended cements containing bassanite can exhibit premature stiffening (false set), which is effectively suppressed by incorporating Class C fly ash [3].
  • Ternary blends significantly reduce carbon dioxide emissions and lower the overall environmental footprint of concrete production [13].

Blended Cement Performance

Blended cement is a hydraulic binder produced by intergrinding ordinary Portland cement clinker with one or more supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash, slag, or silica fume [1] [2] [3] [4] [9] [11] [16]. The performance of these blends is driven by the pozzolanic or latent hydraulic reactions of the supplementary materials with calcium hydroxide released during hydration, which generates additional calcium-silicate-hydrate gel to densify the microstructure [1] [3] [4] [9] [15] [16]. This chemical interaction modifies key properties by reducing early-age heat evolution and permeability while enhancing long-term strength, durability against chemical attacks, and workability [1] [2] [3] [4] [8] [9] [12]. By adjusting the type and proportion of supplementary materials, engineers can tailor setting characteristics and mechanical performance to meet specific application requirements while reducing clinker consumption [11] [13] [15] [16].

Research problem — Current performance-based specifications fail to account for the significant chemical and physical variability of supplementary cementitious materials, leaving practitioners without reliable guidance on proportioning and evaluating blended cements [2] [6] [13] [15]. The limited understanding of how these materials modify concrete behavior leads to unpredictable early-age strength development and setting times, which complicates construction scheduling and risks premature pavement failure [1] [6] [7] [11]. Inconsistent material chemistry and unfavorable mineral balances cause severe workability issues and durability defects, such as excessive shrinkage cracking and alkali-silica reaction, undermining long-term structural integrity [3] [11] [13].

State of the art — The field has transitioned from prescriptive ingredient limits to performance-based mix design paradigms, such as the Performance-Engineered Mixture (PEM) approach, which utilizes rapid durability tests and predictive models to verify that ternary blends meet transport, freeze-thaw, and strength targets while lowering Portland cement content [15] [18]. Current practice routinely employs binary or ternary systems combining ordinary Portland cement with supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash, slag, and silica fume to optimize durability, reduce carbon emissions, and control costs without sacrificing long-term pavement performance [1] [2] [6] [11] [13]. Ongoing research focuses on developing quantitative predictive models for strength and heat evolution, monitoring mineralogical variability to mitigate issues like false set, and integrating life-cycle criteria into mix proportioning guidelines [3] [6] [13] [15].

Findings from the reports

  • Blended cements consistently delayed both initial and final setting times while improving fresh-state workability compared to plain Portland cement [1] [6] [7] [11] [13].
  • The inclusion of supplementary cementitious materials lowered the peak heat of hydration and shifted the thermal peak to later ages, thereby reducing the risk of early-age thermal cracking [1] [4] [6] [7] [13].
  • Early-age strength development was slower for blended cements due to reduced heat evolution and delayed hydration kinetics [1] [7] [11].
  • Ternary blends containing slag, fly ash, and silica fume or metakaolin achieved superior durability performance by mitigating sulfate attack and alkali-silica reaction at lower replacement levels than binary systems [2] [11] [13].
  • Ternary mixtures demonstrated robust long-term strength development, often exceeding plain Portland cement strengths at later ages such as 180 days [3] [6] [7].
  • The use of blended cements substantially reduced carbon dioxide emissions and the overall environmental footprint of concrete production [11] [13].
  • Blended cements significantly reduced chloride ion penetration and increased electrical resistivity compared to plain Portland cement mixes [13].
  • The presence of bassanite in certain blended cements caused premature stiffening (false set), which was effectively mitigated by the inclusion of Class C fly ash [3].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a performance-based specification framework that replaces traditional percent-replacement methods by directly linking cement chemistry and supplementary material characteristics to concrete-pavement behavior through integrated modeling and expert systems [15]. A distinctive contribution is the systematic characterization of binary and ternary blended cements across varying curing temperatures, utilizing measured datum temperature and activation energy to calibrate maturity-based strength predictions rather than relying on conventional assumptions for plain cement [1]. The methodology establishes a comprehensive data-driven approach that correlates bulk chemistry with durability metrics using high-throughput experimental matrices and statistical regression models, enabling the development of predictive tools for early-age heat evolution and setting times prior to field implementation [6].

Across the reviewed reports, blended cements consistently exhibit delayed setting times and reduced early-age heat evolution compared to plain Portland cement, with fly ash generally extending set duration more significantly than slag. While early-age strength development is often retarded by higher supplementary cementitious material contents, many ternary blends achieve substantial later-age compressive strengths that meet or exceed control mixes, particularly when optimized with materials like silica fume or metakaolin. The incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials enhances long-term durability by mitigating alkali-silica reaction and sulfate attack, reducing shrinkage, and lowering chloride permeability, although specific effects on workability and air-void systems depend heavily on the type and proportion of the blended components. [1] [2] [3] [4] [6] [7] [11] [13]

Implications — Specifications should transition from prescriptive limits to performance-based criteria that account for the synergistic durability benefits of ternary blends, allowing for higher supplementary cementitious material utilization [2] [6] [11] [13]. Designers must adjust curing regimes and traffic opening criteria to accommodate slower early-age strength development and delayed set times associated with blended cements [1] [2] [6] [13]. Mix designs require calibration for specific supplier variability in supplementary cementitious materials to manage workability and mitigate risks such as premature stiffening or thermal cracking [1] [3] [6].

Limitations — Conclusions regarding performance remain provisional due to reliance on literature reviews and advisory input without laboratory testing, alongside inherent variability in supplementary cementitious materials that necessitates further research before firm specifications can be established [2]. Performance predictability is compromised by wide variations in material chemistry and physical properties, leading to practical issues such as rapid slump loss, unstable air content, delayed setting, and cracking under specific environmental conditions [1] [6] [11] [13] [18]. Current predictive models and guidelines are limited by their derivation from mortar experiments or specific regional projects, raising concerns about extrapolation to broader concrete applications and field conditions [3] [6].

Durability & Environmental Resistance

Research problem — It remains unresolved whether air-entraining admixtures are necessary for low-permeability concrete made with blended cements, as the industry lacks clear thresholds to balance freeze-thaw durability against the potential loss of compressive strength and consistency in high-density mixes [9]. Supplementary cementitious materials may be insufficient to prevent premature joint deterioration under aggressive moisture saturation and chloride ingress, necessitating the evaluation of additional protective strategies such as penetrating sealers for cold-region pavements [17]. Incorporating high volumes of supplementary cementitious materials into pervious concrete compromises structural performance and early-age strength development, creating a conflict between environmental sustainability goals and the durability requirements for traffic-bearing wearing courses [10]. The widespread use of locally available reactive aggregates causes alkali-silica reaction damage that accelerates freeze-thaw deterioration, yet blended cements capable of mitigating this expansion are often unavailable or not utilized in current mixes [5]. There is significant concern regarding whether high replacement levels of slag reduce resistance to deicing salt-induced surface scaling, particularly given the discrepancy between severe laboratory test results and more favorable field observations [8] [12] [14].

State of the art — The prevailing practice for high-durability blended cement involves binary or ternary systems using slag, fly ash, or silica fume at replacement levels up to 50%, which significantly reduce permeability and enhance resistance to chemical attacks such as sulfate and alkali-silica reactivity [5] [8] [9] [12] [14] [16] [17]. While traditional ASTM C672 scaling tests have historically limited slag usage due to harsh laboratory conditions, current consensus attributes surface scaling failures primarily to construction variables like excessive water-cement ratios rather than the binder composition itself [5] [8] [9] [12] [14] [16] [17]. To better reflect field performance, the industry is shifting toward refined testing protocols and optimized mix designs that target lower water-to-binder ratios and improved air-void spacing to ensure freeze-thaw durability in aggressive deicing-salt environments [5] [8] [9] [12] [14] [16] [17].

Findings from the reports

  • Construction factors such as re-tempering and higher water-cement ratios influenced scaling performance more significantly than the proportion of slag in the concrete mixture [8] [14].
  • Blended cements incorporating supplementary cementitious materials effectively mitigated alkali-silica reaction (ASR), whereas low-alkali cement alone failed to control expansion [5].
  • Reducing the water-to-cement ratio significantly reduced deicer-scaling loss in blended cement mixes, even at high slag contents [12].
  • Blended Type IP cement with fly ash produced very low-permeability concrete with excellent freeze-thaw performance and durability factors above 85% when air entrainment was used [9].
  • Supplementary cementitious material blends were more workable and required less compaction than pure Portland cement mixes, with some combinations producing higher early-age tensile strengths [10].
  • Replacing Portland cement with slag, limestone powder, or fly ash in pervious concrete altered workability, density, compressive strength, and hydraulic conductivity, with fly-ash blends showing the best workability [16].
  • Fly-ash and limestone blends in pervious concrete removed roughly thirty percent of naphthalene contaminants, whereas slag blends removed almost none due to high permeability limiting interaction [16].
  • A blended cement consisting of 70% Type I Portland cement and 30% Class C fly ash produced fresh and hardened air content values that satisfied ACI 318 limits for concrete exposed to freeze-thaw cycles [17].
  • Field cores incorporating blended cement with ground-granulated blast-furnace slag exhibited very limited deicer-induced scaling, with mass losses well below the allowable limit in most cases [8].

Novelty & rigor — Research has introduced a systematic evaluation protocol for determining the specific water-to-binder thresholds at which air entrainment can be omitted in low-permeability concrete containing fly ash, providing new design guidance for freeze-thaw durability [9]. A novel laboratory scaling test procedure has been developed that modifies standard curing and exposure practices to better capture the slower strength gain and moisture behavior of slag-blended concretes during deicer-induced freezing [12] [14]. The methodological approach is distinguished by its use of full-size cores extracted from in-service structures to correlate field workmanship variables with durability performance, moving beyond typical single-test laboratory evaluations [8].

Across the reviewed reports, blended cements incorporating supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash or slag generally enhance durability by reducing permeability and mitigating alkali-silica reaction, although their resistance to deicer scaling is highly sensitive to water-to-binder ratios and construction practices rather than binder composition alone. While low permeability from fly ash or slag blends improves freeze-thaw performance when air entrainment is properly controlled, field observations indicate that construction errors like re-tempering and high water-cement ratios are often more detrimental to scaling resistance than the specific type or percentage of blended cement used. The relationship between slag content and chloride resistance is consistently positive across laboratory studies, with higher slag percentages significantly lowering charge passed values, yet scaling performance remains variable and often requires stricter water-to-cement ratios to compensate for the potential reduction in air-void stability at high replacement levels. [5] [8] [12] [14]

Implications — Designers can confidently specify high-volume slag blends for pavements and bridge decks, provided that rigorous construction quality control is enforced to mitigate scaling risks associated with poor workmanship rather than the cementitious material itself [8]. Regulatory limits on slag content should be raised significantly based on field evidence, and specifications must mandate alternative laboratory testing methods that accurately reflect service conditions instead of relying on tests developed for plain Portland cement [12] [14]. To ensure durability against freeze-thaw cycles and deicer scaling, mix designs must prioritize low water-to-binder ratios and optimized air-void systems with fine spacing factors, as these construction parameters are more critical than the specific type of supplementary cementitious material used [9] [12]. For applications requiring stormwater treatment, fly ash or limestone blends should be selected to balance structural integrity with pollutant removal capabilities, whereas slag is better reserved for scenarios where rapid drainage and downstream treatment are prioritized [16]. Ternary mix designs incorporating multiple supplementary cementitious materials should become standard practice to mitigate alkali-silica reaction risks while maintaining workability and strength in regions with reactive aggregates [5].

Limitations — Achieving reliable air-entrainment in certain blended cements requires higher admixture dosages at low water-to-binder ratios, and the theoretical durability limits for non-air-entrained mixes fall below practical field construction capabilities [9]. Standard scaling tests designed for plain Portland cement tend to overstate deterioration in high-slag blends, rendering current agency replacement limits potentially overly conservative and necessitating further validation of alternative testing protocols [12]. Experimental programs are often constrained by small specimen counts, unresolved correlations between porosity and unit weight, and a reliance on early-age strength criteria that leave long-term performance uncertain [10] [16].

Hydration Kinetics & Microstructural Characterization

Research problem — There is a lack of practical, rapid, and low-cost methods for monitoring the heat evolution during the hydration of concrete mixes containing supplementary cementitious materials [4]. The absence of reliable field techniques to characterize these hydration features hinders the ability to verify mix proportions, ensure quality control, and predict early-age behavior such as setting time and thermal cracking risk [4].

State of the art — Calorimetric monitoring of heat evolution is the dominant state-of-the-art method for assessing blended cement hydration, with semi-adiabatic and isothermal devices serving as the industry’s de facto standard due to the absence of specific ASTM standards for concrete [4] [7]. Heat-index parameters derived from temperature curves are routinely used to predict early-age set times and strength development with high correlation, enabling performance-based tools like HIPERPAV to assess thermal cracking risks [7]. Despite the availability of mathematical models, a consensus on interpreting heat-signature curves remains elusive, prompting ongoing research into rapid, low-cost calorimeters and performance-based specifications that link hydration kinetics directly to concrete performance [4].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel workflow that integrates low-cost semi-isothermal calorimetry with newly defined heat-evolution indexes to generate mix-design metrics for performance-based specifications [4]. This approach uniquely converts raw thermal data into compact quantitative indices, such as the Heat Evolution Number and derivative-derived set times, enabling rapid characterization of blended cement hydration behavior within a single day rather than weeks [7]. The methodology ensures reproducibility through strict adherence to standardized mechanical mixing protocols, precise specimen dimensioning, sensor calibration against known references, and ambient-temperature corrections aligned with established guidance [4] [7].

Implications — The delayed hydration and extended setting times characteristic of blended cements necessitate adjustments to construction schedules to accommodate the slower early-age strength development [7]. Routine calorimetric monitoring should be implemented during production to detect variations in supplementary cementitious material dosages or admixture incompatibilities, ensuring consistent mix performance [7].

Limitations — Calorimetry methods struggle to accurately predict certain cement phases and supplementary materials, particularly when results are sensitive to temperature variations [7]. Heat-index approaches may fail to distinguish between different supplementary materials that produce similar hydration curves [7]. The applicability of the findings remains provisional because the research scope excluded sufficient field tests and advanced analyses needed for real-world validation [7].

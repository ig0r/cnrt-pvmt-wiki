Wenner Probe / Surface Resistivity

Part of: Low-Permeability Concrete is-evaluation-method-of
Wenner Probe / Surface Resistivity is an evaluation method of Low-Permeability Concrete.
chloride-penetrationconceptnon-destructivequality-assurancetesting
Also known as: AASHTO TP 95, Wenner four-probe, Wenner meter, surface resistivity
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-11

The Wenner Probe is a rapid, non-destructive four-probe electrical resistivity method used to evaluate low-permeability concrete by measuring surface electrical resistivity, which serves as an indirect indicator of permeability where lower resistance correlates with reduced pore connectivity and thus lower permeability [7]. Validated through the FHWA TPF-5(117) ternary mixtures study as a field quality assurance tool, research has demonstrated an excellent correlation between these resistivity results and direct permeability tests, supporting its use in concrete field testing [2], [1].

Introduction to Wenner Probe Testing

Multiple state DOTs have adopted surface resistivity as an acceptance test for concrete quality assurance, following a guide specification from the ternary mixtures project that recommends a minimum surface resistivity of 10 kΩ/cm at 28 days [2]. Testing can be conducted on specimens as early as one day after casting, which allows for very early-age assessment of concrete quality before standard strength testing periods [2]. Furthermore, cylinders cast as QA specimens and placed in wet curing for strength testing can be reused for resistivity tests, adding no additional sampling cost [2].

Testing Principles and Methodology

The Wenner probe applies a known alternating current through two outer electrodes and measures the voltage drop across two inner electrodes, allowing surface resistivity (kΩ·cm) to be computed from the current, voltage, and electrode geometry [1]. Utilizing four probes with constant spacing (typically 5.1 cm or 2 inches), the method measures three-dimensional current flow and requires a geometric correction factor (K=2.7 for standard cylinders) to convert curved-surface readings into equivalent semi-infinite slab resistivity values [1]. A probe spacing of 2 inches is preferred over shorter spacings (e.g., 1.5 inches) because it reduces interference from large aggregate particles, which occurs when spacing is not more than twice the maximum aggregate size [1].

The test requires no sample preparation and takes less than five minutes on moist-cured cylinders [1], though the Wenner method generally takes approximately 30 minutes per specimen [1]. This offers a significant time advantage over the AASHTO T277 rapid chloride permeability test, which requires more than 24 hours to complete [1].

Standardized Test Procedures (AASHTO & LADOTD)

The Wenner probe test, specified under AASHTO TP 95, standardizes the procedure for determining surface resistivity of concrete using a four-probe array [2]. This LADOTD test method for surface resistivity is one of several standardized procedures referenced in concrete durability research alongside ASTM C1679 calorimetry and AASHTO T277 rapid chloride permeability testing [3].

The test utilizes a four-pin Wenner probe array on cylindrical specimens (100 mm by 200 mm or 150 mm by 300 mm) where an AC potential difference is applied across the outer pins while inner probes measure the resulting potential difference [4].

Schematic of the four-point Wenner array probe setup [4]

Correlation with Chloride Permeability

An excellent correlation was established between AASHTO T277 (rapid chloride permeability, coulombs) and Wenner surface resistivity measurements:

| Chloride Penetration | ASTM C1202 (coulombs) | Surface Resistivity (kΩ·cm) | |---------------------|----------------------|---------------------------| | High | >4,000 | <12 | | Moderate | 2,000–4,000 | 12–21 | | Low | 1,000–2,000 | 21–37 | | Very Low | 100–1,000 | 37–254 | | Negligible | <100 | >254 | [2]

Analytical conversion between surface resistivity and rapid chloride permeability (AASHTO T277) requires two specific corrections: a geometric correction factor to account for cylinder curvature, and a Joule effect adjustment to compensate for specimen heating caused by high currents in more permeable mixtures. [1]

Joule effect adjustment – resistivity vs. AASHTO T277 resistivity (1 in.=2.54 cm) [1]

Discrepancies between surface resistivity and bulk permeability tests arise because the Wenner probe only measures conductivity within a shallow depth equal to the probe spacing, which often contains higher paste content than the concrete center. [1]

Adjusted and unadjusted Wenner resistivity vs. T277 resistivity (1 in.=2.54 cm) [1]

LADOTD classifies concrete chloride penetrability based on surface resistivity thresholds: high penetration corresponds to less than 12 kΩ·cm, moderate to 12–21 kΩ·cm, low to 21–37 kΩ·cm, very low to 37–254 kΩ·cm, and negligible to greater than 254 kΩ·cm. [5]

Factors Affecting Surface Resistivity

High-performance concrete utilizing lightweight fine aggregates for internal curing can achieve a surface resistivity of approximately 13.90 kΩ·cm at 28 days, which falls within the moderate chloride penetrability range [6]. While internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) has been shown to improve concrete permeability characteristics as indicated by surface resistivity tests, these improvements occur despite minimal differences in mechanical properties or early-age structural performance [3]. Furthermore, immersion in certain deicing liquids, such as Ossian Season One, can improve concrete surface resistivity compared to traditional chloride-based solutions like sodium or calcium chloride [7].

Surface scaling after 50 freezing-thawing cycles: a) plain water, b) 3% sodium chloride, c) 4% calcium chloride, d) Ossian Season One [7]

Additionally, high fly ash content mixtures may require extended curing periods to reach optimal resistivity values; for example, a mixture with high fly ash showed an average resistivity of 26.8 kΩ·cm at 56 days and continued to increase over time [5].

Averaged surface resistivity results of three mixes [5]

Formation Factor Definition

The formation factor, a key parameter for evaluating transport properties, is defined as the ratio of bulk concrete resistivity to pore solution resistivity [8]. Once the pore solution resistivity is obtained for the mixture design, surface resistivity measurements can be converted to a formation factor [8]. Measurement of the formation factor on sealed resistivity samples requires a correction using the saturation coefficient to account for differences between sealed and saturated sample resistivities [8]; thus, it can be determined using sealed samples with a saturation coefficient correction [8].

Regarding specific mixture compositions, internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) improves surface resistivity at ages greater than 7 days, indicating enhanced hydration despite storage in a moist environment [9]. This improvement occurs even though LWFA may reduce the concrete’s modulus of elasticity, which is desirable for reducing cracking risk under strain [9]. Furthermore, moisture gradients in internally cured concrete sections are consistently lower than in control sections due to the buffering action of lightweight fine aggregate, which helps maintain higher surface resistivity over time, contributing to reduced slab movement and improved long-term durability [9]. In contrast, the incorporation of superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) as internal curing agents slightly reduces the electrical resistivity of concrete, although values remain within acceptable durability bounds [10].

Surface resistivity test results (kΩ.cm) [9]

Other factors influencing resistivity include an inverse exponential relationship between the water-to-cementitious materials ratio and 56-day surface resistivity, where increasing the w/cm ratio leads to a measurable decrease in resistivity values, highlighting the sensitivity of electrical resistance to mixture proportioning even after extended curing periods [4]. Additionally, surface resistivity development in latex-modified cementitious systems differs from conventional concrete, as air curing after initial water curing is critical for pore refinement and resistivity increase [11].

Field Application and Validation

During Phase III of the ternary mixtures project (taylor-2012-ternary-mixtures-field-demo), the Wenner probe was deployed at eight field sites using FHWA’s mobile concrete laboratory, where surface resistivity results (AASHTO TP 95) were consistent with companion rapid chloride permeability tests (ASTM C1202). All eight field mixtures showed low to very low chloride penetrability [2]; specifically, California field data showed 56-day resistivity of 15.2 kΩ·cm, which corresponded to “low” chloride penetration (1,279 coulombs average) [2]. To facilitate such testing, FHWA’s mobile concrete laboratory crews utilize the method to perform field testing on standard 4-inch by 8-inch cylinders at both 28-day and 56-day intervals [2]. While surface resistivity measurements on cored concrete samples often yield lower values than cast specimens due to differences in curing conditions and hydration levels at different depths within a slab [5], field validation of internal curing shows that laboratory and field surface resistivity data are similar, confirming the reliability of lab-based measurements for predicting in-situ performance and supporting the use of standard cylinder testing as a proxy for pavement durability assessment [9].

On bridge decks, surface resistivity measurements can be conducted after soaking the surface for a specified duration to ensure consistent moisture conditions; readings taken after 30 minutes of soaking yielded average values of 52.7 kΩ·cm for internal curing sections and 50.3 kΩ·cm for control sections [3]. Long-term field monitoring shows that surface resistivity increases over time, as readings rose to 73.9 kΩ·cm for internal curing sections and 70.7 kΩ·cm for control sections after approximately one year in service [3]. However, in field applications on overlay surfaces, seasonal variations such as late fall conditions can cause unstable and highly fluctuating surface resistivity readings even when standard wetting procedures are followed [11].

Measurement Artifacts and Conditioning

Surface resistivity measurements are highly sensitive to post-curing drying conditions, as allowing wet cylinders to dry for just 50 minutes before testing can increase resistivity readings by up to 24%, necessitating strict standardization of exposure time [1]. Furthermore, when measuring resistivity after immersion in ionic deicing solutions, results may be biased upward because the penetrating ions increase the concrete’s conductivity [7]. Specific storage solutions also significantly impact measurement consistency; for example, surface resistivity values exhibit high variability when specimens are conditioned in lime water compared to other conditioning methods, suggesting that standardizing the conditioning protocol is critical for reliable quality control assessments [4].

Surface resistivity versus age for various locations [4]

Measurement accuracy is further influenced by specimen age and contact methods. When measuring surface resistivity on older, drier specimens such as those aged 170 days or more, a two-day water soaking period is required to stabilize readings, as merely wetting the probe electrodes is insufficient for adequate contact [11]. Additionally, the use of a saturated cloth between the Wenner probe and the concrete surface can significantly bias resistivity readings downward; for instance, using four layers of wet cloth resulted in a 58% reduction in measured values compared to direct contact [11].

Surface resistivity measurement using four-point Wenner probe [11]

Embedded Sensor Monitoring

Embedded Decagon 5TE sensors can simultaneously measure relative dielectric permittivity, electrical resistivity, and temperature at specific depths within concrete pavements, with data transmitted via cellular networks to remote research teams. These sensors are typically installed in pairs at shallow (1 inch) and deeper (5.5 inches) depths below the pavement surface. [9]

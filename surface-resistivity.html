Surface Resistivity

Part of: Low-Permeability Concrete is-evaluative-test-of
Surface Resistivity is an evaluative test method of Low-Permeability Concrete.
aashtodurabilityelectrical-resistivitytest-methodtransport-properties
Also known as: AASHTO T 358, electrical resistivity
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-11

Surface resistivity is a nondestructive electrical test method that serves as a rapid, practical evaluation tool for low-permeability concrete by measuring electrical resistivity to indicate permeability [5]. This approach provides a fast assessment requiring less than five minutes and no sample preparation, offering a cost-effective alternative to more time-consuming procedures such as the Rapid Chloride Penetration Test (RCPT) [5]. The resulting measurements act as a proxy for estimating chloride ion diffusion resistance, which is the primary indicator of permeability in these mixtures, thereby evaluating the ability of water-saturated concrete to resist moisture penetration [5].

Overview and Advantages

  • Nondestructive testing
  • No chemicals required
  • Faster (5 minutes vs. 6 hours)
  • Specimens can be retested
  • Lower cost
  • Growing popularity among agencies

Test Methods and Techniques

AASHTO T 358 measures the electrical resistivity of saturated concrete cylindrical specimens (100 mm or 150 mm diameter) using a four-pin Wenner probe array [1]. During this nondestructive test, which takes approximately 5 minutes and allows specimens to be tested again at later ages or used for other testing, an AC potential difference is applied across the outer pins to generate current flow while the two inner probes measure the resulting potential difference to calculate resistivity in Ohms-cm [1]. Because surface resistivity measurements only penetrate a depth equal to the probe spacing and may reflect higher paste content at the concrete surface, whereas bulk resistivity tests measure conductivity through the entire specimen depth [2], alternative methods are utilized. The uniaxial resistivity test, also known as the bulk resistivity test, passes current through the length of a specimen using electrode plates rather than surface probes, sampling a larger volume of material and reducing sensitivity to surface variations [1]. This method requires attaching pins to plates for use with four-pin devices but offers simpler geometric correction compared to surface testing [1].

Schematic of the four-point Wenner array probe setup [1]

The Wenner technique requires geometric correction to account for three-dimensional current flow interference caused by the concrete-air interface on cylindrical specimens; specifically, readings from a 10 cm by 20 cm cylinder with 5.1 cm probe spacing must be divided by a factor of K=2.7 to convert them to equivalent semi-infinite slab resistivity [2]. Additionally, AASHTO T277 testing induces specimen heating that varies with permeability, requiring a Joule effect adjustment to normalize data, as mixtures with higher permeability experience larger temperature rises and thus require greater correction factors than low-permeability mixes [2].

AASHTO T277 testing apparatus [2]

Relationship to Chloride Penetration

Surface resistivity measures the ability of concrete to resist the transport of conductive ions, such as chloride ions, through the pore solution, linking electrical resistance directly to ionic diffusion mechanisms [3]. The Louisiana Department of Transportation and Development (LADOTD) utilizes specific criteria for assessing surface resistivity to classify chloride penetrability in concrete [4]. These surface resistivity values correlate to RCPT classifications, where high permeability is <9.7 kOhm-cm (corresponds to >4,000 coulombs), moderate is 9.7-19.3 kOhm-cm (2,000-4,000 coulombs), low is 19.3-38.6 kOhm-cm (1,000-2,000 coulombs), very low is 38.6-386 kOhm-cm (100-1,000 coulombs), and negligible is >386 kOhm-cm (<100 coulombs) [1]. For example, a 56-day surface resistivity measurement can yield specific values such as 15.2 kOhm-cm, which falls within the moderate permeability range when compared to Rapid Chloride Permeability Test classifications [5]. When evaluating transport properties, specifications may mandate a maximum charge passed in the Rapid Chloride Permeability Test (RCPT) at an equivalent age of 91 days as an alternative to resistivity measurements [6].

Rapid chloride permeability test (left) and surface resistivity meter (right) [5]

Higher compressive strength does not inherently guarantee higher surface resistivity or improved durability; in many instances, increasing cement content to boost strength may result in lower resistivity values due to increased permeability [1]. This decoupling of mechanical and transport properties necessitates separate evaluation criteria for structural and durability performance [1].

Scatter data for surface resistivity versus compressive strength [1]

Factors Affecting Resistivity Results

Probe spacing significantly impacts measurement accuracy by mitigating large aggregate interference; specifically, a 2-inch probe spacing reduces this interference compared to shorter spacings, provided the spacing exceeds twice the diameter of the largest aggregate size [2]. To normalize surface resistivity results across different geometries, conditioning methods, and pore solution conductivities, the formation (F) factor is calculated, which provides a more fundamental measure of transport properties as it is directly related to concrete pore volume and connectivity [1]. Because surface resistivity results depend on sample geometry, test temperature, degree of saturation, and sample storage conditions, adherence to published testing standards is essential for reliable results and standardized conditioning methods are critical for repeatability [1]. For instance, the PEM project found higher variability for samples conditioned in lime water compared to other methods [1], and standardizing drying time before testing is critical because leaving wet-cured cylinders out for 50 minutes can increase resistivity readings by 24%, introducing significant variability if not controlled [2]. Furthermore, seasonal temperature variations significantly impact field measurements; highly fluctuating surface resistivity readings have been specifically observed during late fall trips compared to stable readings in spring due to environmental effects on the concrete’s moisture and thermal state [3].

Surface resistivity versus age for various locations [1]

Field Implementation and Monitoring

Surface resistivity testing can be performed on cylinders cast as quality assurance specimens for strength testing, which allows the same samples to be used for both mechanical and transport property evaluations after wet curing [5]. While cored samples typically exhibit lower surface resistivity values compared to cast samples due to differences in curing conditions between the two specimen types [4], field-resistivity data from cored samples taken directly from pavements show values similar to those obtained from laboratory-cast cylinders, validating the use of field cores for durability assessment [7]. For bridge decks, measurements can be conducted after a 30-minute soaking period to ensure adequate saturation before testing [8]. In field applications on bridge deck overlays, using a saturated wet cloth between the Wenner probe and the surface stabilizes fluctuating readings but introduces a conductivity artifact that requires correction; specifically, four layers of wet cloth reduce measured resistivity by approximately 58% compared to direct contact measurements [3]. Additionally, testing on freshly cast overlays can be performed as early as three hours after placement by placing electrodes directly on the moist surface, allowing for immediate assessment of hydration heat and initial pore structure development [3]. To provide real-time data on moisture and transport properties in field pavements, embedded Decagon 5TE sensors can continuously monitor electrical resistivity at specific depths, such as 1 inch and 5.5 inches below the surface [7].

Surface resistivity measurement using four-point Wenner probe [3]

Field specifications may mandate a minimum surface resistivity of 10 kΩ/cm at 28 days to ensure adequate durability performance alongside other requirements like sulfate expansion limits [5]. Consequently, multiple states have adopted surface resistivity specifications: Colorado requires surface resistivity >12 kOhm-cm at 28 days or RCPT <2,500 coulombs; North Carolina is investigating a 28-day target correlating to 56/90-day durability performance; West Virginia has purchased surface resistivity meters for each district; and Connect is developing a resistivity specification with a 2023 implementation target. The impact of mixture design was demonstrated in field testing on a bridge deck using internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate, which showed average surface resistivity values of 52.7 kOhm-cm for the test section and 50.3 kOhm-cm for the control section after one year, indicating improved durability performance in the modified mixture [8].

Material Composition and Mix Design

Increasing the water-to-cementitious materials ratio leads to a decrease in surface resistivity, with 56-day measurements showing an exponential regression trend where higher w/cm ratios correspond to lower resistivity values [1]. This inverse relationship highlights how mixture proportioning directly impacts the concrete’s ability to resist moisture penetration [1]. In contrast, ternary cementitious mixtures significantly reduce diffusion coefficients and increase surface resistivity compared to pure portland cement controls, with many ternary combinations achieving higher compressive strengths than the control by 28 days despite potential early-age strength delays from high SCM replacement levels [5]. Furthermore, high fly ash content mixtures require extended curing periods to achieve optimal low chloride ion penetrability classifications, as resistivity values continue to increase over time [4].

Surface resistivity versus age line data for various conditioning methods [1]

Internal curing techniques also influence these properties; for instance, internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) improves surface resistivity at ages greater than 7 days compared to control mixtures, indicating enhanced hydration despite moist storage conditions [7]. The use of superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) presents a more complex profile, as the incorporation of SAPs as internal curing agents slightly reduces the electrical resistivity of concrete, although these values typically remain within acceptable durability bounds [9]. Additionally, specimens containing superabsorbent polymers exhibit higher secondary water absorption ability compared to control concrete at ages ranging from 1 to 7 days, indicating greater permeability correlated with the porosity and swelling capacity of the SAPs [9].

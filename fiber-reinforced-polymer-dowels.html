Fiber-Reinforced Polymer Dowels

Part of: Dowel Bar is-corrosion-resistant-alternative-to
Fiber-Reinforced Polymer Dowels is a corrosion-resistant alternative type of Dowel Bar.
dowelfrpmaterialspavement
Also known as: FRP dowels, GFRP dowels, fiberglass dowels, glass fiber-reinforced polymer dowels
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-11

Fiber-Reinforced Polymer Dowels are a specific type of Dowel bar constructed from glass fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) or other fiber composites, serving as corrosion-resistant alternatives to traditional steel dowel bars in concrete pavements [1]. This substitution is particularly beneficial in environments exposed to deicing salts, which create harsh conditions that deteriorate steel components [1], as utilizing FRP materials significantly reduces the risk of galvanic and moisture-induced corrosion compared to standard steel options [1]. While epoxy-coated steel dowels remain common, these composite dowels are validated through Accelerated aging, a primary testing methodology that utilizes heated alkaline solutions to simulate long-term environmental degradation [1]. This process specifically evaluates how GFRP materials resist chemical breakdown and mechanical failure under sustained stress, distinguishing the validation method from the structural component itself [1].

Advantages of GFRP Dowels

Because they are corrosion-resistant, Glasform GFRP dowels require no maintenance over the pavement lifespan. Their larger diameters (typically 1.5 in vs 1.25 in steel) reduce bearing stresses on concrete, while their lower flexural stiffness reduces bearing stress concentration at the joint face. In scaled model testing under static and cyclic loads, Glasform GFRP dowels consistently achieved joint effectiveness above 90 percent, outperforming both epoxy-coated steel dowels (which exceeded 75 percent) and FiberDowel products [1]. Ultimately, all dowel types performed adequately under loading.

Lower Modulus And Stiffness

Because a lower modulus of elasticity requires closer spacing or larger diameters, GFRP dowels exhibit lower $k_0$ values than steel or stainless steel due to their softer material properties and larger diameters, which increase the dowel-concrete bearing area [2]. Indeed, GFRP dowels had the lowest K₀ of all types, attributed to softer material with lower flexural rigidity.

Theoretical and measured moments, round GFRP specimen, east dowel, 0-inch joint [2]

The polymer matrix of FRP dowels is hydroscopic and can absorb water, potentially causing swelling that may interfere with the slippage mechanism required for joint function over time [3]. Consequently, moisture effects, aging, and chemical resistance remain research gaps; therefore, the study recommended future hygroscopic testing of GFRP to determine swelling effects on joint behavior, and long-term exposure testing against alkali deicing solutions [2]. Additionally, strain gage monitoring of elliptical FRP dowels was terminated due to sensor failures, highlighting challenges in long-term instrumentation durability under field conditions [4].

Location of strain gages on elliptical FRP dowel bars [3]

Moisture absorption in FRP dowels can also cause expansion, potentially increasing stress within the surrounding concrete and leading to cracks or oblonging of the installation hole [1].

During dynamic field testing, GFRP dowels exhibit higher joint deflections compared to steel, a behavior that is directly correlated with their lower shear strength and flexural stiffness modulus [1].

Relative deflection between adjacent pavement slabs [1]

Design Considerations and Spacing Requirements

Because steel design guidelines are not applicable to GFRP, specific considerations for fiberglass dowel bars must be addressed; for instance, 1.5-in. GFRP dowels at 12-in. spacing are inadequate for design life, whereas 6-in. spacing is effective [1]. Furthermore, one-inch diameter fiberglass dowel bars are not recommended for rigid pavement applications, as their performance was found to be similar to that of 1.5-inch steel dowels but without sufficient benefit to justify their use [5]. European research indicates that shorter dowel bars may be as effective as longer ones, and smaller diameter dowels spaced more closely (e.g., at 10-inch intervals) can produce good pavement performance [6], though dowel bars located outside the wheel paths have little to no effect on critical pavement response and could potentially be omitted without adversely affecting pavement performance [6].

Spacing Factor in Front and behind the Paver for GA, LA, and OK [5]

Regarding analytical modeling, Timoshenko’s finite beam theory requires accurate boundary conditions to calculate the deflected shape of the dowel, but these conditions are difficult to verify in field installations [3]. Additionally, the modulus of dowel support (k) must be adjusted depending on the level of applied load even within the joint’s global linear range, as Timoshenko’s analytical expression fails to account for complex nonlinear behavior associated with locally high bearing stresses [1]. The presence of a gap in the joint (e.g., 1/8-inch or 1/2-inch) is critical for accurate $k_0$ measurement, as cold joints without gaps exhibit significantly higher values due to arching action and frictional forces between blocks that artificially reduce deflection [2].

Slope and deflection of dowel at joint face [1]

Specifically, one-inch diameter fiberglass dowel bars are not recommended for rigid pavements because their performance is similar to that of 1.5-inch steel dowels but offers insufficient structural benefit [5].

Elliptical FRP Dowel Performance

The FHWA Project 5 field evaluation (Porter et al. 2005) studied elliptical FRP dowels (2.25 x 1.25 in, 18 in long) installed on Iowa Highway 330 at three spacings (10, 12, 15 inches). During the monitoring period, which included four testing periods from 2003–2005, $K_0$ was calculated from FWD testing for each spacing, with the 10-inch spacing producing the highest $K_0$ values. Under 830 lbf crawl truck loading, the 10-inch spacing produced a maximum deflection of 0.56 mils (0.014 mm), while faulting measurements ranged 0.1–0.8 mm with no significant trends between spacings; however, joints with 15-inch spacing showed ~1.0 mm change vs ~0.6 mm for 10- and 12-inch. No visual distress was recorded throughout the monitoring period, and the lab-tested $K_0$ for elliptical FRP was 939,000 psi [3]. While elliptical dowel bars provide a larger bearing area to reduce concrete crushing, they induce weak-axis flexural bending, which lowers the modulus of dowel support ($k_0$) compared to circular dowels [2].

Theoretical and measured moments, elliptical GFRP specimen, east dowel, 1/8-inch joint [2]

Heavy elliptical FRP dowels with a major axis of 1.969 inches and minor axis of 1.338 inches provide a cross-sectional area of 2.084 square inches, offering increased bearing capacity compared to standard round bars [4]. Field evaluations indicate that elliptical dowels achieve higher load transfer values in fill sections compared to transition sections, where performance levels are lowest [4]. In transition sections, joint openings show greater variation when wheel-path-only dowel baskets are used compared to full-width basket designs, a difference not observed in cut or fill sections [4]. Furthermore, heavy elliptical dowels spaced at 18 inches produced the most desirable ride quality, whereas 12- and 15-inch spacings resulted in higher International Roughness Index values [4].

Passing Lane, Fill Section, Heavy Oval Bar, 15 in Spacing, Full Basket [4]

Laboratory Studies: Round vs. Elliptical GFRP

A comprehensive laboratory study evaluated round GFRP (1.875 in. dia.) and elliptical GFRP (2.25 × 1.25 in.) dowels alongside steel and stainless steel controls under modified AASHTO T253 and cantilever shear tests. For the round GFRP, the properties were recorded as E = 6.51 × 10⁶ psi, I = 0.6067 in⁴, and EI = 3.95 × 10⁶ lb-in², with a K₀ = 400,000 pci (1/2-in. joint). The elliptical GFRP exhibited E = 8.66 × 10⁶ psi, I = 0.2157 in⁴, and EI = 1.87 × 10⁶ lb-in², with a K₀ = 500,000 pci (1/2-in. joint). Additionally, loss on ignition (ASTM D 2584-02) testing determined the resin content to be 24.83% for elliptical GFRP and 31.75% for round GFRP.

Testing Methodologies and Instrumentation

Elemental evaluation was conducted using a modified Iosipescu shear test, while full-scale slab testing involved simulated truck loading for millions of cycles. Field evaluations utilized the Falling Weight Deflectometer and Road Rater, and the AASHTO T253 Test was applied to elliptical GFRP dowels in laboratory specimens [3]. This modified AASHTO T253 test improves upon standard methods by using two linear loads parallel to the joint instead of one, which enhances direct shear behavior and provides more reliable $k_0$ values [2]. Furthermore, instrumentation data indicates that moments induced by slab curling are significantly higher than those developed during nondestructive testing, and forces measured in fiberglass dowels are consistently lower than those in steel dowels [5].

Iosipescu shear test [2]

Fiber-Reinforced Polymer Dowels undergo accelerated aging testing to simulate long-term environmental degradation and evaluate how exposure to elevated temperatures and moisture affects their mechanical integrity over time [1]. This laboratory procedure allows researchers to assess the durability of these components under simulated conditions, such as direct exposure to highly alkaline solutions which can rapidly reduce ultimate tensile strength [1]. For instance, studies have shown that 19 days of accelerated aging is equivalent to approximately 5.4 years of real-time aging, demonstrating significant reductions in material properties compared to unaged specimens [1].

Material Composition And Dosage

In other pavement applications, fiber reinforcement is typically dosed at a rate of 0.2% to 0.5% by volume in overlays between 4 and 6 inches thick to target a residual strength of approximately 20% to 30% of the design flexural strength [7].

Installation And Construction Methods

Installation of FRP dowels requires plastic ties and epoxy rather than welding, utilizing a brittle epoxy formulation that cracks to permit longitudinal movement after the surrounding concrete cures [3].

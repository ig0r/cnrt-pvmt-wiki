Faultmeter

Part of: Joint Faulting is-measurement-device-for
Faultmeter is the measurement device for Joint Faulting.
jointmeasurementpavementtesting
Also known as: Concrete Pavement Fault Gauge, Joint Faulting Measurement Device, Pavement Joint Displacement Meter
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

A faultmeter is a field instrument designed to quantify vertical misalignment, known as fault, across pavement joints by assessing the relative elevation of adjacent concrete slabs. The device typically employs a probe that contacts the slab surface and a displacement transducer that converts the physical movement into a digital reading indicating positive or negative offset in millimeters [4]. By positioning the instrument on one side of the joint, the measured difference directly reflects the amount of vertical offset between the two slab faces [4].

Pavement and Joint Fault Measurement Methodologies

Pavement fault measurement methodologies focus on quantifying the vertical misalignment that occurs across joints between adjacent concrete slabs. Field instruments achieve this by positioning a probe against one slab face while measuring its relative elevation compared to the opposing side using displacement transducers [4]. The resulting digital readings indicate the magnitude and direction of the vertical offset, providing critical data on joint performance [4].

Research problem — Existing instrumentation lacks sufficient resolution to capture subtle vertical differential displacements at transverse joints, preventing reliable statistical analysis of distress trends and comparative evaluations of dowel performance [4]. There is a critical need for accurate field measurement methodologies that can effectively quantify joint faulting to assess load transfer efficiency, ride quality, and the long-term structural integrity of concrete pavement overlays [3]. Current predictive models fail to consistently estimate joint faulting over full design lives, creating uncertainty regarding how unbonded overlays react to climate and traffic loads and hindering informed maintenance decisions [2] [5].

State of the art — Current practice relies on FHWA-approved digital faultmeters, such as the standard LTPP model or Gilson units, to measure pavement and joint faulting with high precision [1] [3]. Field protocols typically involve taking measurements at specific locations relative to wheel paths, often using multiple devices simultaneously for efficiency, followed by temperature normalization of the raw data [3]. Despite the capability for high-resolution measurement, observed faulting values in many operational contexts are so small that they are effectively treated as zero for decision-making purposes due to instrument and placement errors [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Demonstrated that changes in dowel spacing did not produce a consistent effect on joint faulting levels for steel dowels [3].
  • Observed that elliptical steel dowels produced less joint faulting than conventional round dowels [3].
  • Found that joint faulting was similar in cut-and-fill sections but tended to be higher in transition sections [3].

Novelty & rigor — The methodology is distinguished by its use of dual faultmeters with differing sensitivities to capture joint displacement, followed by averaging the readings from opposing travel directions to mitigate directional bias [1]. Reproducibility is ensured through strict adherence to fixed probe placement relative to wheel paths and the application of temperature normalization to a standard reference point, which allows for consistent comparison across varying environmental conditions [1] [3]. The approach relies on conventional instrumentation rather than novel device development, focusing instead on rigorous protocol standardization and simultaneous dual-lane data acquisition to enhance efficiency and reliability [2] [4].

Implications — Designers should prioritize dowel shape over precise spacing when aiming to control joint faulting, as spacing variations do not consistently influence fault measurements [3]. Transition zones require specific design or construction attention because they exhibit greater faulting regardless of the dowel type used [3]. Long-term performance trends for elliptical dowels cannot be reliably established using current measurement resolutions, necessitating caution in interpreting short-term field data [4].

Limitations — Instrument resolution limits and placement errors render small measured fault values indistinguishable from zero, preventing confident linkage to specific design variables [1]. Data magnitudes often approach the lower detection threshold of monitoring equipment, while short observation periods preclude the establishment of statistically reliable long-term trends [4]. High variability in measurements across similar sections and inconsistent patterns obscure clear performance trends, necessitating further data collection before definitive conclusions can be drawn [3].

Faultmeter Monitoring Results

Research problem — Researchers need to determine whether vertical joint movements recorded by faultmeters exceed instrument resolution limits to distinguish true pavement deformation from measurement noise [1]. There is a need to understand how faulting evolves over the service life of unbonded overlays and how different base-preparation methods influence the magnitude of this movement [1].

The figure below illustrates a specific faulting scenario characterized by a 3.5-degree depth in an HMA surface without fibers.

Average faulting measurements over time for two pavement sections. [1]

The data illustrates how this relative elevation difference between adjacent slabs evolves and fluctuates, sometimes showing positive offset and other times negative offset.

Findings from the reports

  • Faultmeter monitoring showed that measured faulting was essentially negligible for practical pavement performance, with movements recorded within a narrow band across all test sections [1].
  • The addition of synthetic fibers did not produce a measurable change in faulting, and neither panel dimensions nor overlay depth had any significant effect on the results [1].
  • Base preparation method was the only factor that influenced faulting magnitude, with scarified or patched bases consistently yielding lower faulting values than sections prepared with an asphaltic concrete stress-relief layer [1].
  • Faulting measurements followed a sinusoidal trend over time for each slab configuration, indicating cyclic movement in the pavement structure [1].

Novelty & rigor — The methodology introduces a systematic, calibrated deployment of faultmeters across diverse thin-PCC overlay designs, normalizing all measurements to a common temperature to ensure consistency [1]. This approach uniquely tracks faulting over several years by averaging traffic directions, revealing that synthetic fibers and panel dimensions do not significantly impact recorded faulting values [1]. The study further distinguishes itself by identifying a sinusoidal temporal evolution of faulting and demonstrating how base preparation methods, such as scarification or patching, moderate this behavior compared to stress-relief layers [1].

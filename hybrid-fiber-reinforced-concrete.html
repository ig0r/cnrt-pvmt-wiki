Hybrid Fiber-Reinforced Concrete

Part of: Fiber-Reinforced Concrete
bridge-deckscompositeconcretefiber-reinforcement
Also known as: hybrid FRC
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

Fiber-reinforced concrete containing both microfibers and macrofibers, designed to address cracking at multiple scales. Microfibers control low tensile stresses (such as shrinkage tension) at the microcrack level, while macrofibers contribute to post-peak strength when macrocracks form from coalesced microcracks.

Dual-Scale Reinforcement Strategy

The dual-scale reinforcement strategy targets the full lifecycle of crack development:

  1. Microfibers bridge microcracks in the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) and cement matrix during early ages
  2. Macrofibers bridge macrocracks that form from the growth of multiple microcracks, providing post-peak ductility and residual strength

Fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) containing both microfibers and macrofibers can leverage the benefits of both types; microfibers control low tensile stresses, such as shrinkage tension, while macrofibers contribute to post-peak strength when macrocracks form .

This combination provides both early-age cracking resistance and post-crack ductility .

Hybrid Fiber Combinations in TR-767 Study

Stage 3 of the TR-767 study investigated hybrid FRC combinations with:

  • PP microfibers at 0.0625% and 0.125% volume
  • PP, AR glass, and PVA macrofibers at 0.125%, 0.1875%, 0.25%, and 0.50% volume

Key findings:

Performance of AR Glass Macrofibers

AR glass macrofibers showed the best synergy with PP microfibers, and at low macrofiber dosages (0.125%, 0.1875%), AR glass FRC had the highest flexural strength. Furthermore, microfibers improved AR glass FRC toughness more than PVA FRC toughness.

At low dosages (0.125%), AR glass macrofibers can increase compressive strength by 5% at 56 days due to high stiffness, but higher dosages reduce strength by introducing air voids from packing difficulties that microfibers help mitigate [1].

Compressive strength of hybrid FRC with AR glass macrofibers [1]

Performance of PVA Macrofibers

Due to their water absorption capability, PVA macrofibers require higher water reducer demand than PP or AR glass macrofibers [1], which also reduces workability more than AR glass or PP and marginally decreases compressive strength [1]. However, at a 0.25% macrofiber dosage, PVA FRC outperformed others in flexural strength.

Water reducer demand of hybrid FRC mixes [1]

Impact on Durability and Mechanical Strength

The addition of PP microfibers reduces mean chloride penetration depth by 7.9% at 0.25%, 15.2% at 0.50%, and 19.1% at 1.0% volume compared to control concrete, though this incremental benefit diminishes at higher dosages [1]. Furthermore, increasing macrofiber dosage generally increases splitting tensile strength across all microfiber contents by providing additional load-bearing conduits, although most hybrid combinations still show lower tensile strength than control concrete due to higher porosity [1].

Rapid chloride migration test results for (a) mean chloride penetration depth and (b) migration coefficient [1]

Recommended Fiber Combination

0.125% PP microfiber + 0.25% AR glass macrofiber — satisfies practical constraints (workability) and provides suitable pre- and post-peak mechanical properties.

Field Implementation and Mixing Techniques

In field applications, structural fibers are added at 3 pounds per cubic yard of concrete alongside polypropylene monofilament and fibrillated fibers, with inclusion managed via a hopper system that blows the fibers directly into the mixing drum [2]. To ensure uniform distribution and prevent clumping during pneumatic inclusion, operators may need to modify blower suction by placing an obstruction, such as a cardboard piece, over the blower opening to increase blowing power [3] [2].

Adjustment to blower for better fiber dispersion [3]

Surface Finishing and Practical Challenges

The inclusion of structural fibers can cause surface finishing issues where tining makes the fibers stand up, creating a “hairy” appearance that is subsequently worn away by highway traffic [2]. Because these fibers at the end-of-day joints caused finishing difficulties, the practice of omitting fibers from the last two truckloads of the day and the first two truckloads of the next morning was adopted to ensure smoother headers [3].

A test method for determining the air void structure of fresh mortar or concrete by measuring the buoyancy change as air voids are released from a sample mixed with glycerol and water. Used extensively in the Phase I Ternary Mixtures study to characterize spacing factor, specific surface, and percent of air voids smaller than 300 µm. [1]

The Air Void Analyzer is recommended as a routine quality control tool for paving projects due to its reliable results. [2]

Placement activities such as slipform paving can reduce the quantity of entrained air in concrete by up to 2 percent. [2]

Key Air Void Parameters

  • Spacing factor: average distance between air voids (target ≤ 0.2 mm for freeze-thaw durability)
  • Specific surface: surface area of air voids per unit volume (target 23–43 mm⁻¹)
  • % D < 300 µm: percent of air voids smaller than 300 µm — the effective size for freeze-thaw protection

Testing Procedure and Equipment

The AVA device is limited to measuring air voids with a diameter equal to or less than 2 mm, which means it does not capture larger entrained air bubbles that are typically included in gravimetric measurements [3]. The AVA testing procedure involves placing water into the riser column and filling the bottom with a blue AVA-2240 release liquid using a special funnel, which has a specified volume requirement of 200 ml per test; the remaining riser cylinder is then filled with water to facilitate bubble rise [1] [4]. This viscous fluid allows air bubbles released from the mortar sample to ascend at rates governed by Stokes’ Law based on their size [4], and a blue fluid viscosity of 0.075–0.130 Pa·s produced optimal air-void measurements [5]. Specifically, a viscosity range of 0.075 to 0.130 Pa·s yields the highest air content and lowest spacing factor values [5].

Bubbles Rising from the Blue AVA Liquid [4]

Mortar samples for testing are extracted using a syringe from a wire cage that has been vibrated into fresh concrete with a percussion drill, excluding aggregate larger than 6 mm [4]. The sample is then injected into the viscous liquid at the bottom of the riser cylinder and mixed for 30 seconds with a magnetic stir bar to release air voids recorded as a mass change over time on an inverted glass dish [1] [4]. The device utilizes a buoyancy recorder bowl attached to a balance at the top of the cylinder where rising bubbles collect, measuring the change in buoyancy over time as air is released from the mortar sample into the liquid medium [4]. Testing duration is recorded for 25 minutes or until no weight change occurs for three consecutive minutes, with results typically available in under an hour [4]; the test can take up to 30 minutes to complete [1]. Computer software uses an algorithm to report specific surface, spacing factor, and total air content based on the buoyancy recorder bowl’s weight changes over time [4], while displaying the cumulative distribution of air voids, a histogram of the air voids, and calculated values of spacing factor and specific surface [1]. Additionally, the rapid air void analysis system utilizes automated image analysis on polished samples with a surface enhancement technique that produces a black background with white air voids to determine specific surface and spacing factor [6]. Separately, calorimetry tests conducted using an AdiaCal device recorded peak values reaching approximately 105°F at 14.6 hours for one mixture [7], and the rapid chloride permeability test measures electrical conductance as resistance to chloride ion penetration [7].

Material Composition and Interactions

In experimental studies, two types of coarse aggregate were utilized, specifically MDOT gradation 6AA highcalcium limestone with 1.73% absorption and MDOT gradation 29A granite with 2.33% absorption [7], while fine aggregate consisted of MDOT gradation 2NS natural sand with 1.2% absorption [7]. Chemical admixtures included a Micro-Air Type AE air entraining agent, Delvo retarding agent (ASTM Type D), and Rheobuild 1000 (Type MR) water reducer, all sourced from BASF [7]. For ternary blends, a performance-based cement meeting ASTM C 1157 containing 10% ground limestone flour was used, utilizing the same clinker as the control cement blended with limestone flour [7]. Microwave water-cementitious materials ratio tests yielded results of 0.44 and 0.45, compared to a design value of 0.42 [7]. Concrete mixtures were tested for rapid chloride permeability using ASTM C1202, with results indicating a classification of ‘very low’ permeability of chloride [7]. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is utilized to determine the minerals present in bulk samples and selected paste specimens, while glass content of various supplementary cementitious materials and blended cements is estimated using semi-quantitative XRD analysis [7].

Regarding material properties, fly ash generally increases the setting time of mixtures, whereas slag cement and silica fume do not show a significant impact on setting time [7]; specifically, a low-range water reducer demonstrated a significant reduction in time to set when used with Class C fly ash, an effect not observed with other admixtures or fly ashes [7]. Metakaolin use in concrete tends to increase water demand requiring larger dosages of water-reducing admixture and increases the demand for air-entraining admixture comparable to silica fume concrete [1], as silica fume’s tremendous surface area often requires the use of high-range water reducers, while carbon particles in the material may cause air-entrainment issues [1]. In IP cement mixtures, achieving stable air entrainment requires increasing the dosage of the air-entraining admixture as the water-to-binder ratio decreases and the total amount of cementitious material increases [3]. Water-to-cement ratios between 0.35 and 0.48 cause AVA total air content to increase while spacing factor decreases, but increasing the ratio further to 0.55 reverses this trend due to large air voids that the analyzer fails to capture [5]. In two-stage mixing processes, the air void spacing factor generally increases as total air content decreases, making concrete more susceptible to freeze-thaw deterioration if admixture dosages are not adjusted [6]; furthermore, when air-entraining agent is added directly to the slurry in two-stage mixing, entrainment becomes difficult due to the limited ability of fine binder materials to trap air, resulting in a weak correlation between total air content and spacing factor [6]. While most mixtures met the 0.2 mm threshold for air void spacing factor, some mixtures containing Class F fly ash failed to meet the minimum specific surface criteria of 23–43 mm⁻¹ (600–1000 in.⁻¹) with certain water-reducing admixtures [7].

MCO study—distribution of AVA spacing factor [5]

Phase I Findings: Ternary Mixes

Strength development was evaluated using 28/7-day compressive strength ratios [7], revealing a good correlation between spacing factor, specific surface, and % D < 300 µm. An increasing spacing factor was associated with increasing compressive strength (up to ~31,000 kPa) [7]. While blended cements (TIP, TISM, TIPM) generally produced better air void structure, high LOI Class F fly ash significantly reduced specific surface and required polycarboxylate HRWR for adequate air entrainment [1]. Furthermore, concrete mixtures containing supplementary cementitious materials generally performed well in reducing the severity of surface scaling after 50 cycles, with the exception of Iowa mixtures utilizing Class C fly ash [7].

Phase I Findings: AVA Performance and Correlation

The AVA test results correlate closely with hardened concrete measurements using ASTM C457, showing agreement within 10% accuracy [4]. This correlation allows the fresh concrete analysis to serve as a reliable proxy for the air void structure typically measured only after hardening [4]. While AVA variability (CVs) differs from C231 and C457 by < 15%—which is considered acceptable—the AVA total air content is ~50% of C231/C457 values (~2.8% vs ~5.8% gravimetric) [5]. For example, an AVA spacing factor of 0.012 in. corresponds to AVA total air of 3%, C231 air of 5%, and a C457 spacing factor of 0.008 in. [5]. Furthermore, the agreement between AVA and C457 spacing factor at a 0.008 in. criterion is ~50–64% across datasets [5].

Relationship between AVA Air Content, Bubble Size, and Spacing Factor [4]

Small air voids (< 300 µm) correlate much better (R² = 0.82) with spacing factor than total air content, and the frequency of small spacing factor (0.005–0.015 in.) may control concrete quality [5]. Regarding performance standards, based on a spacing factor criterion of ≤ 0.20 mm, acceptable results were achieved in 7 out of 14 samples from the same concrete mix [7].

Phase 3 Study: Deicer Scaling and Repeatability

A Phase 3 repeatability study at Iowa State University measured AVA spacing factors on 28 slag cement concrete mixtures, which revealed a poor correlation between AVA and C457 spacing factor [8]. This finding is consistent with earlier AVA studies and suggests that the two methods measure different aspects of the air void system [8]. Although increasing the spacing factor by either method correlated with better scaling performance, this relationship was stronger for ASTM C672 data than for the new method [8].

While the Air Void Analyzer is recommended for routine quality control, its use as a process monitoring tool is currently preferred over a strict acceptance criterion because a definitive threshold distinguishing good from bad concrete has not yet been established [9].

Field Testing and Deployment

Field testing has recorded fresh concrete air contents ranging from 3.5 percent to 9.0 percent, with the lowest measurements occurring during California field tests [7]. In other field tests, air content ranged from 5.2% to 9.0%, with an average of 7.3% over nine tests conducted [7]. Slump results in field testing varied from a maximum of 7.5 inches to a minimum of 3.0 inches [7], while unit weight measurements ranged from 142.4 lb/ft³ to 135.6 lb/ft³, with an average value of 138.9 lb/ft³ [7]. Additionally, eight temperature sensors were deployed to track concrete and ambient temperatures during construction [7].

Temperature sensor data from MDOT [7]

The AVA can be deployed in a mobile lab using an isolation base with a three-legged stand lowered through a floor portal, protected by a weather shield and isolated from trailer vibrations to ensure accurate buoyancy measurements [4]. This setup allows for real-time quality control adjustments on the jobsite without external vibration interference [4].

Air Void Analyzer [4]

Regulatory Applications and Specifications

When evaluating the effectiveness of air entrainment, a durability factor greater than 85% is consistently achieved if the concrete contains at least 6% total air and maintains a spacing factor of 0.2 mm or less [3]; consequently, proposed acceptance criteria suggest an AVA spacing factor ≤ 0.012 in. for concrete durability factor ≥ 85%. Driven by premature joint distress caused by poor air void spacing despite sound aggregates, the Kansas Department of Transportation developed specifications in 2002 based on AVA data, establishing a minimum total air content tied to a maximum spacing factor of 0.25 mm for prequalification and jobsite verification [4].

Relationship between durability factor and % air and spacing factor of air entrained samples, obtained by AVA [3]

In Utah, state specifications require a minimum cementitious material content of 470 lbs/yd³ for pavements and limit Class F fly ash replacement to a maximum of 30% when replacing blended hydraulic cement [7]. Furthermore, Utah state specifications prohibit the use of Class C fly ash, while other pozzolans are permitted provided they expand less than 0.1% in ASR testing according to ASTM C 1567 [7].

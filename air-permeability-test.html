Air Permeability Test

Part of: Transport Properties & Permeability
conceptconcrete-durabilitypermeabilitysealer-evaluationtesting
Also known as: air permeability, gas permeability, permeability test
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

Test method measuring the rate of airflow through concrete under a pressure gradient, used to assess the permeability of the concrete pore structure. In the TR-765 study, air permeability was found to be a poor indicator of sealer effectiveness for most product types. [1]

Test Principle and Methodology

To determine permeability, a pressure gradient is applied across a concrete specimen and the resulting airflow is measured, where lower airflow indicates lower permeability; this test typically uses the Figg air permeability method or similar apparatus. The air permeability index is specifically calculated as the negative logarithm of the Darcy coefficient of permeability measured in a falling head permeameter [2]. Alternatively, the University of Cape Town (UCT) method utilizes sealed discs measuring 4 inches (100 mm) in diameter and 2 inches (50 mm) in thickness, applying pressurized air for six hours to monitor pressure decay rates. Rather than steady-state flow, this specific protocol employs the rate of pressure decay to determine diffusion parameters [1].

Air permeability cell [2]

Furthermore, concrete specimens treated with silver nitrate after immersion in sodium chloride solutions reveal white rings around aggregate particles, which indicates that salt solution preferentially penetrates the concrete through the interfacial zone [3].

Interpretation of Air Permeability Index

Interpretation thresholds for the air permeability index classify results as excellent (>10.0), good (9.5 to 10.0), poor (9.0 to 9.5), and very poor (<9.0). [2]

Air (gas) permeability index for concrete [1]

TR-765 Phase 1: Treatment Performance

Phase 1 Findings

  • Pore liners (LS, SC, silane): no measurable reduction in air permeability vs. control
  • Pore blockers (SoTa, SME): some reduction in air permeability
  • Barrier coating (Acrylic): greatest reduction in air permeability

TR-765 Phase 2: Sealer Evaluation Findings

Regarding the findings of Phase 2, there were no significant differences in air permeability observed between untreated and sealer-treated concrete. Furthermore, the test could not distinguish between different sealer types or mechanisms, and the results did not correlate with other measures of sealer performance.

Limitations of Air Permeability Testing

  • Only measures gas-phase transport; does not capture liquid-phase behavior that drives most concrete deterioration
  • Sensitive to specimen moisture condition and temperature
  • Cannot distinguish between pore-lining and pore-blocking mechanisms
  • Does not reflect the hydrophobic effects that are most relevant for freeze-thaw protection
  • Not recommended as a standalone test for sealer evaluation

Factors Influencing Material Permeability

The permeability of concrete is influenced by various material compositions and treatments, such as the observation that increasing cement content beyond a critical minimum can increase air permeability rather than improve it [2]. The amount of fines content (passing the No. 200 sieve) is a key factor influencing permeability, as lab and field measurements show that as fines content increases, permeability decreases dramatically [4]. Aggregate characteristics also play a significant role; specimens containing porous aggregate exhibit significantly higher air permeability compared to those using denser aggregates, with the control mixture showing lower permeability than silane-treated samples, suggesting that aggregate porosity is a dominant factor in gas transport rates independent of surface treatments [1]. Furthermore, concrete mixtures utilizing limestone aggregate demonstrate significantly higher durability compared to those using gravel aggregate, although this difference is primarily attributed to pop-outs from lightweight materials in the gravel rather than permeability alone [3]. In mixtures containing high water-to-cement ratios and gravel aggregate, increasing silica fume content has been observed to improve both absorption and air permeability characteristics [3].

Influence of w/c on the permeability of (a) cement paste and (b) concrete [2]

Chemical additives and curing methods further modify these properties. The application of internal curing using lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) has been shown to improve permeability characteristics in concrete mixtures [5], as internal curing promotes more efficient hydration of supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and slag cement [5] and contributes to lower permeability in concrete members alongside other improved material properties [5]. However, supplementary cementitious materials can significantly alter gas transport properties through microstructural densification; for instance, in mixtures containing Class C fly ash replacing 25% of ordinary Portland cement, pozzolanic pore refinement leads to higher air permeability indices compared to plain OPC concrete [1]. Regarding surface treatments, sealers such as silane and silicone methyl ethyl (SME) reduce air permeability by acting as pore blockers or liners, but this reduction does not correlate with water absorption rates, meaning the test fails to characterize durability performance for concrete elements exposed to wetting and drying cycles where liquid transport is critical [1]. While hydrophobic sealers can transmit vapor to decrease saturation levels in concrete elements not in continuous contact with water, they may adversely affect durability in continuously saturated conditions because the test’s focus on gas movement overlooks the pumping effect where sealers trap water behind the treated layer, accelerating deterioration during freeze-thaw cycles [1].

In-Situ Application Methods

An air permeability test device has been developed to provide a rapid measure (less than 30 seconds) of in situ saturated hydraulic conductivity for granular base materials. [4]

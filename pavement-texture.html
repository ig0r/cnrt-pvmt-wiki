The surface characteristics of a pavement, typically categorized by wavelength range. Four regimes are distinguished in terms of their effect on vehicle performance (Ayton 1991):

Microtexture (wavelengths < 0.5 mm): Surface asperities at the aggregate scale. Provides adhesion necessary for skid resistance at low slip. Measured indirectly via friction testing. [1]

Macrotexture (wavelengths ~0.5–50 mm): Coarse surface features such as tining grooves and aggregate protrusions. Provides drainage pathways for water escape at the tire/road interface, necessary for wet-pavement friction. Also mitigates tire air-pumping noise by preventing air trapping. Measured via Circular Track Meter (Mean Profile Depth) or sand patch test. [1]

Megatexture (wavelengths ~50–500 mm): Larger features such as joints, cracks, and coarse pavement deterioration. Increases tire vibration beyond the contact patch (side wall vibrations). Generally undesirable — increases noise and roughness. [1]

Roughness (wavelengths > 500 mm): Long-wavelength deviations affecting ride quality and vehicle dynamic loading. Measured via the International Roughness Index (IRI). [1]

Overview of Concrete Surface Textures

The fundamental challenge in pavement surface design is that optimizing for one objective, such as macrotexture for drainage, may trade off against another, such as noise generation. To address this, the research framework in Karamihas and Cable (2004) emphasizes a systematic study of texturing parameters—including direction (longitudinal vs. transverse), spacing, groove width/depth, and aggregate characteristics—to find the optimal balance. A texture inventory includes 140 transverse tining, 104 longitudinal tining, 59 drag, 39 diamond ground, 12 transverse grooved, 10 shot peened, 4 longitudinal grooved, 5 exposed aggregate, 2 cold milled, and 20 HMA/other [2].

Texture Types and Geometric Properties

Concrete pavement textures are anisotropic, meaning their geometric properties vary depending on whether they are oriented transversely or longitudinally relative to traffic flow [3]. Various methods are used to create these surfaces, such as drag textures (artificial turf, burlap) which feature a 1.5–3 mm groove depth and provide quieter pavements, though they present skid resistance concerns at high speeds (>72 km/h). Transverse tining utilizes a 3 mm width/depth with either 12.5–25 mm uniform spacing (tonal whine) or 10–76 mm random spacing, and it is the most common method for high-speed highways. In contrast, Longitudinal tining features a 3 mm width/depth and a recommended 19 mm spacing; while it produces lower noise than transverse methods, it requires ≥25% siliceous sand for friction durability. To provide physical demonstrations of surface characteristics for future reference, rubber molds and positive lab casts are created from pavement textures [4].

Microtexture is primarily determined by fine aggregate in concrete mortar and serves as the main contributor to tire wear [5]. Additionally, Pervious concrete overlays exhibit a negative, open texture that facilitates noise reduction by allowing trapped air to escape during tire contact, resulting in OBSI measurements as low as 92–98 dBA; this acoustic performance is attributed to the uniform porosity and void structure inherent to the material’s permeable design [6].

Texture Variability and Measurement Accuracy

The RTP (Real-Time Profiler) cannot detect roughness below a wavelength of 0.5 feet, meaning it fails to quantify the impact of burlap drags and tining operations on megatexture and very short wavelength roughness. [7]

Profile measurements from plastic concrete using devices like the RTP can accurately isolate problems created by various paving train operations, including slip-form paver issues, mechanical floats, and human floating. [7]

RoboTex measurements proved that as-constructed width-depth-spacing differs from specified values. Quality control during construction needs electronic sensing beyond the sand patch test — depth/pressure sensors or line lasers on texturing equipment to provide operator feedback. [8]

Preliminary field data (1,012 test sections, 395 unique textures across 15 states + Quebec) shows wide variability overlap between texture types. Even some transverse tining sections are quieter than longitudinal tining. Subtle variations in texture geometry have appreciable influence on noise. Texture variability is highest for transverse tining, lowest for drag and ground textures. [8] [2]

RoboTex 3D Texture Measurement System

The RoboTex system developed for the ISU-FHWA-ACPA project uses an LMI Selcom RoLine line laser to create 3D texture maps with 0.5–1.0 mm lateral and 0.01 mm vertical resolution. This revealed that nominal (specified) texture differs significantly from actual as-constructed texture, even under controlled conditions — making nominal specifications of limited value. [8]

RoboTex with laser line technology [8]

Sensor Protection During Construction

When employed in the slip-form paving train, shrouds should be placed around profile sensors to prevent concrete and curing material splatter from damaging sensors or adversely impacting measurements [7]. Profile measuring equipment can be used at the slip-form paver and behind the cure/tining machine to determine if an additional operation is needed to remove surface irregularities created by aggregates and texturing that increase profile values [7]. The wiegand-2005-iowa-data-collection Iowa field experiment constructed 18 texture combinations on US Highway 30, providing practical insights into construction challenges. Pre-texture methods included a burlap drag, where a piece of burlap spanning the slab width was attached to a bridge behind the paver with ~0.75 m in contact with concrete and an application rate dependent on paver speed [4]; however, AstroTurf drags are prone to significant concrete buildup that flakes off onto the slab, creating debris that complicates subsequent tining operations [4]. Adding extra weight to drag textures using four shovels of limestone and two 20 ft #5 rebars can increase texture depth, though this approach is limited by the tensile strength of the dragging material [4].

Burlap drag [4]

Achieving target texture depth with a tining machine requires adjusting equipment elevation based on concrete moisture: wetter concrete requires more pressure, necessitating lower machine elevation and a larger tine tooth angle from vertical, whereas drier concrete requires less pressure, requiring the machine to be raised and tine teeth placed closer to vertical [4]. While excessive forward speed produces a wavy surface texture, it does not affect the actual groove depth [4]. Regarding tining patterns, consecutive passes for transverse tining must run parallel with minimal overlap; however, because current equipment designs lack adjustable broom angles, tine depth varies across the slab and is deeper at the crown [4]. Furthermore, longitudinal texturing on superelevated sections requires continuous horizontal adjustment of the broom angle to maintain uniform tine depth across the varying cross-slope [4]. Tining depths were measured at 50-meter increments using a tire tread gauge on a portable bridge, with eight markers per section (four per lane: outer edge, two wheel paths, quarter-point), and depths were recorded in thirty-secondths of an inch and converted to metric [4].

Tire gauge [4]

Factors Affecting Texture Depth and Quality

The relationship between mix and texture needs further examination [2]. Most tining values were lower than specified despite machine adjustments, a phenomenon attributed to hot/humid weather causing faster concrete set, aggregate near the surface, and concrete too dry for tine penetration [4]. Specifically, hot and humid weather conditions accelerate concrete setting times, often causing the surface layer to become too dry for tining teeth to penetrate to specified depths [4]. Furthermore, surface aggregate proximity is a primary factor limiting tining depth, as coarse particles near the slab surface prevent tining combs from sinking fully into the concrete matrix [4].

Texture wear occurs most significantly within wheel paths due to traffic, while winter maintenance activities and environmental factors also contribute to surface changes that affect friction and noise over time [2].

Curing Effectiveness and Surface Sorptivity

Surface texture significantly influences curing compound application rates, as rougher surfaces require higher volumes to achieve adequate coverage compared to smooth finishes [9]. Consequently, field application efficiency for curing compounds typically falls below 100% due to environmental factors, contrasting with laboratory conditions where compounds evenly fill pinholes on texture-free specimens [9].

Furthermore, the sorptivity of near-surface concrete is highly sensitive to curing effectiveness, serving as a more reliable indicator of microstructural changes and hydration levels than conventional compressive or flexural strength tests [9].

Effect of Curing Compound Application and Curing Condition on Sorptivity [9]

Tire-Pavement Noise Mechanisms and Performance

For dry pavement, tire noise generation involves three distinct mechanisms: radial vibrations from tread impact causing sidewall vibration, tangential vibrations from tread sticking and releasing within the contact patch, and air pumping shock waves resulting from rapid compression-expansion cycles of trapped air [1]. Tire tread design significantly influences the air pumping mechanism by allowing trapped air to escape during contact, whereas its impact on radial and tangential vibrations is limited due to functional stiffness constraints in tread and sidewall construction [1]. While macrotexture mitigates air pumping noise by preventing air trapping, megatexture exacerbates noise by inducing tire vibrations beyond the contact patch, such as sidewall vibrations [1]; furthermore, megatexture and overall roughness contribute to rolling resistance, which directly increases vehicle wear over time [5]. A deeper (more aggressive) texture causes more surface disturbances and latent concrete deposits that increase noise, meaning depth is only indirectly related to noise via these mechanisms [2]. Because texture spectral components and skew (bias) indicators correlate rationally with tire-pavement noise, suggesting that nominal texture geometry—width, depth, and spacing—does not reliably predict noise levels [2], ISO 13565-2 proposed for redefining texture as the correlation of texture spectral components and skew (bias) with noise is clearer than simple depth metrics [2].

OBSI measurements at 60 mph using a Goodyear Aquatred III tire demonstrate that concrete pavement textures generally produce noise levels ranging from approximately 100 dBA to 111 dBA, with differences attributed to texture configuration and surface condition [2]. Texture noise performance can be categorized into three zones based on OBSI measurements: Zone 1 (below ~99/100 dBA) contains no known conventional dense concrete textures, Zone 2 (~99–105 dBA) represents the best balance of cost-effective solutions including grinding and drag textures, and Zone 3 (above 104/105 dBA) includes highly variable textures and aggressive transverse tining [2]. Regarding measurement techniques, the close-proximity noise measurement method isolates tire-pavement contact noise by mounting a microphone near the vehicle’s contact patch, but it ignores sound propagation effects and does not correlate directly with roadside passby measurements [1]. Finally, the cross-over speed at which tire-pavement interaction becomes the dominant noise source over powertrain noise varies based on vehicle type, traffic conditions, and pavement texture, though tire noise is universally recognized as dominant at highway speeds [1].

The zone concept categorizes pavement noise performance into three distinct ranges based on OBSI measurements: Zone 1 represents the low or ‘Innovation’ zone below 99/100 dBA, while Zone 3 represents the high or ‘Avoid’ zone above 104/105 dBA [2].

Megatexture, defined as texture between 50 to 500 mm, is considered undesirable because it can be a major cause of tire-pavement noise and interior vehicle noise, often originating from construction irregularities like corrugations [8].

Relationship of pavement surface texture to various characteristic s [8]

Using the OBSI measurement system with a Goodyear Aquatred III tire at 60 mph, concrete pavement textures generally range from approximately 100 dBA to 111 dBA, with differences attributed primarily to texture configuration and surface condition [2].

OBSI catalog of all concrete pavement textures including typical ranges of HMA alternatives [2]

While texture spectral components and skew indicators correlate with tire-pavement noise in a rational manner, nominal texture geometry—width, depth, and spacing—does not reliably predict corresponding noise levels [2].

Minor variations in texture geometry can also have an appreciable influence on noise at the tire-pavement interface, as seen in some transverse tined sections measuring quieter than longitudinally tined surfaces that feature short spacings such as 0.5 inches [2].

OBSI and texture spectra for a shorter section of the random transverse tined section [2]

Friction Mechanics and Skid Resistance

Peak longitudinal friction force for both wet and dry pavements typically occurs at approximately 20 percent slip, with most tire-pavement combinations peaking between 5 and 30 percent slip [1]. At low slip levels, longitudinal force is transmitted primarily through adhesion provided by microtexture, whereas high slip speeds require macrotexture to dissipate energy as tire tread elements deform over coarse surface features [1]. Furthermore, when a tire operates near its lateral handling limit, its capacity to generate longitudinal braking force is significantly reduced, often approaching zero when cornering forces are maximized [1].

Longitudinal force versus slip. [1]

To assess these characteristics, the International Friction Index (IFI) is calculated by coupling Data Friction Tester (DFT) microtexture measurements with Circular Track Meter (CTM) macrotexture data to provide a comprehensive friction assessment [2]. While the ASTM E 274 locked wheel skid trailer assesses both macrotexture and microtexture effects when using a smooth tire, the DFT is primarily a measure of microtexture [2].

Locked wheel friction trailer [2]

Pavement Roughness and Dowel Bar Ripple

Vibration from dowel-bar inserters can cause RTP sensors to record short-wavelength surface irregularities that are subsequently removed by auto-floats and hand finishing, leading to real-time IRI measurements that are significantly higher than hardened profile values [10]. Additionally, stringless robotic total stations controlling paver elevation can introduce regular IRI fluctuations at intervals matching their spacing, with measurements on the side further from the stations showing greater roughness [10]. Through power spectral density analysis, specific joint spacings (e.g., 15 feet) and concrete load intervals (e.g., 13 feet) have been identified as dominant wavelengths contributing to pavement roughness, alongside their associated harmonic wavelengths [10].

Dowel bar ripple is a major contributor to pavement roughness, occurring when the paving machine compresses the dowel basket into the foundation; upon rebound, it creates bumps near each joint that are significantly more pronounced in outside wheel tracks than inside ones [7]. Consequently, roughness distribution across concrete slabs is inconsistent, as inside wheel tracks are significantly smoother than outside ones due to the larger amplitude of dowel bar ripple in those outside tracks [7]. Applying an auto-float after the initial float pan significantly reduces dowel bar ripple, with one study showing it reduced the International Roughness Index (IRI) by 11 inches/mile and Profile Index (PI) by 6.5 inches/mile over a measured section [7]. While manual floating improves surface smoothness if an auto-float is not used, its improvements are less consistent compared to those achieved by using an auto-float [7].

Simulated profilograph trace, passing lane outside trace [7]

Concrete Durability And Permeability

Permeability serves as an effective measure of concrete durability, with well-performing sections (PCI rating 1 or 2) demonstrating an average water-to-cement ratio of 0.49 compared to 0.55 for poorly performing sections [11].

Pavement Distress Mechanisms

longitudinal cracking in low- to mid-volume county roads often results from early-age issues related to concrete materials or mix design, as well as heavy agricultural loading and subgrade failure [11].

Picture showing a PCC pavement section in Pocahontas County, Iowa, depicting the commonly observed longitudinal cracking in relatively low-volume Iowa roads. [11]

Long-Term Durability and Distress Mechanisms

Regarding specific distress mechanisms, D-cracking is characterized by cracks initiating at the pavement surface and propagating downward, though core examinations reveal that this cracking develops upward from near the bottom of the slab [12].

Reducing the maximum coarse aggregate particle size has been shown to minimize or eliminate D-cracking [12].

Moisture And Saturation Characteristics

Moisture measurements indicate an increase in the degree of saturation after one year, with levels remaining somewhat higher near the bottom than the top [12], and saturation levels are generally higher near the base than the top [12].

Base Material Selection Effects

Sections with asphalt-treated free draining (ATFD) bases perform better than those with cement-treated or other base types, which may develop substantial numbers of cracks, highlighting the importance of base material selection in preventing early-age distress [12].

Interlayer Stress And Bonding

The “bimetallic” effect occurs when two layers with different properties create stress intensities at their bond, particularly due to chemical contraction in a stiffer top layer compared to the bottom layer [13].

This phenomenon requires specific design techniques for realistic stress registration and careful consideration of composition, thickness, and curing differences between the lifts [13].

Two-lift paving in Austria (Source: http://www.walter-heilit-vwb.de/english/toechterg/wien.htm) [13]

Regarding rigid pavement foundations with poor permeability and frost susceptibility similar to silts, these issues can be mitigated by increasing concrete thickness and utilizing reinforcing steel [12].

The installation of retrofit subbase underdrains is recommended to improve foundation drainage conditions in areas where the base contains gravity drainable water [12].

In Situ Strength Measurement Methods

Maturity technology provides a nondestructive means to indicate in situ compressive strength, which allows for earlier form removal and construction operations such as sawing pavement joints or opening pavements to traffic [12].

Average Three- and Seven-Day Compressive Strengths for Each State [12]

Data Collection and Measurement Methodologies

This method is considered more reliable and timely than testing conventional 6”x12” compressive strength cylinders [12].

The Iowa experiment utilized X-ray tomography alongside inertial profiling and circular texture meters to collect micro-, macro-, and megatexture data, enabling a detailed analysis of the relationship between noise generation and specific surface characteristics over time [4].

Advanced Texture Measurement Systems

To analyze surface characteristics, the RoboTex texture measurement device provides a resolution of less than 0.5 mm laterally and 0.01 mm vertically, enabling comprehensive analysis of the relationship between noise generation and specific surface characteristics [2].

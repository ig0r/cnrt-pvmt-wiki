Excess Paste Theory

Theory first proposed by Kennedy (1940) explaining the mechanism governing concrete workability. States that sufficient paste must exist in mortar or concrete to fill spaces between aggregate particles and coat particle surfaces, minimizing inter-particle friction.

Application to SFSCC

In Semi-Flowable Self-Consolidating Concrete, the rheological properties and thickness of the excess paste layer significantly contribute to both flowability and shape-holding ability. The degree of inter-particle friction greatly influences the required excess paste properties and thickness [1].

The difference between loose (unrodded) and compacted (rodded) bulk densities of coarse aggregates serves as an indicator for the energy required to consolidate concrete, where smaller differences correlate with higher compaction factors. [1]

Slump cone shape versus concrete compactability [1]

Naphthalene-based plasticizers generally provide a greater positive effect on concrete flowability under external compaction energy compared to polycarboxylate-based plasticizers at equivalent dosages. [1]

Actigel nano-clay is highly effective at reducing flowability and increasing green strength in conventional SCC, whereas metakaolinite increases flowability while maintaining green strength. [1]

Effect of different additives on green strength and flowability for the SCCF mixtures [1]

Nano-clay materials, including Actigel, metakaolinite, and kaolinite, are typically added at dosages ranging from 1% to 2% by weight of cement. [1]

For plain cement mixtures, at least 150 percent Vp/Vvoids is required to achieve any workability, whereas the critical value for all mixtures containing supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) is about 125 percent. [2]

Compressive strengths for different binder systems at 28 days as a function of Vp/Vvoid [2]

The critical minimum paste content varies by binder type: Portland Cement (PC) and Class C Ash require 500 pcy, while Slag and Fly Ash (F Ash) require 600 pcy. [2]

Compressive strengths for different binder systems at 28 days as a function of binder content [2]

Increasing paste content beyond the threshold required for consolidation leads to increased chloride penetrability and air permeability, even though compressive strength remains constant. [2]

Effect of cement content on concrete compressive strength [2]

Relative Excess Mortar Thickness

Calculated as the ratio of excess mortar volume to total aggregate surface area:

Γ = Pₑ / Σ nᵢ sᵢ Dₚᵢ

Where:

  • Γ = relative thickness of excess mortar
  • Pₑ = volume of mortar
  • nᵢ = number of aggregate particles of size i
  • sᵢ = surface area of each aggregate particle of size i
  • Dₚᵢ = diameter of aggregate size i

Design Targets

  • Spherical/rounded aggregates: Γ ≥ 0.25
  • Highly angular aggregates: Γ ≥ 0.45

Shape stability is controlled by the mortar phase, which must have sufficient viscosity and cohesion to drag coarse aggregate particles during flow while controlling segregation.

The relative excess mortar thickness (Γ) is calculated using the formula Γ = Pₑ / Σ nᵢ sᵢ Dₚᵢ, where Pₑ represents the volume of mortar and the denominator accounts for aggregate particle count, surface area, and diameter. [1]

For SFSCC mixtures utilizing highly angular coarse aggregates, a minimum relative excess mortar thickness of 0.45 is required to ensure adequate flowability and shape stability. [1]

Relationship between concrete slump and spread [1]

When using spherical or regular-shaped aggregates with smooth surfaces, the required minimum relative excess mortar thickness is reduced to 0.25. [1]

Relative excess mortar thickness for different trials [1]

Paste Rheology

Rheological properties such as viscosity, yield stress, and thixotropy are determined using a Brookfield rheometer with a loading history that increases shear rate from 0 to 100 1/s in 60 seconds and decreases it back to 0 in another 60 seconds. [1]

Loading history for paste with Brookfield rheometer [1]

Paste thixotropy is quantified by calculating the difference in area between the up curve (increasing shear rate) and the down curve (decreasing shear rate) of the shear stress-strain rate plot. [1]

Paste thixotropy with increasing Actigel content and hydration time [1]

Air-entraining agents (AEA) increase paste yield stress, whereas high-range water reducers (HRWR) do not significantly affect it. [1]

Paste yield stress with increasing Actigel content and hydration time [1]

Rheology of SFSCC

SFSCC rheological testing utilizes an IBB rheometer with an H-shaped impeller to record torque required for motion initiation and maintenance against a shear rate. [1]

Typical concrete flow curve for IBB rheometer [1]

Yield torque (G) represents the torque needed to initiate concrete motion, while slope (H) indicates the torque required to sustain movement at a given shear rate. [1]

Paste Thickness and ITZ

The Paste Thickness Index (PTI) ranges from 100 to 250 µm, which is significant because the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) typically measures only 40 to 50 µm thick. [2]

Binder-to-Aggregate Ratio

In pervious concrete, the binder-to-aggregate (b/a) ratio typically ranges from 0.22 to 0.25 and directly determines the thickness of the paste layer coating aggregate particles and the volume of paste filling void spaces. [3]

Binder Specific Gravity and Paste Volume

Replacing Portland cement with lower-specific-gravity materials like fly ash (2.28), slag (2.95), or limestone powder (2.7) at a 15% replacement rate increases the total paste volume, which can improve workability despite higher specific surface values adsorbing more mixing water. [3]

Workability test results of pervious concrete mixtures: Portland cement (top left), Portland cement - 15% Fly ash (top right), Portland cement - 15% Slag (bottom left), and Portland cement - 15% Limestone (bottom right) [3]

Water-to-Binder Ratio Constraints

A water-to-binder ratio between 0.27 and 0.30 is preferred for pervious concrete; ratios below 0.27 result in very low workability, while higher ratios cause excessive paste segregation at the bottom of the formwork. [3]

Fine Aggregate in Excess Paste Systems

The addition of fine sand at approximately 7% by weight of total aggregate to single-sized coarse aggregate mixes improves compressive strength by 47% while maintaining adequate water permeability. [3]

Paste Void Structure

Pervious concrete paste structure contains two distinct void groups: small-sized air voids (0.01 mm to 0.03 mm) within the cement paste/mortar that control pollution removal, and large-sized voids (0.5 mm to 3.0 mm) among aggregate particles that contribute significantly to water permeability. [3]

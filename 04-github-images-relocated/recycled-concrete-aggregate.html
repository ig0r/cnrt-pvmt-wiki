Recycled Concrete Aggregate

Part of: Aggregates
aggregateconceptconcreterecyclingsustainability
Also known as: RCA, recycled concrete aggregates
Created 2026-05-20 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA) is aggregate produced from crushing existing concrete structures for use in new construction applications. It differs from crushed concrete aggregate (CCA), which is processed from concrete that was never in service — CCA is a type of RCA. [1]

Physical and Mechanical Properties

RCA consists of one or more virgin aggregate types bound by hardened mortar, a mortar fraction that results in lower relative density and higher porosity than most virgin aggregates. Key properties of RCA include higher absorption and water demand, as well as strength that is typically lower than hard virgin aggregate but may exceed soft virgin aggregates; furthermore, quality can be improved by minimizing residual mortar through optimized crushing methods. The suitability of concrete made with RCA for new mixes is determined by specific properties including strength, permeability, workability, and durability [2].

The mechanical properties of RCA-based foundation layers can exhibit significant spatial variability, with in situ modulus values showing coefficients of variation around 50% and ranging widely from approximately 35.5 MPa (5.1 ksi) to 709.5 MPa (102.9 ksi) [3].

σB versus M_r for subbase and subgrade composite sample [3]

Permeability and Durability Characteristics

When used in granular base courses, recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) exhibits lower permeability and reduced resistance to particle degradation compared to virgin limestone or gravel [4], resulting in greater susceptibility to freeze-thaw damage and mass loss. Despite these factors, RCA subbase layers typically demonstrate low permeability and variable modulus of elasticity, yet they maintain pavement surface conditions comparable to virgin aggregate sections over time [4].

Global Applications in Pavement Construction

Research by the Federal Highway Research Institute of Germany indicates that using different properties in two layers can create stress intensities from a “bimetallic” effect at the bond between layers [5]. In Washington state test sections, concrete with a flexural strength of 900 psi exhibited minimal wear from studded tires compared to lower-strength sections [5]. Additionally, RCA can be utilized in shorter performance life pavements, such as those designed for an 8-year service period, by investigating the specific use of its fine or coarse fractions [2].

Two-lift paving in Austria (Source: http://www.walter-heilit-vwb.de/english/toechterg/wien.htm) [5]

Regional Usage Standards and Examples

In the U.S., RCA is most commonly used in base and subbase layers, with 41 states recycling concrete for such uses. However, only 11 states report using RCA in new portland cement concrete (PCC). International usage includes:

  • Austria: Up to 20% RCA in lower lift of two-lift construction
  • Australia: Up to 30% coarse aggregate replacement in curbs/sidewalks (Class 1A/1B)
  • Japan: Up to 20% coarse aggregate replacement; JIS classification (H/M/L)
  • UK: Up to 30% coarse aggregate replacement with minimal property effects

Alternative Uses: Base, Backfill, and Stabilization

Recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) can exhibit cementitious behavior, which allows it to perform better than virgin aggregate in base and subbase applications [1]. Consequently, RCA can be utilized in various applications such as cement-treated base (CTB), backfill, embankment stabilization, erosion control (riprap), and landscaping [1]. In Austria, for example, recycled aggregate from shattered jointed reinforced concrete pavements can be secured with cement and incorporated into an 8.5-inch deep bottom layer of a two-lift pavement system [5].

Furthermore, in cement-treated permeable base applications, RCA can serve as an open-graded drainage course aggregate when blended with Portland cement and water to create a stable, drainable mixture that hardens in place [3].

Particle-size distribution curves of subgrade, subbase, and OGDC base materials [3]

Barriers to Implementation

Three categories of barriers limit RCA use in new concrete paving. However, properly engineered mixtures containing RCA can achieve durability in freeze-thaw environments even when specimens fail sulfate soundness tests [1]. Furthermore, durability testing of RCA cement-treated base mixtures demonstrates that specimens can withstand multiple wet-dry and freeze-thaw cycles with percent mass loss remaining below the maximum allowable limits specified by standards such as PCA (1971) and Department of Army, Navy, and Air Force (1994) [3].

Compacted CTB sample from split mold for wet-dry and freeze-thaw testing [3]

Compliance and Quality Challenges

  • Compliance: Meeting state specifications for soundness (sulfate test), abrasion, gradation, strength; ASR concerns
  • Quality: Maintaining consistency; perception of RCA as waste material

Production and Supply Logistics

  • Production: Storage, workability, supply, debris removal, equipment location

Quality Control and International Classification Systems

International quality classification systems include the Japanese JIS (H/M/L), where Type H is required for concrete and limits are based on relative density and absorption, as well as RILEM, which utilizes three categories based on absorption, density, and sulfate content that are applicable to new concrete pavement mixtures with additional testing. To ensure consistency, RCA processing guidelines should include methods for separating material based on origin, strength, and air content [1].

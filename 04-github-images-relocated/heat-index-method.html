Heat Index Method

Part of: Calorimetry
calorimetryconceptheat-of-hydrationquality-control
Also known as: calorimeter indexes, heat evolution indexes, heat indexes
Created 2026-05-20 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

A method for interpreting calorimetry test results developed in Phase II of FHWA Project 17 (wang-2007-simple-rapid-test-heat-evolution-phase2). Six parameters characterize the heat evolution curve to predict concrete performance and enable quality control.

Area-Based Parameters (A1–A4)

Heat generation is measured using four area-based parameters that track heat produced in sequential 6-hour windows: A1 (heat generated from 1 to 6 hours), A2 (heat generated from 6 to 12 hours), A3 (heat generated from 12 to 18 hours), and A4 (heat generated from 18 to 24 hours). Increasing fly ash replacement levels causes a decrease in areas A1 and A2, while area A3 increases with higher fly ash content; notably, at 40% replacement, the values for A2 and A3 become similar [1]. This distinct shift allows the method to flag changes in fly ash dosage, as deviations from expected A3 and A4 similarity can indicate incorrect concrete proportions [1].

Derivative-Based Set Time Parameters

Set times are determined by two derivative-based parameters. The initial set occurs at the time when the first derivative of the heat evolution curve d(q)/dt reaches its maximum value, which corresponds to the fastest increase in the heat generation rate. The final set is defined as the time when the first derivative returns to zero, corresponding to the peak of the original heat evolution curve. For mixes containing fly ash where the first derivative re-ascends before reaching zero, the descending limb is extended to cross the x-axis [1].

Determining initial set time from the derivatives method [1]

Data Processing and Equilibrium Requirements

Data prior to 1 hour is excluded to allow the calorimeter system to reach equilibrium after sample loading. [1]

Repeatability of calorimeter tests performed by a given operator at different times [1]

Applications in Concrete Testing

The heat indexes support eight potential applications of calorimetry, including verifying mix proportions and prescreening mix designs and materials [1]. This method can be applied using semi-adiabatic calorimeters, such as the AdiaCal device, which records concrete temperature every 30 seconds and allows for the prediction of field concrete set times with high accuracy [2].

AdiaCal calorimeter [2]

Predicting Strength and Setting Times

Calorimetry data can be utilized for forecasting the time of set, achieving an initial R²=0.95 and a final R²=0.96 vs. ASTM C 403 [1], as well as for predicting early-age strength (up to 2 days) and estimating sawing and finishing time [1]. A linear relationship exists between the total generated heat and concrete strength up to two days of age, enabling the prediction of early-age strength directly from calorimetry data when combined with software capable of calculating field heat generation conditions [1].

Comparison of the set times from ASTM C 403 and calorimetry tests [1]

Material Characterization and Incompatibility Detection

The heat index method can identify material incompatibility by detecting hydration disturbances, such as those caused by cement-admixture interactions or retardation effects, which allows for the quantification of retardation and detection of compatibility issues through changes in the heat evolution curve’s shape [3]. This capability includes flagging cementitious changes regarding cement type, source, and fly ash content/type. The method’s sensitivity to admixtures allows it to distinguish effects such as superplasticizers mildly retarding setting time and reducing early-age heat generation without altering total heat evolution, whereas accelerators can increase the rate of heat liberation during both the setting and hardening stages depending on their chemical composition [3]. Specifically, Type III cement exhibits the highest values for area A1 and area A3 alongside the lowest set times among tested cements, indicating a faster early-age hydration rate compared to Type I and ISM cements; in contrast, Type ISM cement demonstrates lower A1 but higher A2 values relative to Type I cement, signifying slower initial hydration followed by accelerated rates between 6 and 12 hours [1].

Effect of cement type on heat of hydration [1]

Furthermore, the method can detect specific hydration disturbances caused by cement-admixture interactions, such as those resulting from adding water reducers to mixes with Class C fly ash [1]. When material incompatibility occurs due to the addition of water reducers to mixes containing Class C fly ash, initial set time decreases significantly (e.g., from 6.2 to 2.6 hours) and final set time drops (e.g., from 8.4 to 3.0 hours) [1]. Concurrently, area A1 increases sharply while area A2 drops precipitously, reflecting an early sharp exothermic reaction followed by a period of minimal hydration between 6 and 12 hours [1].

Effect of fly ash type and temperature on set times [1]

Integration with HIPERPAV and Field Validation

When integrated with the HIPERPAV computer program, the heat index method provides input data for predicting pavement strength gain and evaluating the risk of thermal cracking based on specific climate and project conditions [2]. FHWA Project 17 Phase III (wang-2008-simple-rapid-test-heat-evolution-phase3) validated all six heat indexes using field concrete at three construction sites [2]. The A1–A4 area parameters and derivative-based set times successfully distinguished mix variations in field concrete (not just lab mortar), while matching curing temperature between calorimeter and field samples significantly improved the correlation between laboratory heat indexes and field performance [2].

Predicted pavement temperatures versus measurements (pavement top, Ottumwa, IA) [2]

Methodological Limitations

Because heat indexes cannot always distinguish between similar materials—for example, fly ashes from sources B and L produced very similar heat curves—the method requires software/experienced staff for result interpretation [1]. Furthermore, isothermal calorimeters used in this method are limited to approximately seven days of testing due to signal sensitivity constraints, beyond which the heat flow signal becomes indistinguishable from background noise; this duration limit restricts the ability to capture long-term hydration kinetics without extrapolation [3].

Statistical analysis indicates that fly ash replacement levels have a more significant impact on thermal set times and setting windows than water reducer dosages, with increased fly ash reducing both the peak temperature and the duration between initial and final set [2].

Statistical analysis from the US 63 bypass (Ottumwa, IA) AdiaCal robust test [2]

Computational Conversion Models

The method utilizes a mathematical conversion model that employs a finite difference method and Visual Basic Application to transform isothermal cement mortar data into semi-adiabatic concrete heat evolution profiles. [2]

Isothermal calorimetry results for US 71 (Atlantic, IA) field concrete samples [2]

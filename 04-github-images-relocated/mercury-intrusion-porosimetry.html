Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry

Part of: Aggregates
aggregateconceptlaboratorypore-structuretesting
Also known as: MIP, Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry Test
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

A laboratory technique for characterizing the pore structure of materials by forcing mercury into pores under increasing pressure. Used as a reference method for validating the Iowa Pore Index test and providing detailed information about pore size distribution, total porosity, and pore connectivity in concrete aggregates.

Test Principle and Washburn Equation

Mercury is a non-wetting liquid that does not spontaneously penetrate pores, though applied pressure forces mercury into pores, with smaller pores requiring higher pressure. The Washburn equation relates this applied pressure to pore diameter via the formula P = -4γcosθ/d, where γ is mercury surface tension, θ is contact angle, and d is pore diameter. This technique involves injecting mercury into an evacuated sample at increasing pressures to measure the volume fraction of increasingly smaller pore throats [1].

Measured Parameters and Porosity Metrics

Mercury intrusion porosimetry provides a detailed pore-throat size distribution curve that quantifies effective porosity by measuring mean, median, and mode values for each rock type [2]. Within this method, total porosity is defined as the volume of intruded mercury per unit mass of sample, while the pore size distribution represents the differential intrusion volume vs. pore diameter [2]. Furthermore, the median pore diameter is the pore diameter at 50% cumulative intrusion, and the pore volume distribution describes the proportion of pores in different size ranges [2].

Pore-throat size distributions were measured on a Quantachrome PoreMaster 33, intruding pores from 100 µm down to 5.6 nm for 40 rock types across 21 carbonate aggregate sources [2].

Aggregate Characterization: Carbonate vs. Non-Carbonate

MIP effectively characterized pore structure differences between carbonate and non-carbonate aggregates [3]. Specifically, carbonate aggregates exhibited finer pore structures with a median diameter of 0.01-0.5 μm [3], whereas non-carbonate aggregates had coarser pore structures with a median diameter of 0.5-4.5 μm [3]. Across aggregate sources, total porosity ranged from 0.6% to 18.7% [3].

Pore Size Distribution of Aggregates Studied (C: Carbonate, NC: Non-Carbonate) [3]

Carbonate aggregates exhibit a distinctively higher porosity than non-carbonate aggregates, with a significantly larger volume of finer pores in the 0.01–10 µm range [3]. Conversely, non-carbonate aggregates are dominated by pores larger than 100 µm, with volumes of other pore sizes being much less significant [3]. MIP data confirms that carbonate aggregates have a finer pore structure than non-carbonate ones, as evidenced by higher pore surface area for the same mercury intrusion volume [3]. Within carbonate aggregates, limestones typically exhibit modal pore-throat diameters less than about 0.4 µm, whereas dolostones display modal pore-throat diameters larger than 0.4 µm [1].

Large pore-throat diameters yielding low secondary Iowa IPI values [1]

Correlation with Absorption and Field Tests

  • MIP results correlated well with Iowa Pore Index test parameters

MIP-derived total pore volume shows a strong correlation with both absorption and the combined Iowa Pore Index parameters (PPI + SPI), indicating that MIP can validate these faster screening tests. [3]

MIP-derived total pore volume correlates well with ASTM C 127 absorption results, validating MIP as a reference for bulk porosity measurements. [3]

Critical Pore-Throat Ranges for Durability

For carbonate aggregates, the most significant pore volume consists of pores in the 0.1–1 µm range, which often appear as a hump in the distribution curve that substantially affects freeze-thaw durability [3]. While the general consensus for the critical range of pore-throat sizes that cause deterioration in aggregates spans from 0.005 to 0.2 µm [2], analysis of mercury data suggests that the critical pore-throat diameter range for D-cracking susceptibility lies between 0.02 and 0.1 µm, rather than relying solely on modal pore-throat diameters [1]. This critical pore-throat range for freeze/thaw susceptibility was specifically refined from 0.04–0.2 µm to 0.02–0.1 µm [2].

Comparison of Pore Size Distributions (C: Carbonate, NC: Non-Carbonate) [3]

Of 40 rock types analyzed, 13 had modal pore-throat diameters within this critical range [2], and high mud content identified from thin-section petrography correlated with capillary pores in the critical range [2]. However, because 5 of 11 sources with rock types in the critical range were classified as durable, it is suggested that the critical range may need re-evaluation for D-cracking prediction [2].

Applications in Concrete Technology and Material Science

Since its adoption in the late 1960s, mercury porosimetry has been extensively applied to study the pore networks of cement paste, ceramics, mortar, and aggregates [1], serving purposes such as characterizing aggregate pore structure for durability assessment and quality control for aggregate sources in concrete construction. Furthermore, mercury intrusion porosimetry has been used to verify that the addition of PAN microfibers modifies the pore structure of fiber-reinforced concrete, which contributes to significant reductions in autogenous shrinkage-induced strains [4].

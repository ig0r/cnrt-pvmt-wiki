Ground Granulated Blast-Furnace Slag

Part of: Supplementary Cementitious Materials is-member-of
Ground Granulated Blast-Furnace Slag is a primary member of Supplementary Cementitious Materials.
concreteggbfsmaterialsscmslag
Also known as: GGBFS, blast-furnace slag, slag, slag cement
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T06:19:07Z

Ground granulated blast-furnace slag is a glassy, amorphous by-product of iron production that is created by rapidly quenching molten slag and grinding it into a fine powder. It functions as a supplementary cementitious material with latent hydraulic properties, meaning it reacts slowly on its own but becomes highly active in the presence of alkaline environments such as those provided by Portland cement hydration [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [14]. The material participates in pozzolanic reactions with calcium hydroxide to form additional calcium-silicate-hydrate gel, which drives later-age strength development and reduces the overall heat of hydration [6] [7] [8] [14]. Its reactivity is governed by fineness and chemical composition, particularly the ratio of calcium and magnesium oxides to silica, which determines its potential for strength contribution [4] [7] [14]. In concrete applications, it enhances long-term durability by lowering permeability and improving resistance to chemical attacks such as sulfate attack and alkali-silica reaction [6] [8] [11] [13] [14].

Key findings

  • Enhanced long-term compressive strength in many ternary mixes, achieving greater strengths than plain Portland cement by 28 days despite slower early strength gain [2] [7] [14].
  • Increased setting time in many cases, though the influence was not uniform and could be reduced or accelerated depending on the specific slag source and combination with other materials like fly ash [4] [5] [14].
  • Decreased heat evolution and thermal cracking risk compared to ordinary Portland cement, with maximum hydration heat decreasing as the amount of slag replacement increased [4] [5].
  • Increased water requirement for a given consistency compared to ordinary Portland cement replacements [4].
  • Raised the mix’s viscosity and yield stress, affecting flowability and shape-holding ability in self-consolidating concrete applications [16].
  • Reduced carbon dioxide emissions by roughly half when incorporated into ternary blends with fly ash and limestone-blended cement [14].

Material Characterization: Composition and General Properties

Ground granulated blast-furnace slag is a finely ground, largely amorphous by-product of iron production that serves as a supplementary cementitious material in concrete [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [14] [16]. Its reactivity is governed by its glassy structure and chemical composition, particularly the ratio of calcium, magnesium, and alumina oxides to silica oxide [4] [7] [14]. As a latent hydraulic material, it reacts slowly with water on its own but accelerates significantly in the presence of alkaline activators to form additional calcium-silicate-hydrate gel [5] [6] [7] [14]. The degree of reactivity and resulting performance are strongly influenced by particle fineness and the availability of calcium hydroxide from Portland cement hydration [5] [6] [7] [14].

Research problem — Engineers hesitate to use slag-blended cement because existing specifications do not address how GGBFS alters concrete behavior, particularly regarding hydration kinetics and early-age strength development [4]. The material exhibits significant chemical and physical variability between batches and sources, making it difficult to predict its influence on concrete properties such as setting time and durability [7]. There is a lack of reliable quantitative data on how GGBFS behaves in ternary cementitious blends, specifically regarding durability-critical properties like sulfate resistance and alkali-silica reaction expansion [14]. Portable X-ray fluorescence analyzers struggle to accurately quantify slag content within concrete mixes due to elemental composition overlaps with other cementitious binders, complicating quality control [19].

State of the art — GGBFS is standardized by ASTM C989 into activity grades based on mortar compressive strength, with quality control relying on bulk oxide chemistry via X-ray fluorescence and mineralogical assessment using X-ray diffraction [1] [3] [7] [14]. The material’s reactivity is governed by its chemical composition, particularly high calcium oxide and low alumina content, as well as its fineness, which determines early-age strength development and heat evolution rates [7]. Modern practice typically replaces 20% to 40% of Portland cement with GGBFS in binary or ternary blends to enhance long-term durability, reduce permeability, and mitigate alkali-silica reaction [1] [3] [6] [8] [14] [18].

Findings from the reports

  • The influence of slag on setting time varied by source and blend composition; some slags did not delay set times or even reduced delays caused by fly ash, while others consistently increased both initial and final set times as their proportion rose [4].
  • Slag replacement lowered the peak heat of hydration and extended the dormant period less than ordinary Portland cement, thereby reducing the risk of thermal cracking in concrete [4].
  • Field sampling confirmed that slag content in blended cements was reasonably uniform over time, though dosages varied significantly across different sites and applications [3].
  • Finer slag grades exhibited higher activity indices and generated greater heat of hydration than coarser grades, while also reducing mortar flow and shortening set times in some contexts [7].
  • Slag contributed positively to durability indicators by mitigating alkali-silica reaction expansion and maintaining low sulfate-induced expansion levels in most ternary blends [14].
  • Slag-containing mixes showed improved freeze-thaw durability and reduced surface scaling when air entrainment was properly controlled, while also achieving high compressive strengths at 28 days [2].
  • Portable X-ray fluorescence analysis revealed that the ground granulated blast-furnace slag contained high levels of calcium oxide, silica dioxide, alumina, and magnesium oxide, with an elevated sulfate content exceeding typical cement ranges [19].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a systematic experimental framework that moves beyond conventional assumption-based practices by directly measuring kinetic parameters, such as datum temperature and activation energy, for slag-rich ternary blends rather than relying on generic values [4]. This approach uniquely integrates measured thermal properties into maturity models to eliminate timing errors in pavement opening predictions and reveals manufacturer-specific set-time behaviors that existing specifications fail to capture [4]. The methodology is further distinguished by its comprehensive evaluation of slag fineness within an extensive matrix of ternary blends, establishing a predictive architecture that links paste- and mortar-phase test results to concrete durability outcomes across broad compositional spaces [14].

Across the reviewed reports, ground granulated blast-furnace slag is consistently characterized as a glassy material with high calcium oxide and silica dioxide content, where its reactivity and thermal behavior are primarily governed by particle fineness rather than chemical composition alone. While the addition of slag generally increases water demand and can delay setting times depending on the specific cement source or grind size, it consistently reduces peak heat evolution compared to pure Portland cement, although finer grades exhibit higher early-age heat release. In terms of hardened properties, slag-blended mixes demonstrate superior durability through significant mitigation of alkali-silica reaction and low shrinkage, while achieving compressive strengths that often surpass those of control mixes at later ages despite potential early-age strength deficits. [3] [4] [7] [14] [19]

Implications — Designers must adjust water demand and curing regimes based on the specific GGBFS source to manage workability, as different suppliers exhibit distinct effects on setting time and early-age strength development [4]. Specifiers should select slag fineness grades to balance heat mitigation against early strength requirements, recognizing that finer particles accelerate reactions while coarser variants reduce peak hydration temperatures [5] [7]. To ensure dimensional stability and durability, high replacement levels of GGBFS should be combined with fly ash to mitigate drying shrinkage and alkali-silica reaction risks in ternary blends [3] [6] [14].

Limitations — The accuracy and sensitivity of simple calorimetry tests are seldom documented, and there is no consensus on how to interpret heat-evolution curves for cementitious materials [5]. Regression models for material characterization are only valid within the specific ranges of fineness and content examined, making extrapolation beyond those parameters potentially inaccurate [7]. Laboratory-generated data may not reflect actual field performance under varying climatic environments [7].

Durability and Surface Performance

Research problem — The inherent compositional variability and supply inconsistencies of ground-granulated blast-furnace slag make it difficult to predict concrete workability, setting time, and strength development, thereby jeopardizing constructability and long-term durability [1] [3] [15]. High slag dosages are associated with increased permeability, excessive shrinkage cracking, and surface scaling under freeze-thaw conditions, which compromise pavement service life despite the material’s sustainability benefits [6] [8] [11] [13]. Standard laboratory testing methods often yield overly pessimistic scaling results for slag-containing concrete, creating a disconnect between test predictions and field performance that restricts the adoption of higher replacement levels [12] [13]. The use of ground-granulated blast-furnace slag as coarse aggregate introduces high absorptivity and moisture loss issues that hinder cement hydration and promote secondary ettringite formation, leading to microcracking and reduced durability [6] [8].

State of the art — GGBFS is widely used as a supplementary cementitious material in highway and bridge concrete, typically replacing 20–50% of Portland cement to enhance long-term strength and reduce permeability [11] [13]. While standard ASTM C672 scaling tests often penalize slag blends, field experience indicates that proper air-entrainment, low water-to-cement ratios, and construction quality are more critical factors in preventing deicer-induced surface scaling [11] [12] [13].

Findings from the reports

  • Construction variables such as water-to-cement ratio and curing practices dominated durability outcomes more than slag content, with lower water-to-cement ratios dramatically improving deicer scaling resistance [11] [13].
  • GGBFS enabled substantial reductions in Portland cement content, allowing for approximately one-quarter reduction in total binder while maintaining fresh-state and durability requirements in self-consolidating concrete [16].
  • Concrete mixes containing GGBFS achieved compressive strengths comparable to plain Portland cement, particularly when the water-to-cement ratio was reduced [13].
  • GGBFS increased paste viscosity and yield stress in self-consolidating concrete mixes, affecting flowability and shape-holding ability [16].
  • The coarse slag aggregate itself posed durability risks if not properly handled, as high absorption could deprive cement of water and reduce strength unless stockpiles were wetted before use [6].
  • Higher slag content correlated with increased carbonation depth despite reduced chloride penetration, creating a trade-off that can become detrimental if carbonation exceeds specific thresholds [13].
  • GGBFS mitigated alkali-silica reaction in concrete pavements when incorporated as a supplementary cementitious material alongside other additives like fly ash or silica fume [6].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel laboratory scaling protocol that modifies standard testing procedures by employing milder saline solutions and insulated curing regimes to better replicate the field performance of slag-containing concrete [12] [13]. Methodological rigor is established through inter-laboratory repeatability assessments and systematic comparisons against existing standards, ensuring that the proposed durability metrics accurately reflect real-world deicer-induced distress [12] [13]. Distinctive analytical approaches include the application of performance-based specifications to field-extracted cores and the use of quantitative heat-evolution indices linked directly to pavement-performance models for comprehensive durability monitoring [5] [11].

While laboratory tests often show that higher slag contents can increase scaling susceptibility or require specific curing conditions to perform well, field investigations indicate that construction deficiencies and moisture management are the primary drivers of surface deterioration rather than the proportion of slag in the mix. [6] [8] [12] [13] [16]

Implications — Specifications should permit GGBFS replacement levels up to fifty percent, provided that mix designs enforce low water-to-cement ratios and rigorous air-entrainment controls to maintain scaling resistance [13]. Designers must prioritize strict moisture control and curing management over material composition alone, as construction deficiencies are the primary drivers of surface deterioration rather than the slag content itself [8] [11] [12]. Agencies should adopt more representative laboratory testing methods for salt scaling to avoid unduly restricting high-slag mixes that perform adequately in field conditions [12] [13].

Limitations — Predictions for high-slag blends are unreliable because the underlying strength models rely on visual regression and lack experimental data beyond approximately seventy-five percent replacement [3]. Current laboratory scaling tests may be overly aggressive compared to field conditions, leading to misleading results that necessitate cautious interpretation of durability benefits [12] [13]. Attributing scaling resistance directly to slag content is difficult because construction variables and core extraction damage often exert a stronger influence on performance than the material composition itself [11].

Mechanical Performance and Structural Integrity

Research problem — Excessive heat from cement hydration creates temperature gradients that induce slab curling, warping, and cracking, thereby compromising pavement smoothness and service life [10]. The specific influence of ground granulated blast-furnace slag on mitigating these thermal stresses remains unclear due to its complex interaction with other mix variables [10]. Slag-induced reductions in drying shrinkage cause significant delays in early-entry saw cut cracking, which leaves joints weak and increases the risk of uncontrolled random cracking during service [9].

State of the art — Current mechanistic-empirical design guides for sustainable pavements specify ground granulated blast-furnace slag to help control early-age temperature gradients, thereby mitigating curling and warping [10]. The research community has not reached consensus on the specific effect of slag on early-age curling and warping, with contemporary practice treating the impact as uncertain and dependent on other mix variables such as paste content [10].

Findings from the reports

  • Evaluated the performance of concrete containing ground-granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) and observed that it produced relatively modest curling and warping, resulting in the lowest downward deflection among the sites studied [10].
  • Recommended the use of supplementary cementitious materials like GGBFS to help control heat evolution and shrinkage, which can reduce curling and warping in pavement mix design [10].
  • Reported that the influence of GGBFS on concrete curling and warping remains controversial and depends on other factors such as paste content rather than inherent properties alone [10].
  • Earlier studies linked the presence of slag in ternary mixes to slower joint cracking compared to conventional concrete [9].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a distinctive approach by combining high-volume ground granulated blast-furnace slag replacement with comprehensive field instrumentation to monitor structural behavior under real-world conditions [8]. This methodology is uniquely characterized by the integration of specialized epoxy-coated steel dowel bars and extensive sensor networks that capture detailed data on temperature gradients, slab shape, and joint movement [8].

Implications — Designers should incorporate ground granulated blast-furnace slag into pavement mixes to mitigate hydration heat and reduce shrinkage-induced curling, but must recognize that this is not a guaranteed solution [10]. Specification of slag as a supplementary cementitious material requires case-specific evaluation rather than universal application, given its efficacy depends on other mix factors such as paste content [10].

Limitations — The applicability of the findings is restricted to specific slag sources and blending methods, limiting generalization to other grades or suppliers [4]. Predictive models may fail to account for cracking risks under extreme seasonal temperatures that fall outside typical average conditions [4]. The influence of slag on concrete curling and warping remains unresolved due to its dependence on other mix variables, preventing definitive recommendations [10].

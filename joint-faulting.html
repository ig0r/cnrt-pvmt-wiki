Joint Faulting

Part of: Concrete Pavement Joint
distressjointpavementperformance
Also known as: faulting
Created 2026-05-20 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

A vertical discontinuity (difference in elevation) at transverse joints in concrete pavement, caused by differential settlement of adjacent slabs when load transfer across the joint is inadequate. Typically more severe at the outer edges of the driving lane. [1]

The only identified child is the Faultmeter, a device used to quantify vertical displacement at pavement joints. Different models vary in precision, with FHWA meters offering ±0.1 mm accuracy compared to Gilson meters’ ±1.0 mm accuracy [2].

Causes of Joint Faulting and Cracking

Joint distress and faulting can be driven by the erosion of subgrade/subbase material from beneath joints, inadequate Load Transfer Efficiency across joints, and the pumping of fines through joints under repeated traffic loading. Shear forces induced by truck speeds increase significantly when vehicle speeds drop below 30 mph, whereas speeds between 45 and 65 mph produce similar force magnitudes [1]. While the presence of a joint undercut initially reduces the magnitude of forces in the dowel bar, this protective effect diminishes after a short period of time [1]. Furthermore, coarse aggregate composition significantly influences blowup occurrence, specifically with gravel-lime-stone mixes in roughly 50/50 proportions requiring careful attention during acceptance testing to predict future joint deterioration [3]. Non-uniform subgrade support increases localized deflections and causes stress concentrations in the pavement structure, which can lead to premature failures including faulting; however, modeling indicates that using a uniform subgrade reduces maximum principal stresses and deflections, increasing fatigue life by approximately 1.7 times compared to non-uniform conditions [4]. To mitigate these issues, the mean wander of traffic increases from 18 inches to 42 inches away from the slab edge in the longitudinal direction when using widened slabs, which helps reduce potentially dangerous edge drop-offs associated with faulting [5].

Stresses at the bottom surface of the widened slab for the “wheel path” load case [5]

Darter’s research on zero-maintenance pavements indicates that preventing joint faulting requires short joint spacing, adequate subdrainage with outflow rates matching infiltration, and shoulder thickness equal to the mainline pavement [6]. This study also emphasizes that transverse joints must be identical to traffic lanes while longitudinal joints require sealing to maintain structural adequacy over extended service periods [6]. Pavement performance databases include sections that have remained in service for 70 to 80 years, demonstrating that jointed concrete pavements can achieve structural adequacy well beyond the standard 20-year design life [6]. These long-term sections provide a basis for identifying attributes that prevent distress accumulation over decades of traffic loading [6].

Mitigation Strategies and Design Improvements

Widened concrete slabs (e.g., 14 ft) reduce joint faulting by moving truck axles further from free edges to create interior-loading conditions rather than edge-loading, which significantly decreases critical stresses and deflections at slab edges [5]. To mitigate longitudinal cracking that often initiates from slab transverse joints, using a rectangular joint design option instead of a skewed jointed design is recommended for widened JPCP [5].

Effects of lateral edge support on joint faulting in JPCP [5]

Effective pavement performance also relies on proper dowel bar design (diameter, spacing, material). For instance, Pennsylvania DOT changed from skewed to perpendicular joints based on LTPP data, reducing faulting [1]. Furthermore, faulting prediction models incorporate traffic, climate, subgrade, dowel diameter, drainage, joint spacing, and base type [1].

Dowel Bar Performance: Steel vs. FRP

The interim report on elliptical steel dowel performance (Cable et al. 2006) on Iowa Highway 330 found that elliptically shaped steel dowels showed higher levels of faulting performance than circular bars. Dowel spacing changes showed no consistent performance trends. Cut and fill sections faulted equally, while transition sections were subject to increased faulting levels [7].

In the FHWA Project 5 field evaluation on Iowa Highway 330, elliptical FRP dowels at 10-, 12-, and 15-inch spacings showed no significant faulting trends over the monitoring period (2003–2005). Measurements ranged from 0.1 to 0.8 mm (0.0039 to 0.031 in), shifting from negative to positive faulting over time. No statistical conclusions could be drawn due to small magnitudes and short project duration. Steel dowels in an adjacent section were also monitored (Appendix F). [8]

The use of a larger diameter fiberglass dowel (1.5 in.) can achieve load transfer performance comparable to that of a smaller 1.0 in. steel dowel. [1]

Glasform GFRP dowels consistently performed above a 90 percent joint effectiveness acceptance level, outperforming epoxy-coated steel dowels which followed as the second-best material. [1]

The larger diameter of GFRP dowels (38 mm or 1.5 in.) provides higher shear stiffness and lower bearing stresses on concrete, compensating for their inherently low shear strength. [1]

Long-term faulting data across monitored joints show maximum displacements of 0.067 inches (1.7 mm) with an average displacement of 0.020 inches (0.508 mm). [8]

Changes in joint opening averages, steel dowels [8]

Iowa Highway 13 Study Results

The Iowa Highway 13 unbonded overlay study (cable-2006-composite-pavement-unbonded-overlays-phase3) found that faulting values moved in a sinusoidal pattern over time and were effectively zero for operational purposes, with measurements in unbonded overlay test sections exhibiting a sinusoidal pattern over time as values oscillated between positive and negative displacements rather than showing cumulative permanent settlement [2]. The addition of fibers to 3.5-inch and 4.5-inch overlay mixes did not produce significant differences in faulting levels compared to non-fibered sections, despite variations in fiber type (A, B, or C) and panel size [2], and fibers had no significant impact on faulting cable-2006-composite-pavement-unbonded-overlays-phase3. Furthermore, overlay depth comparisons between 3.5-inch and 4.5-inch sections showed no significant difference in faulting performance, contradicting historical expectations that deeper overlays would exhibit larger faulting values [2], while panel size and overlay depth did not produce appreciable differences cable-2006-composite-pavement-unbonded-overlays-phase3. Regarding surface preparation, scarification and patching provided nearly equal performance [2]; however, the HMA stress relief layer produced more variance [2], and the addition of an asphaltic concrete stress relief layer resulted in higher variance and greater maximum/minimum faulting values [2].

Interface bonding significantly affects pavement deflection behavior, with unbonded layer configurations exhibiting different deflection profiles compared to fully bonded composite sections [9].

Comparison of deflections for bonded layers and unbonded layers [9]

Measurement Protocols and Instrumentation

Joint load transfer efficiency (LTE) is quantified through deflection measurements expressed as a percentage, tracking both initial and present LTE values; this metric serves as a critical indicator of joint performance alongside other geometric properties such as saw cut depth and tie bar specifications [6]. Joint load transfer efficiency testing using the Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) is typically performed across joints every 100 to 500 feet, or up to every 1000 feet depending on project length and resources [10]. Such FWD LTE tests are preferably conducted at three specific load levels—8 kips, 12 kips, and 16 kips—to accurately evaluate joint performance [10], requiring the load plate to be placed 6 inches from the joint or crack with deflection sensors positioned at the center of the plate and 12 inches across the joint [10].

Seismic Pavement Analyzer (SPA) [10]

High-speed digital cameras, such as the Photron Fastcam®, can capture concrete joint movement at 1000 frames per second to detect faulting on the order of one mil (0.001 inch) resolution [10]. Faulting data collected during field testing are normalized for temperature and averaged over ten joints in each test section, with separate averages calculated for the driving lane and passing lane [7].

Joint Preparation and Construction Techniques

The ultimate strength and elastic dowel-concrete interaction at the interface are substantially impacted by concrete strength, dowel diameter, and joint opening width [1]. Even before being placed in concrete, steel dowels can experience wear from nicks occurring during construction, manufacturing, moving, and placing [1]. Furthermore, misalignment of dowel bars during placement or displacement can cause high-level stresses, necessitating finite element modeling to develop tolerance levels that prevent performance jeopardy while allowing construction cost savings [3]. While loose fitting dowel joints can provide several years of service without excessive faulting when properly constructed with good base support and exact elevation matching, they are recommended only for pavements 15 years old or older [3]. For preventing faulting of repair slabs, the use of tied joints constructed by grouting tie bars into drilled holes in existing concrete is a practical method, as hole drilling takes less than half the time required to manually saw steel for tying [3]. Regarding surface maintenance, concrete sealers are typically not applied until 28 days after placement to supplement weathering characteristics; these film-forming and penetrating types are designed to reduce contaminant penetration rather than replace proper curing [3]. Additionally, field monitoring using surveyor mag nails placed flush with the concrete surface allows for precise tracking of transverse joint opening changes relative to temperature fluctuations [8].

Relative deflection between adjacent pavement slabs [1]

In specific applications, such as ultrathin whitetopping, the use of a 1/8-inch joint width eliminates the need for joint sealing, though high-pressure air blasts are required to remove concrete dust [11]. For rehabilitation, using a rock saw to cut along damaged transverse joints at a width of 6 inches and then filling the void with hot mix asphaltic concrete before overlay placement serves as an effective technique for existing vertical movement and expansion damage [12]. In overlay applications with depths between 2 and 5 inches, the maximum distance for skipping joints during early-entry sawing should be limited to five joints to prevent premature transverse cracking [12]. To control centerline cracking in bonded concrete pavements, conventional saws guided by GPS receivers can align overlay joints within 1 inch horizontally from existing underlying joints [12]. Finally, a redesigned “knife” joint former successfully forms longitudinal joints when pavement is constructed one lane at a time, though finishing across the joint may occur if simultaneous finishing from both sides is not possible [12].

Overlay Design Guidelines and Parameters

To accommodate curl and warp without causing reflective cracks, joint spacing in PCC overlays should not exceed twice the depth in inches unless fibers are used [9]. For PCC depths of 6 inches or greater, tie bars across longitudinal joints are recommended due to placement problems; however, when overlay thickness is between 3 and 6 inches, they should be nailed to the existing ACC surface at 36-inch spacings [9]. Because concrete quantities for overlays often overrun by 10% to 20% due to irregularities in slab wheelpaths, construction surveys on 25-foot intervals are necessitated to identify high points and ensure minimum depth requirements [9]. Regarding structural design, the practical range for the layer coefficient of rubblized PCC pavements is 0.23 to 0.31, a value required by AASHTO design guides to determine HMA overlay thicknesses based on structural number-layer coefficient principles [13].

Layer condition underneath HMA after coring; (a) PCC layer without rubblization (IA-139 in Winneshiek County), (b) Rubblized PCC layer (IA-3 in Delaware County) [13]

To minimize the severity and extent of cracking that can lead to faulting, a width-to-thickness ratio between 1.2 and 1.5 should be used for 14 ft wide panels [5]. Better performance is expected with 14 ft panels when used in conjunction with a normal joint orientation, untreated aggregate base, and transverse joint spacing of 15 ft [5]. Additionally, the Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt Mechanistic-Empirical (BCOA-ME) design procedure calculates required overlay thickness based on maximum allowable cracked slab percentages rather than predicting performance directly [14].

15-foot joint spacing concrete overlays using BCOA-ME design predicted thickness versus maximum allowable percentage of cracked slabs [14]

Rubblization and Low-Volume Applications

Research identifies a critical need for cost-effective load transfer technologies specifically designed for low-volume roads that function as slab-on-grade systems, aiming to provide reliable performance at lower costs than traditional dowel bars [15]. Regarding pavement modification, rubblization using a modified whip hammer (MHB) produces a stiffer fractured layer with an average modulus of 175 ksi, which may be too stiff to effectively prevent reflection cracking compared to the lower and less variable modulus of approximately 67 ksi achieved by rotary fracture breakers [13]. Primary distresses contributing to underperformance in rubblized pavements include top-down cracking, joint reflective cracking, and raveling, with the majority of these issues found in areas of segregated HMA resulting from poor construction quality [13].

Modified Rubblization Method proposed by Antigo [13]

Internal Curing and Material Performance

Increasing the thermal conductivity of concrete pavement materials reduces both faulting and cracking, though cracking exhibits higher sensitivity to this parameter than faulting [16]. Furthermore, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) for concrete decreases as aggregate content increases, with cement paste CTE typically ranging from 11 to 20 microstrain/°C [16]. By lowering the coefficient of thermal expansion, modulus of elasticity, and ultimate shrinkage, internally cured concrete mitigates the negative effects of increased joint spacing on distress accumulation [17]. This allows internally cured concrete to reduce the minimum required pavement thickness from 8 inches to 7 inches and from 10 inches to 9 inches while maintaining equivalent joint faulting performance limits over a 30-year design life [17].

Relationship between thermal expansion of concrete and thermal expansion of its components (adopted from Ziegeldorf et al. 1978) [16]

Additionally, using internally cured concrete slightly compensates for the detrimental effect of increasing joint spacing from 15 feet to 20 feet on the ultimate International Roughness Index (IRI) value, thereby maintaining smoother pavement surfaces despite wider joint intervals [17]. This performance benefit occurs because internal curing decreases thermal expansion and shrinkage while simultaneously increasing compressive strength [17]. Consequently, internally cured pavements exhibit improved distress performance over time compared to conventionally cured counterparts, requiring less maintenance at later ages to provide satisfactory service life performance [17]. Although initial construction costs may be approximately 3.2% higher for internally cured concrete of the same thickness, reducing pavement thickness by one inch can offset this cost increase [17].

IRI of internally cured pavements over design life [17]

Faulting Severity and Joint Activation Analysis

Markov chain approaches provide the best correlations with field data for predicting transverse cracking probability and joint performance over 15 years of service, facilitating early estimation of future conditions [3]. Regarding bonded concrete overlays (BCOA), faulting severity levels are categorized such that low-severity distress is defined as vertical displacements between 0.12 and 0.24 inches, which constitutes the majority of observed field cases [18]. Field analysis indicates that for BCOA projects with 15-foot joint spacing, approximately 6% of total joints per mile may exhibit low-severity faulting by the 25-year service mark [18]. Conversely, in thinner bonded concrete overlays ranging from 3 to 4 inches in depth with typical joint spacing of 5.5 feet, faulting incidence remains lower, with most data points showing fewer than 14 faulted joints per mile [18]. Empirical studies on bonded concrete overlays demonstrate no significant correlation between traffic volume and faulting counts, suggesting that load transfer mechanisms are not primarily driven by traffic intensity in these applications [18], and there is little to no statistical relationship between the thickness of existing underlying hot-mix asphalt (HMA) layers and the resulting faulting counts in bonded concrete overlays [18].

Total faulting count per mile (4-in. BCOA) [18]

The ratio of slab length to radius of relative stiffness (L/ℓ) serves as a critical indicator for joint activation behavior, with optimal overlay performance achieved when this ratio is designed between 4 and 7 [14]. Furthermore, when contraction joints in bonded concrete overlays fail to deploy cracks under saw cuts, the pavement becomes susceptible to dominant joint behavior and increased faulting risks [18].

Joint activation vs. L/ℓ and joint spacing for Mitchell County concrete overlay test sections [14]

Computational Modeling and Mechanistic Analysis

Timoshenko’s analytical expression often fails to accurately back-predict measured displaced shapes of embedded dowels because it does not account for complex nonlinear behavior associated with dowel yielding [1]. Consequently, mechanistic analysis using a plate on a Winkler foundation model identifies joint load transfer phenomena as critical factors in the development of major distress types including faulting, pumping, and corner breaking [19]. Furthermore, mechanistic modeling of joint infiltration demonstrates that water and incompressible particles entering joints cause stress buildup that leads to base erosion, spalling, and blowups, with the potential for infiltrated materials to push bridge back walls and abutments [15].

Slope and deflection of dowel at joint face [1]

To address these complexities, advanced high-speed computational models are being developed to predict joint deterioration, pumping, and faulting by incorporating mechanistic principles that evaluate the combined effects of pavement design, site conditions, climate, and construction factors on long-term performance [15]. Additionally, artificial neural network models trained on finite element analysis data can predict maximum bending stresses and deflections in jointed concrete pavements with an average absolute error of less than 0.5 percent, effectively capturing complex multidimensional mappings of load transfer efficiencies and slab thicknesses [19].

ILLI-PAVE-based ANN backcalculation models for flexible pavements [19]

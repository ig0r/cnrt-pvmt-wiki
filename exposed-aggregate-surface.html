Exposed Aggregate Surface

Part of: Pavement Texture is-surface-finish-type-of
Exposed Aggregate Surface is a concrete pavement surface finish type of Pavement Texture.
frictionnoisepavementsurface-texture
Also known as: EAC surface, exposed aggregate concrete
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-11

Exposed Aggregate Surface is a specific texture finish within Pavement Texture in which the fine mortar is removed from the surface to expose the coarse aggregate, creating a rough, macrotextured surface intended to reduce tire/pavement noise while maintaining friction. This surface type can be achieved through techniques such as shot peening, which propels steel shot to remove a thin layer of material and create an open porous texture [3]. Additionally, it may result from two-lift paving methods where a special surface mix is used to build an exposed aggregate layer for durability and friction resistance [1].

Mix Design Requirements

High-performance concrete pavements require a maximum water-to-cement ratio of 0.38 and a minimum cement content of 450 kg/m³ (758.5 lb./cu. yd.), utilizing a plasticizer and air entrainer for workability and durability. To ensure quality, sand particles larger than 1 mm (0.04 in.) should be eliminated from the top layer, and polish-resistant aggregates with a polished stone value (PSV) >50 should be used. The use of ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) in these pavements affects early-age performance and strength gain, necessitating the use of maturity functions to monitor these effects during curing; furthermore, field measurements including slab temperature and curvature are employed alongside dynamic deflection testing on hardened slabs to evaluate the structural response [2]. Additionally, using the largest practical top-size coarse aggregate, such as a 50.0 mm (2 in.) maximum size, enhances the strength characteristics and durability of concrete pavements by producing greater aggregate interlock across pavement crack interfaces compared to smaller aggregate sizes [2].

The selection of air entraining agents is critical, as the use of synthetic air entraining admixtures as substitutes for vinsol resin can lead to significantly reduced concrete strengths due to interactions with low-alkali cements. These synthetics form thinner-walled air bubbles that develop rapid draining bubble flocculations more readily than vinsol resin, a problem exacerbated by high summertime temperatures [2]. Laboratory testing has demonstrated that synthetics have substantially different properties compared to vinsol resin, particularly regarding bubble wall thickness and flocculation [2]; however, mixtures containing 50% or more vinsol resin air entraining agents behave similarly to pure vinsol resin, mitigating strength reductions associated with synthetic admixtures [2].

Construction Methods and Techniques

Various methods are employed to manage pavement texture and historical construction techniques. Shot peening or shotblasting creates an open porous surface texture by propelling tiny steel shots onto the pavement to remove a thin layer of mortar and aggregate, which appears to increase skid numbers and reduce noise characteristics [3]. Similarly, sprinkle treatment involves distributing small, polish-resistant stone chips on fresh concrete to partially embed them, a technique used in the 1970s and 1980s for skid and noise applications that requires innovative application equipment [3]. To minimize surface irregularities such as corrugations that cause undesirable megatexture, a transverse finishing screed may be used in advance of a longitudinal oscillating float during construction [3]. However, transverse tining machines face geometric limitations where broom angles cannot be adjusted for crown variations, resulting in deeper tines at the road crown compared to the edges; this requires continuous horizontal angle adjustments during transitions to superelevation to maintain uniform tine depth [4]. Achieving consistent texture also depends on operator skill and concrete moisture content, as wetter mixes require lower machine elevation and greater tine angles while drier mixes need higher elevation and more vertical positioning [4].

Tining machine operations [4]

Historical applications include “Granitoid Concrete Streets” patented in 1906, which used a top lift of crushed granite (monument granite) with reduced fine aggregate to withstand steel-wheeled wagon wear; some sections constructed in 1909 and 1910 remain in service today [1]. Earlier implementations in the 1950s through 1990s utilized two-lift paving primarily to facilitate the placement of welded wire mesh between dowel baskets during interstate construction [1].

Two-Lift Paving Applications

Constructed using a two-layer “wet-on-wet” paving process, this method involves applying a set-retarding agent to newly placed concrete pavement so that after ~24 hours, the surface mortar can be brushed or washed away to expose durable aggregates [3]. The resulting top layer is 38–70 mm (1.5–2.75 in.) thick and consists of fine siliceous sand and high-quality coarse aggregate, with an ideal max size of 6–12 mm [3]. This two-lift construction enables exposed aggregate surfaces without using expensive non-polishing aggregates throughout the full pavement thickness, as only the thin top lift requires specialized aggregate, thereby reducing material costs [1]. During the Michigan I-75 project (1994), the European two-lift system was used specifically to construct an exposed aggregate surface for noise reduction; in this instance, the top layer was 64 mm (2.5 in.) thick with polish-resistant aggregates, while the bottom layer was 191 mm (7.5 in.) thick with conventional aggregates [1], [3]. However, Michigan DOT found only 0.4 dBA noise reduction, which was attributed to excessive macrotexture and large sand particles (>1 mm) [1], [3].

Finished exposed aggregate surface [3]

In regions with studded tire usage, such as Washington state, two-lift construction can be employed to address rutting by utilizing different flexural strengths for each layer [1]. Experimental test sections demonstrated that higher flexural strength concrete (900 psi) exhibited minimal wear from studded tires compared to lower strength sections (550 psi and 650 psi) [1]. Similarly, in regions with studded tires such as Sweden and Austria, exposed aggregate surfaces have been refined to withstand winter wear; specifically, Austrian specifications reduced coarse aggregate size to substantially lower tire-pavement noise levels compared to conventional textures [3].

two-lift paving can create an exposed aggregate surface in the top layer by incorporating coarse aggregate and reducing fine aggregate content, yielding a texture that is both quiet and rough for enhanced durability and friction resistance [1].

Noise Performance and Texture Geometry

For adequate friction, the recommended average texture depth is 0.9 mm (0.035 in.) [3]. To meet higher skid requirements for low- and high-speed resistance, the United Kingdom specifies larger aggregates and deeper texture depths than Austria, utilizing this technique on continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP) rather than jointed pavements [3]. While European projects claim 4–5 dBA noise reductions, the effectiveness of aggregate size varies; smaller aggregates (8 mm) provide better noise reduction (3.0–3.5 dBA lower than HMA) than larger aggregates (16 mm: 1.0–1.5 dBA lower), and a Dutch study found that 20 mm aggregates outperformed 32 mm aggregates [3]. Additionally, a “plateau with gorges” texture is quieter than “peaks and valleys” texture, and a Swedish study noted that 8 mm aggregate sections remained quieter than HMA after one year, though all pavements deteriorated after three years [3].

Exposed aggregate concrete pavement is perceived to have better tonal qualities than conventionally textured concrete surfaces, particularly when compared to transversely tined surfaces [3]. Furthermore, aggressive texturing of fresh concrete can lead to latent deposits of concrete on the surface, which subsequently increases tire-pavement noise levels [5]. Finally, exposed aggregate surfaces on European-style concrete pavements have been observed to lose macro-texture in inner lanes over time, resulting in increased surface friction numbers but only slight reductions in exterior noise levels, a performance degradation that contrasts with the expected noise reduction benefits of the texture [2].

Long-Term Durability Case Study

In Bettendorf, Iowa, a fifty-year-old concrete pavement exhibits an exposed aggregate surface that provides a quieter and reflection-free performance while maintaining good structural condition; this long-term durability is attributed to the use of river bed material for both the subbase and the concrete mix [6].

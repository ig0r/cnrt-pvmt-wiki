L/ℓ Ratio

Part of: Slab Design & Geometry is-design-parameter-of
L/ℓ Ratio is a design parameter of Slab Design & Geometry.
conceptconcrete-pavementdesign-parameterslab-geometrystructural-analysis
Also known as: L-over-ell, L/ℓ, slab length to radius of relative stiffness ratio
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-11

The L/ℓ Ratio is a key component of Slab Design & Geometry, defined as the ratio of slab length (L) to the radius of relative stiffness (ℓ). This dimensionless parameter serves as a primary input for determining transverse reinforcement distribution and predicting joint activation behavior in concrete overlays. It influences how stresses are calculated within concrete pavements, with historical research by Bradbury establishing that curling stresses are affected by slab length and the degree of support under the slab [2]. Identified in IHRB Project TR-698 Phase 2A as a key design indicator for optimizing joint spacing in thin concrete overlays, this parameter is essential for understanding geometric aspect ratios in rigid pavement design.

Definition of Radius of Relative Stiffness (ℓ)

The radius of relative stiffness ($\ell$) was defined by Westergaard (1927) as:

$$\ell = \sqrt[4]{\frac{Eh^3}{12k(1-\mu^2)}}$$

In this equation, $E$ represents the modulus of elasticity, $h$ is the slab thickness, $\mu$ is Poisson’s ratio, and $k$ is the modulus of subgrade reaction.

Westergaard’s Theoretical Framework

When the calculated L/ℓ ratio falls below eight, Westergaard’s infinite plate equations require specific corrections applied to both the maximum deflection and the radius of relative stiffness to account for finite slab behavior [1]. Furthermore, the nonlinear temperature distribution within PCC slabs deviates from the linear assumption in Westergaard’s original equations due to material and geometrical nonlinearities [2]; this nonlinearity can cause measured curling stresses to reach magnitudes comparable to those produced by the heaviest legal wheel loads [2].

Typical temperature distribution throughout concrete slab depth [2]

Factors Influencing Curling Stress and Slab Behavior

As L/ℓ increases from 1 to 8, the differential curling stress coefficient (Cx) increases significantly (Bradbury 1938), leading to higher stresses that promote joint activation. Outside this range (L/ℓ < 1 or > 8), slab proportions have little effect on the stress coefficient, and temperature differential becomes the dominant parameter. [3]

Creep acts as a time-dependent stress relaxation mechanism that reduces slab curling over the pavement’s service life. This viscous effect causes substantial drying shrinkage tensile stresses at the top of the slab to decrease, allowing for partial recovery and reducing upward built-in curling at the slab corners. [2]

Effect of creep on concrete deformation [2]

Loss of contact between the slab and subgrade occurs at the center during downward curling and at the corners during upward curling, which increases stresses and aggravates deterioration through repeated loading. This loss of contact is a critical factor because curling and warping stresses can be more significant than traffic loads in causing premature cracking. [2]

Stresses exerted due to PCC curling and warping: tensile stress exerted at top of PCC slab with upward curvature (top), and tensile stress exerted at bottom of PCC slab with downward curvature (bottom) [2]

Longer joint spacing increases the risk of larger joint openings and random mid-panel cracking due to elevated curling stresses, which can compromise ride quality and load transfer efficiency over time. [3]

Joint activation vs. L/ℓ and joint spacing for Mitchell County concrete overlay test sections [3]

Design Recommendations for L/ℓ Ratios

Designing joint spacing to achieve an L/ℓ between 4 and 7 provides a desired balance between maximum, timely joint activation—as higher L/ℓ values correlate with more rapid and complete joint activation—and good overlay performance, which involves avoiding excessive L/ℓ that increases curling stress, random cracking risk, and larger joint openings. This recommendation was validated by test sections in both Mitchell County (BCOA) and Buchanan County (UBCOC), where the relationship between L/ℓ and joint activation was consistent across overlay types.

Impact of Overlay Thickness and Fibers on Performance

Thicker overlays (6 inches) exhibit faster initial joint activation rates compared to thinner (4-inch) sections, particularly during the first winter of service when activation in 4-inch slabs may lag at 33–47% versus 80% for 6-inch slabs [3]. This difference is attributed to increased temperature differentials and curling stresses inherent to greater slab thicknesses [3]. Furthermore, the addition of synthetic macro-fibers at a dosage of 4 lb/yd³ does not significantly alter joint activation rates or ultimate activation percentages [3]; while fibers may control crack widths, they do not impact the stress required to initiate cracking in this context [3].

Percentage of joints activated for different joint spacing of 6-inch thick concrete overlays [3]

Practical Joint Spacing Calculations

For typical concrete overlay parameters, a 4-inch thick overlay on 150 psi/in. subgrade results in ℓ ≈ 2.1 ft, where a 6-ft joint spacing gives L/ℓ ≈ 2.9, 12-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 5.7, 15-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 7.1, and 20-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 9.5. Similarly, a 6-inch thick overlay on 150 psi/in. subgrade results in ℓ ≈ 2.4 ft, where a 6-ft joint spacing gives L/ℓ ≈ 2.5, 12-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 5.0, 15-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 6.3, and 20-ft gives L/ℓ ≈ 8.4. Depending on subgrade conditions, the L/ℓ range of 4–7 corresponds to joint spacings of approximately 8–16 feet for 4-inch overlays and 10–17 feet for 6-inch overlays.

Measurement Considerations and Environmental Factors

Joint activation measurements using MIRA devices can be influenced by ambient temperature, as slabs contract and joints open in cold weather (e.g., 14°F) but close in warmer conditions (e.g., 57°F). This thermal effect may cause underestimation of activation rates during warm-weather testing for short slab sections. [3]

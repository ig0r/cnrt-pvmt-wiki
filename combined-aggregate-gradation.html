Combined Aggregate Gradation

Part of: Mix Design & Specialty Mixtures is-design-approach-of
Combined Aggregate Gradation is a mix design approach grouped under Mix Design & Specialty Mixtures.
materialsmix-designpcc
Also known as: Aggregate Gradation Optimization, Combined Grading
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Combined aggregate gradation is the overall particle-size distribution achieved by blending coarse, intermediate, and fine aggregates in a concrete mix to form a continuous grading curve. This process involves the purposeful selection and proportioning of various aggregate sizes so that their particles interlock and fill voids efficiently within the mixture [1] [4]. The resulting dense packing reduces the required volume of cement paste while enhancing workability, minimizing shrinkage potential, and lowering permeability to create a durable concrete matrix [1] [8].

Key findings

  • Well-graded mixes clustered in lower-coarseness and moderate-workability zones demonstrated satisfactory finishing and better edge retention, whereas sandier or gap-graded mixes exhibited higher water demand and an increased risk of early cracking [1].
  • Adding intermediate-sized aggregate improved workability and reduced the water demand required for gap-graded blends [1].
  • Increasing the proportion of fine aggregate raised workability but made further compaction harder once the sand-to-coarse-aggregate ratio exceeded approximately 7.5% by mass due to thickened paste around aggregates [7].
  • Porosity decreased as sand content rose, reaching its lowest point near a 12.5% sand proportion, while seven-day compressive strength peaked at roughly 7.5% sand [7].
  • Field evaluations confirmed that combined aggregate gradations across multiple sites fell within prescribed power-curve limits and were consistently judged to be well-graded based on coarseness factor analysis [6].
  • Different aggregate systems, including those with varying limestone-sand blends, satisfied recommended gradation zones despite differences in void content and workability classifications [4].
  • Employing optimized combined aggregate gradation allowed designers to reduce cement content while maintaining a workable mix, thereby lowering CO₂ emissions, shrinkage, and cost without sacrificing performance [11].

Evaluation, Optimization, and Effects of Combined Aggregate Gradation

Combined aggregate gradation is the overall particle-size distribution achieved by blending coarse, intermediate, and fine aggregates to create a continuous range that fills voids efficiently [1] [4] [8]. The underlying mechanism relies on proper proportioning so that particles interlock and pack densely, which minimizes the volume of cement paste required for workability and strength [1] [8]. Evaluation typically involves plotting the combined sieve curve against standard target grading charts or measuring the voids content of the packed skeleton to assess suitability [4] [8]. Optimization of this gradation yields a stable interlocked framework that enhances workability, reduces shrinkage and permeability, and improves overall durability by limiting segregation [1].

Research problem — Concrete mixes often suffer from improper combined aggregate gradation, leading to poor workability, segregation, and reduced durability due to excessive voids or inappropriate particle size distributions [1]. Establishing a consistent fine-to-total aggregate ratio is challenging when available materials produce non-ideal grading, creating excess inter-particle voids that compromise strength and durability if not properly managed by cement paste volume [2]. There is a need to verify whether field-used combined aggregate gradations satisfy accepted grading limits and target performance zones to ensure adequate workability, compaction, and long-term pavement performance [4] [6]. Substituting fine aggregates with alternative materials requires careful verification that the resulting combined gradation remains within acceptable limits to maintain fresh concrete properties and hardened performance characteristics [9]. Prescriptive specification requirements often lock designers into fixed aggregate gradations, forcing reliance on excess cementitious content to achieve workability and durability, which increases costs and environmental impact without necessarily improving performance [11]. Achieving a suitable combined aggregate gradation is difficult when raw aggregates vary between sources or when incorporating specialized materials like lightweight fine aggregates or electrically conductive fibers, as improper grading can undermine structural and functional performance goals [8] [10]. In roller-compacted concrete, inadequate combined aggregate gradation prevents dense packing, leading to higher water demand, lower density, and reduced strength and durability due to an imbalance in cohesion and compactibility [3]. Pervious concrete practice faces challenges in controlling void ratio and achieving sufficient strength when relying on single-sized aggregates, necessitating the optimization of combined coarse- and fine-aggregate gradations to balance permeability with mechanical performance [7].

State of the art — The industry standard for evaluating combined aggregate gradation relies on laboratory sieve analysis to calculate coarseness and workability factors, which are then plotted against established graphical tools such as the Shilstone chart or Tarantula curve to classify mixtures and verify compliance with ASTM C33 limits [1] [4] [6] [10]. Optimization techniques including power-curve methods and the Shilstone approach are routinely employed to design dense-graded blends that maximize packing density, reduce cementitious material demand, and ensure adequate workability for construction [1] [2] [6] [8]. Current consensus holds that well-graded or dense blends offer superior durability and lower cracking risk compared to gap-graded or overly sandy mixes, with recent advances focusing on rapid field verification methods to ensure gradation targets are met during construction [1] [4] [7].

Findings from the reports

  • Field evaluations demonstrated that well-graded combined aggregate gradations improved fresh concrete workability and hardened durability, while gap-graded or sandier blends increased water demand and the risk of early shrinkage cracking [1].
  • Laboratory assessments revealed that increasing the proportion of fine aggregate (sand) in pervious concrete mixes raises workability but reduces porosity and compaction resistance beyond a specific threshold, with compressive strength peaking at approximately 7.5% sand content [7].
  • The project evaluated combined aggregate systems using coarseness-factor methods and power-curve plots, confirming that most field mixes fell within prescribed limits and were classified as well-graded across multiple sites [6].
  • Performance-engineered mixture designs utilizing combined aggregate gradations satisfied Tarantula curve recommendations, ensuring acceptable overall gradation for ECON concrete applications [8].
  • The report presented combined aggregate gradations for conventional and internal-curing mixes via haystack charts, though it did not provide specific numerical performance comparisons based on those distributions [9].
  • RCC mix designs utilizing a specific combined particle size distribution achieved target compaction performance with an optimum moisture content of 6.51% and a maximum dry density of 2375 kg/m³ [3].
  • The study evaluated two distinct aggregate systems, finding that one system produced desirable workability and coarseness factors in Zone II with low void content, while the other fell into a sandy Zone IV despite both satisfying Tarantula curve recommendations [4].
  • Employing optimized combined aggregate gradation allowed designers to reduce cement content while maintaining workable mix performance, thereby lowering CO₂ emissions, shrinkage, and costs without sacrificing overall quality [11].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel diagnostic framework that evaluates combined aggregate gradation by plotting coarseness and workability factors on a unified chart, linking these metrics directly to field performance indicators such as cracking resistance [1]. Distinctive optimization strategies include fixing the fine-to-total aggregate ratio at a constant value derived from multiple grading analyses to isolate cementitious effects, and employing multi-criteria graphical validation against tools like the Tarantula curve and Shilstone chart [2] [4]. The approach is uniquely extended to specialized applications by integrating lightweight fine aggregate for internal curing without altering the water-to-cement ratio, and by systematically varying fine-aggregate proportions in pervious concrete to balance workability with porosity [7] [9].

Across the reviewed reports, field evaluations and laboratory studies collectively establish that well-graded combined aggregate systems consistently yield superior workability and finishing characteristics compared to gap-graded or excessively sandy blends. While specific gradation methods vary, multiple investigations confirm that optimizing the blend—often by including intermediate-sized aggregates or targeting specific coarseness factors—reduces void content and water demand without compromising structural integrity. Furthermore, systematic adjustments to the fine-to-coarse aggregate ratio directly control key physical properties such as void content, permeability, and compressive strength, with optimal ratios balancing workability gains against compaction resistance. [1] [4] [6] [7] [9]

Implications — Designers should target dense, well-graded aggregate blends that fall within established coarseness and workability ranges to minimize cement usage, reduce shrinkage cracking, and enhance long-term pavement durability [1]. Specifications can reliably adopt the 0.45 power-curve as a target range for combined aggregate gradations, allowing agencies to ensure consistent fresh properties and strength development without requiring special aggregate modifications [6]. Mix designs should utilize gradation-based charts to select blends that avoid sandy zones, thereby ensuring superior workability, curb integrity, and finishability compared to simpler binary systems [4]. Agencies are encouraged to incorporate optimized gradations like the Tarantula curve into contracts to lower cement content, reduce carbon emissions, and improve surface smoothness while cutting construction costs [11]. The proportion of fine aggregate in a combined gradation should be kept modest to balance workability and strength, as excessive fines can hinder compaction and reduce the effectiveness of the mix structure [7].

Limitations — The benefits of combined aggregate gradation are contingent upon the mix falling within a well-graded region, as gap-graded or overly sandy blends can lead to early cracking and reduced durability [1]. Optimization is constrained by a narrow range of fine-aggregate content, where excessive sand thickens the mortar and stiffens the mix, while insufficient amounts fail to improve compaction resistance [7]. Results are specific to particular coarse-aggregate gradations and may not extend to other aggregate blends, limiting the generalizability of the optimization findings [7].

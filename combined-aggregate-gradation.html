Combined Aggregate Gradation

A mix design approach that optimizes the combined gradation of all aggregates (coarse and fine) together rather than treating them separately. 7 of 17 MCO states had combined gradation specifications in place as of the MCO survey. [1]

Overview of Combined Aggregate Gradation

Combined grading was evaluated as part of the MCO Suite workability focal area, which connects to Performance-Based Mix Design and the Vp/Vvoid Ratio. Uniform aggregate gradation specifications can utilize percent-retained charts with specific boundaries, such as the #30 and 3/4 inch range [1], and specifications may mandate that no two adjacent sieve sizes account for less than 14% of the total gradation within defined boundaries to prevent gap grading [1].

The bulk density of combined aggregates can be measured using ASTM C29, resulting in a unit weight of 131 lb/ft³ and a void percentage of 19.8 percent [2]. In roller-compacted concrete (RCC), the combined particle grading incorporates binder particles; in such cases, a typical aggregate-to-binder ratio of seven yields a maximum dry density around 2375 kg/m³ at an optimum moisture content near 6.51% [3].

Methods for Selecting Fine-to-Total Aggregate Ratios

The Taylor et al. (2012) cementitious content optimization study evaluated three methods for selecting the fine-to-total aggregate ratio to keep void content constant across all test mixtures:

  1. 0.45 Power curve: spreadsheet solver found ratio closest to optimum gradation → 0.45
  2. Shilstone workability factor chart: placed combined system within Zone II → 0.42
  3. Specific surface approach: values from 2 in. to #200 sieves → 0.39

The average of 0.42 was selected. The combined aggregate void content was measured at 19.8% (ASTM C29). [2]

The specific surface approach for determining the fine-to-total aggregate ratio utilizes values from the 2-inch to #200 sieves, yielding a preferred ratio of 0.39. [2]

The voids in the combined aggregate were measured to be 24.5 percent. [4]

Specific combined gradation profiles, such as those analyzed for the I-15 project between Farr West and Brigham City, are evaluated using Tarantula curves alongside coarseness and workability factor charts to assess pavement performance. [5]

Evaluation Tools and Visualization Plots

To evaluate combined aggregate gradation, practitioners may use traditional methods like the 0.45 power curve alongside specialized plots such as the Haystack chart and the Tarantula curve [4]. The Tarantula curve serves as a specialized plot for visualizing the combined percent retained gradation, offering an alternative visualization method to traditional power curves and factor charts [5]. Additionally, the workability factor versus coarseness factor chart is used to assess fresh concrete characteristics by plotting combined gradation results against established control lines [1]. Within these systems, a coarseness factor exceeding 70—such as the 76.2 recorded for a California project using 1-inch maximum size aggregate—indicates that combined gradation falls outside the well-graded region and may cause dips in the percent retained curve [6]. Furthermore, when the combined aggregate gradation is plotted on a workability factor chart, systems falling into Zone IV are classified as sandy, which correlates with an increased risk of early cracking [4].

(a) individual and combined gradation of System I and II, (b) workability factor chart, (c) power 45 chart, (d) Haystack chart, and (e) Tarantula curve [4]

Real-time smoothness monitoring systems can utilize this combined gradation data, including Tarantula curves and coarseness/workability factor plots, to actively improve the initial smoothness of concrete pavements during construction [5].

Impact of Gradation on Fresh Concrete Properties

Well-graded mixes that are close to the 0.45 power maximum density curve hold edge shape better and are easier to finish. In contrast, mixtures falling above control lines are identified as sandy, which is associated with an increased risk of early cracking [1]; specifically, Kansas, Wisconsin, and Georgia had sandy mixtures above control lines, suggesting early cracking risk. Furthermore, sand content, type, and grading significantly influence the compatibility of air-entraining admixtures (AEAs), which can dramatically alter the workability of fresh concrete when used alongside plasticizers [3].

Gravimetric Air Content vs. Unit Weight of Fresh Concrete [1]

Regarding aggregate proportions, increasing fine aggregate content beyond 7.5% by mass relative to coarse aggregate reduces resistance to compaction by increasing paste thickness around particles, while peak compressive strength occurs at 7.5% fine aggregate content [7].

Effect of fine aggregate content on workability, strength, and porosity [7]

Gradation Optimization in Specialized Concrete Types

Aggregate optimization using uniformly graded aggregates can significantly improve workability to the point where pavement smoothness is maintained [1], which also helps solve segregation problems commonly found in ordinary gap-graded mixes composed of large stone and sand [1]. In pervious concrete, the combined aggregate gradation typically includes approximately 30% fine aggregate by mass when using crushed granite coarse aggregate with 18% passing the No. 4 sieve and adding river sand [7], though a combined gradation with 10% fine aggregate relative to coarse aggregate is selected for pervious concrete overlays when balancing workability improvements against minimal compressive strength reduction [7].

In electrically conductive concrete (ECON) production, combined aggregate gradation is optimized to facilitate a continuous percolation network for chopped carbon fibers, with specific mixtures utilizing 49% coarse, 7% intermediate, and 44% fine aggregates or a 50/50 coarse-to-fine ratio [8]. These combined gradations for ECON mixtures are evaluated against Tarantula curve boundaries, where specific plant aggregates conform to these defined limits to ensure proper particle packing and fiber distribution [8]. Specifically, Plant-1 aggregates for ECON consisted of limestone (1 in. nominal maximum size), 3/8 in. gravel intermediate aggregate, and natural sand passing a #4 sieve, while Plant-2 used limestone (3/4 in.) and natural sand without an intermediate aggregate [8].

Internal Curing and Rheology Modification

A Paste Thickness Index (PTI) ranging from 100 to 250 µm indicates that the interfacial transition zone typically constitutes a significant fraction of the nominal paste thickness [2]. In broad terms, about 1.5 to 2 times more paste is required than the space between combined aggregate particles to ensure all voids are filled and particles are coated [2]. Consequently, the paste to combined aggregate voids volume ratio (Vpaste/Vvoids) serves as a critical design parameter that typically ranges between 125 and 175 percent to ensure adequate workability without excessive cementitious content [4].

Cementing efficiency of different binder systems at 28 days as a function of Vp/Vvoid [2]

To facilitate internal curing, replacing approximately 20% by mass of fine aggregate with lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) creates a combined gradation where particles act as internal water reservoirs, providing curing water to the hydrating mixture without altering the batch water-to-cement ratio [9]. The uniform distribution of lightweight fine aggregate within the combined gradation ensures that internal curing benefits extend throughout the entire concrete volume rather than being limited to surface layers [9], and concrete mixtures utilizing this combined gradation approach exhibit reduced autogenous shrinkage, lower permeability, and enhanced stiffness compared to standard mixes [9]. Alternatively, superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) can be incorporated into the combined aggregate gradation to function as internal water reservoirs, allowing for a low initial water-to-cementitious materials ratio while later releasing stored water to sustain cement hydration and mitigate early-age shrinkage cracking [10]. When SAP is added to the mixture, it absorbs a portion of the mixing water in a way that dramatically exceeds its own mass and volume, requiring dosage calculations based on specific absorption rates determined through methods like the tea bag test [10]. In shotcrete applications, SAP-modified combined gradations can serve as rheology modifiers to increase viscosity and decrease rebound without the need for additional set-accelerating admixtures [10]; however, the paste volume, including the absorbed water within the SAP particles, must be limited to no more than 26.5% of the total concrete design volume to maintain mixture integrity [10].

Effects of SAP on the slump of concrete for (a) SAP-a and (b) SAP-b, where Sa-0 is dry and Sa-10 is pre-absorbed (particle size: SAP-a > SAP-b) [10]

Sustainability and Environmental Impacts

A well-graded concrete mix generally requires a relatively low cementitious material content, which reduces the probability of shrinkage cracking [1]. Furthermore, optimized combined aggregate gradations enable a reduction in cementitious content, which directly lowers CO2 emissions during construction while maintaining or improving mixture workability through reduced paste volume [11].

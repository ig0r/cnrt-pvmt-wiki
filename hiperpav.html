HIPERPAV

Part of: Pavement Analysis Software is-early-age-analysis-software-of
HIPERPAV is an early-age analysis software tool of Pavement Analysis Software.
concrete-pavementcrackingearly-agemodeling
Also known as: High Performance Paving
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-11

HIPERPAV serves as a specialized module within the Pavement Analysis Software suite, developed by the Transtec Group, Inc. for the Federal Highway Administration to predict early-age thermal and moisture-induced stresses in concrete pavements [5]. It utilizes hydration parameters derived from isothermal and semiadiabatic tests to accurately forecast pavement temperatures, which are critical for calculating developed stresses and material strength during the initial curing phase [5]. The program assesses the early-age cracking potential of concrete pavements within the first 72 hours after paving [1], a period defined as the first 48 to 96 hours during which PCC strength remains relatively low while stresses become significant due to large volumetric changes from temperature and moisture conditions [2]. By analyzing these factors across different seasonal conditions, HIPERPAV enables engineers to assess whether concrete strength remains sufficient to prevent cracking within the first few days after paving [1].

Design and Material Inputs

The parameters influencing the process include structural factors such as subbase type, slab-base friction, joint spacing, and slab thickness. Material properties are also critical, encompassing OPC type, cement chemistry, SCM type/content, water content, aggregate type/content, and admixtures.

Environmental conditions, including air temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed, play a role alongside construction variables such as curing method/timing, saw cutting timing, initial concrete temperature, and subbase temperature.

Aggregate Characteristics and Mix Integrity

Aggregate shape, texture, moisture content, and gradation significantly influence concrete mix packing, bonding characteristics, and rheological properties [3]. For instance, elongated and flaky aggregates do not pack together in a concrete mix as effectively as rounded aggregates [3], while smooth rounded aggregates do not bond as strongly as crushed aggregates with jagged fractured faces [3]. Additionally, well-graded aggregates tend to produce more workable mixes with better durability compared to poorly graded ones [3]. To maintain quality, materials sources and dosages must remain consistent with trial batches; specifically, materials cannot be changed without new trials, and dosage variations exceeding 5% of total cementitious content require re-evaluation [4]. Furthermore, water addition to mixtures after initial mixing is prohibited to maintain the integrity of the water-to-cement ratio established during trial batch testing [4].

0.45 Power curve of combined aggregate gradation [4]

Robust testing involving 50% variations in water reducer and fly ash dosages demonstrates that heat generation curves shift left or right without indicating incompatibility, a method that can establish acceptable heat evolution boundaries for field concrete quality control [5]. Finally, the Wenner Probe test method provides a rapid and cost-effective means to assess the surface resistivity of concrete, which has been adopted by multiple state departments of transportation as an acceptance tool [4].

Whitetopping Overlay Construction Requirements

In thin whitetopping overlays where PCC depth is less than 3 to 6 inches at longitudinal joints between new widening units and existing pavement, tie bars should be nailed to the existing asphalt surface at 36-inch spacings [2]. For these thin overlays with joint spacings of 15 to 20 feet, initial transverse joints should be sawed first, followed by intermediate joints, a sequence designed to prevent premature cracking during hot weather [2]. Additionally, concrete placement for whitetopping overlays requires the existing surface temperature to remain below 100 degrees Fahrenheit; if this threshold is exceeded, the surface must be sprayed with water and excess moisture removed before concrete placement [2]. For mixes without slag or similar retarding materials, joint formation operations should be maintained within 1,000 to 1,500 feet of the slipform paver [2]. Regarding expansion joints in a thin concrete overlay, they must match those in the existing pavement, which requires prior location of existing expansion joints before placing the interlayer [6]; installation can be accomplished by making two full-overlay-depth saw cuts one inch apart at a contraction joint near an existing expansion joint and replacing the removed concrete with expansion material [6].

For unbonded concrete overlays over existing concrete, providing a positive drainage path through the interlayer bond breaker is necessary in non-arid climates to prevent interlayer erosion under heavy traffic loadings [6], and enhancing final overlay performance can be achieved in these designs by correcting irregularities in cross-slope and profile by varying the thickness of the concrete rather than the depth of the asphalt bond breaker [6]. When placing bonded concrete overlays in cooler weather, specifying a minimum overlay mix temperature of 65°F helps mitigate set-time issues caused by day/night temperature differentials that induce movement in the existing pavement [6], and temporarily covering a concrete overlay with plastic after paving helps the concrete set properly from the bottom up during cool-weather placement, allowing for timely sawing [6]. Construction efficiency can be improved by milling the existing widening unit to a depth matching the new widening unit to reduce the number of longitudinal joints from two to one, simplifying joint patching requirements [6], and using stringless milling technology guided by a computer model based on 9-shot cross sections at 50-foot intervals can significantly reduce concrete quantity overruns compared to plan estimates [6]. Furthermore, when anchoring dowel baskets for load transfer, using uniform thicknesses of separation layer and ensuring adequate anchor numbers helps minimize the head of concrete in front of the paver [6], while in single-lane overlay construction with 24-hour pilot car operations on a two-lane road, work zones should be separated by two miles between flagger stations to manage traffic control effectively [6].

Thermal Behavior and Cracking Risk Factors

A concrete mixture can be characterized by a unique thermal signature representing the amount and rate of heat generated through hydration, which is measured using isothermal, adiabatic, or semi-adiabatic calorimeters [3]. Consequently, research aims to develop nonproprietary test standards for measuring thermal properties like specific heat and thermal conductivity, which are sensitive parameters for predicting concrete pavement behavior [3]. To determine accurate heat evolution predictions, activation energy for hydration curve parameters is computed using the Arrhenius equation; notably, the addition of water reducers and fly ash replacement appears to improve activation energy values [5]. Furthermore, a Heat Evolution Number (HEN) has been proposed as a method to characterize concrete mixes based on their thermal fingerprint, allowing selection of appropriate mix designs for specific weather conditions [7]. The maximum heat of cement hydration in binary/ternary cement concrete decreases with an increase in the amount of supplementary cementitious material (SCM) replacements, resulting in a generally lower risk of thermal cracking compared to ordinary Portland cement (OPC) [1]. Under average seasonal conditions, no cracking is predicted for binary/ternary cements; however, the risk increases for early spring/late fall as actual weather may deviate from seasonal averages. Specifically, summer presents a low cracking risk with a strength-stress margin of 23-37.7 psi, whereas spring/fall carries a higher risk due to slower strength gain and larger thermal/moisture gradients, with the margin as low as 6.2 psi.

Thermal activity of each component (adapted from Maekawa et al. 1999) [7]

Curing temperature significantly impacts early-age strength development, as lowering the curing temperature from 70°F to 50°F more than doubles the set times for both binary and ternary cement concretes [1]. Additionally, rapid drying of moisture on exposed slab surfaces causes non-uniform shrinkage and volume change through the depth, necessitating uniform curing coverage to prevent loss of mixing water [8]. Paving timing also influences pavement quality; field data indicates that morning paving produces smoother jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) compared to afternoon paving due to minimized curling and warping effects [8]. While nighttime paving can reduce permanent curling and warping by preventing the temperature gradient change that occurs before and after concrete hardening, it may increase construction costs and safety concerns [8].

HIPERPAV Modeling and Predictive Analysis

The HIPERPAV program can utilize pavement temperature data collected from embedded sensors to calibrate models and estimate temperature gradients for curling predictions [9]. By incorporating ambient weather data alongside these pavement temperatures, the program can accurately estimate temperature gradients that drive thermal stresses in the slab [9]. Consequently, HIPERPAV serves as a performance-based evaluation tool for paving applications that can be used to verify that early cracking is unlikely for the expected weather conditions during construction [4]. However, HIPERPAV predictions can be sensitive to minor variations in weather or mix characteristics, where slight changes may trigger random cracking even if initial analyses showed no risk [10]; therefore, the program’s sensitivity analysis capabilities are intended to be improved to help identify which specific variables most significantly contribute to potential cracking [10].

(a). Concrete temperatures versus time for heat of hydration [4]

To improve reliability, HIPERPAV can accept direct calorimeter test results or heat evolution indexes as input data, which improves the reliability of pavement strength and stress predictions compared to using default predicted heat evolution [7]. Specifically, FHWA Project 17 Phase III (wang-2008-simple-rapid-test-heat-evolution-phase3) modified HIPERPAV to accept direct calorimeter heat evolution data (both isothermal and semi-adiabatic) instead of relying on default hydration models [5]. This modified software was validated against field temperature measurements at three construction sites (Atlantic IA, Alma Center WI, Ottumwa IA), and calorimetry-based HIPERPAV predictions improved thermal cracking risk assessment compared to default material inputs [5]. Furthermore, HIPERPAV II was modified to accept user-defined hydration curve parameters (αu, β, and τ) instead of relying on linear regression models based solely on cement chemistry, allowing direct input of heat evolution data from calorimetry tests to improve prediction reliability [5].

Windows of inputs of hydration curve parameters in the modified HIPERPAV II software. [5]

The software utilizes these heat evolution inputs to predict temperature development in pavement slabs exposed to specific environmental conditions and curing methods, though HIPERPAV predictions for set time, strength gain, and critical stress-to-strength ratio are sensitive to the duration of calorimetric data input, with considerable differences observed between 24-hour and seven-day datasets [11]. Additionally, HIPERPAV can be complemented with heat evolution tests to predict sawing and finishing windows based on concrete hardening processes [11]. In this context, sawing must occur within a time window starting when concrete supports equipment without raveling and ending before excessive stresses develop [11].

Heat indexes for samples under different curing conditions [11]

Calorimetry Integration and Heat Evolution Testing

FHWA Project 17 Phase II (wang-2007-simple-rapid-test-heat-evolution-phase2) utilized calorimetry results from isothermal and semi-adiabatic methods to determine the degree of hydration (DOH) for HIPERPAV input, revealing a significant DOH difference between the semi-adiabatic vs. isothermal methods [11]; this discrepancy was likely due to inaccurate total heat estimates resulting from the use of models rather than ASTM C 186 [11]. To improve accuracy, it is recommended to use ASTM C 186 for total heat and verify predictions with field measurements [11]. Heat evolution tests serve as an alternative to predictive models using typical ASTM C 150 and C 595 cement characteristics, which vary significantly between plants and kilns, by determining specific heat of hydration characteristics for materials used to provide better input data than generic chemical composition databases [11]. Furthermore, a mathematical model using the finite difference method was developed to convert isothermal calorimetry data from cement mortar into semi-adiabatic calorimetry data for cement concrete, allowing users to utilize isothermal test results within the modified HIPERPAV II framework [5].

Predicted pavement temperatures versus measurements (pavement top, Alma Center, WI ) [5]

Heat indexes derived from the first derivative of the calorimeter curve and the area under the curve can characterize mortar features to predict set time and early-age strength up to two days, allowing for the detection of material incompatibility and flagging of cementitious changes by comparing heat development against reference materials [11]. The AdiaCal calorimeter test device is easy to use and provides very good predictions for the set time of field concrete, enabling early identification of cementitious changes by comparing heat development curves against reference sets [11]. The AdiaCal semi-adiabatic calorimeter device can be modified to compute temperature losses and inexpensively replicate field semi-adiabatic testing results; when used with HIPERPAV, this equipment is recommended for predicting pavement performance characteristics including set times, strength gain, and thermal cracking risk [11]. Additionally, using 24-hour semi-adiabatic calorimetric data instead of seven-day testing requires adjusting the analysis reliability from 50% to 60% to match stress-to-strength ratio predictions, an adjustment that allows reduced testing time to approximate performance results obtained with standard seven-day testing durations [11]; notably, 24-hour calorimetric data approximated 7-day DOH when using this adjusted reliability of 60% vs. 50% [11]. Finally, semi-adiabatic calorimetry serves as an effective method for assessing potential incompatibilities between mixture ingredients before field placement [4].

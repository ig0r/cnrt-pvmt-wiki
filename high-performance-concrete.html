High-Performance Concrete

Part of: Mix Design & Specialty Mixtures is-instance-of
High-Performance Concrete is an instance of Mix Design & Specialty Mixtures.

Also known as: HPC, HPC with internal curing
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-20

High-performance concrete is a class of Portland cementitious material engineered to deliver superior mechanical strength, durability, and service life compared with conventional mixes. Its fundamental composition relies on optimized mix designs that typically feature low water-to-binder ratios, chemical admixtures such as superplasticizers, and supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash or silica fume to refine the paste chemistry [8] [11]. These ingredients work synergistically to create a dense, refined microstructure with fine capillary pores that significantly limit moisture ingress and reduce permeability to deleterious ions [3] [11]. Advanced techniques such as internal curing using pre-saturated lightweight aggregates are often employed to maintain adequate hydration without raising the water content, thereby reducing autogenous shrinkage and cracking [4] [6]. The resulting material exhibits high resistance to freeze-thaw damage, chloride penetration, and sulfate attack while maintaining sufficient workability for placement [3] [11].

Concrete Mix Design & Materials

High-performance concrete is a specialized class of Portland cement concrete engineered to deliver superior mechanical strength, durability, and workability compared with conventional mixes [8]. Achieving these enhanced properties relies on optimized mix designs that typically feature low water-to-binder ratios, chemical admixtures such as superplasticizers, and supplementary cementitious materials to refine the pore structure and reduce permeability [8] [11]. A common strategy involves internal curing, where pre-saturated lightweight or porous aggregates act as distributed water reservoirs that release moisture during hydration to maintain adequate reaction conditions without increasing the overall mix water content [4] [6]. This approach extends the hydration period, reduces autogenous shrinkage and cracking, and improves resistance to deleterious ion transport while potentially lowering cement demand [1] [4].

Research problem — The lack of reliable performance-based guidance for high-performance concrete mixes incorporating two or three supplementary cementitious materials creates significant unpredictability in key properties such as workability, set time, and durability [2]. This uncertainty regarding early-age heat evolution and strength development prevents engineers from confidently designing mixes that meet specific thermal-cracking and durability criteria, often resulting in costly trial-and-error processes and potential field failures [2].

State of the art — Current high-performance concrete practice centers on engineered ternary cementitious blends that pair Portland cement with supplementary pozzolans such as silica fume, metakaolin, fly ash, and ground-granulated blast-furnace slag to meet targeted durability and strength objectives [2]. Modern mix design has shifted from prescriptive limits toward a performance-based approach where material proportions and mixing procedures are rigorously optimized through extensive laboratory testing of dozens of combinations [2] [8]. Production standards now emphasize multi-step mixing sequences to enhance microstructure and interfacial transition zones, alongside real-time monitoring tools like microwave-based moisture sensors to ensure batch uniformity and water-cement ratio control [8].

Findings from the reports

  • Ternary blends containing supplementary cementitious materials, such as slag and fly ash, consistently delivered excellent compressive strength performance at both early and later ages [2].
  • Specific ternary combinations dramatically lowered heat evolution, achieving an adiabatic temperature rise that was approximately 75% lower than conventional Portland cement concrete [2].
  • Incorporating ground-granulated blast-furnace slag consistently reduced peak hydration temperatures and delayed the time to maximum heat generation [2].
  • Adhering to recommended water-reducer dosage rates produced significantly greater three-day compressive strengths compared to non-adherent mixes [2].
  • Mixing efficiency was maximized by selecting appropriate charge methods, limiting mixing time, and utilizing multi-step mixing with delayed admixture addition [8].
  • The addition of fly ash mitigated false-set problems associated with bassanite and improved penetration values toward ideal ranges during testing [8].
  • Ternary cementitious combinations presented no identified technical barriers for wider use in pavements, bridges, and other structures [2].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces novel instrumentation for monitoring in-situ moisture distribution during mixing and employs a vibrating slope apparatus to quantify workability, enabling real-time assessment of batch uniformity that conventional tests cannot capture [8]. A systematic data-driven screening framework utilizes ternary diagrams and regression models based on cement chemistry to predict thermal signatures and setting times, allowing engineers to forecast performance metrics before trial mixing [2]. The methodology uniquely applies a direct dry-batching protocol for superabsorbent polymers as internal-curing agents, optimizing particle size and absorption kinetics to maintain workability without the need for pre-soaking [7].

Internal Curing Strategies and Performance

Research problem — The low water-to-cement ratios characteristic of high-performance concrete create insufficient internal moisture for complete hydration, leading to autogenous shrinkage and early-age cracking that compromise long-term durability [4] [7] [10]. Implementing internal curing strategies using materials such as superabsorbent polymers or lightweight aggregates presents significant challenges in controlling water absorption and release to ensure consistent performance without negatively impacting strength [7] [10]. Premature stiffening caused by specific cement chemistries further complicates the placement of high-performance concrete, necessitating effective internal curing solutions to maintain workability and prevent construction defects [8].

The figure below illustrates how internal curing mitigates drying shrinkage and autogenous shrinkage relative to concrete age.

Drying shrinkage and autogenous versus concrete age in conventional concrete (left) and high-performance concrete (right) after internal curing [7]

The figure displays two graphs plotting shrinkage against concrete age, comparing the behavior of conventional concrete on the left with high-performance concrete on the right. In both scenarios, total shrinkage is composed of autogenous shrinkage and drying shrinkage, which begins after a specific ‘Start of drying’ point marked on the x-axis.

State of the art — Internal curing is now a standard practice for high-performance concrete, primarily achieved by replacing approximately one-fifth of the fine aggregate with saturated lightweight fine aggregate to mitigate autogenous shrinkage and enhance long-term durability [4] [6] [10]. Implementation protocols in North America mandate pre-wetting the lightweight aggregate for at least 48 hours followed by drainage to ensure uniform moisture conditions, with design guidance typically targeting specific internal water requirements relative to cementitious content [10]. Superabsorbent polymers are emerging as an alternative internal curing agent that offers precise control over water release kinetics, though their adoption is currently limited by a lack of specific DOT approval and variability in absorption behavior dependent on solution chemistry [7] [10].

Comparison of external and internal curing mechanisms in concrete specimens. [10]

Findings from the reports

  • The addition of superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) to high-performance concrete reduced 28-day compressive strength by approximately eight percent but significantly improved the degree of hydration and reduced shrinkage [7].
  • The incorporation of SAPs as an internal-curing admixture increased bound water in the paste, which enhanced cement hydration and markedly cut plastic-shrinkage cracking and early restrained shrinkage [7].
  • Internally cured concrete lowered the coefficient of thermal expansion, modulus of elasticity, and ultimate shrinkage, which mitigated the adverse impact of wider joint spacing in pavements [6].
  • Replacing approximately twenty percent of fine aggregate with lightweight material for internal curing noticeably reduced permeability and improved surface resistivity while maintaining comparable compressive and tensile strength [4].
  • Life-cycle modeling projected that the internal-curing mix extended service life by roughly twenty years relative to conventional concrete, despite live-load testing showing no measurable difference in transverse load distribution or deck strain [4].
  • High-performance concrete incorporating lightweight fine aggregates for internal curing achieved compressive strength well above design requirements and reduced beam weight by roughly five percent compared with conventional concrete [1].
  • The specification for internally cured concrete required verification through trial batches, detailed quality-control plans, and strict pre-wetting procedures for lightweight fine aggregate to ensure performance consistency [10].

Novelty & rigor — Research contributions include systematic frameworks for integrating superabsorbent polymers based on kinetic screening and bespoke dosage equations, as well as lifecycle-cost analyses demonstrating the economic viability of using lightweight fine aggregates to reduce pavement thickness [6] [7]. Methodological distinctiveness is characterized by innovative wet-on-wet two-lift placement techniques and full-scale field demonstrations that validate durability benefits through comprehensive laboratory and live-load testing protocols [4] [9]. Reproducibility is ensured through rigorous standardization of pre-wetting durations, precise saturated-surface-dry moisture accounting, and mandatory on-site verification involving supplier representatives during trial batches [10].

Implications — Designers should specify internal curing agents, such as superabsorbent polymers or saturated lightweight aggregates, to mitigate early-age shrinkage cracking and enhance long-term durability in high-performance concrete mixes [4] [6] [7]. Mix proportions must be carefully balanced with water-reducing admixtures and supplementary cementitious materials to offset potential reductions in compressive strength and increases in permeability associated with internal curing additives [4] [7]. The adoption of internally cured concrete enables structural optimizations like reduced slab thickness or lighter beam weights, which can lower material costs and facilitate faster construction while extending service life despite higher initial unit prices [1] [4] [6].

Limitations — The use of super-absorbent polymers can compromise compressive strength and increase permeability, with effectiveness heavily dependent on specific mix variables that require project-specific optimization [7]. Economic assessments often underestimate the true value of internal curing by excluding benefits related to durability improvements such as reduced shrinkage and freeze-thaw resistance [6]. Predictions regarding extended service life rely on modeling rather than long-term field verification, limiting confidence in high-performance claims for lightweight aggregate applications [4].

Durability Criteria and Material Modifications

High-performance concrete achieves enhanced durability primarily through a dense microstructure created by low water-to-binder ratios and the incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials [3]. This refined pore structure significantly reduces permeability and limits freezable water, thereby providing inherent resistance to freeze-thaw damage even without air entrainment [3]. When additional protection is required, the optimization of an air-void system with specific spacing characteristics further improves performance against freezing and thawing cycles [3].

The relationship between these microstructural properties and overall durability is illustrated in the figure below.

Durability factor as a function of porosity [3]

The figure plots the Durability Factor (DF, %) against Porosity (n, %), demonstrating that concrete durability increases significantly as porosity decreases. Concrete without air entrainment (AEA) exhibits a steep decline in durability with increasing porosity, whereas mixes containing AEA maintain high durability factors across the entire range of porosities tested.

Research problem — Current bridge-deck overlay practices require extended wet curing periods that cause significant traffic delays, creating a need for rapid-strength materials that can achieve early load-bearing capacity without inducing shrinkage cracking or compromising long-term durability [11]. There is uncertainty regarding whether high-performance concrete with very low water-to-binder ratios requires air-entraining admixtures to ensure freeze-thaw resistance, as adding these agents may reduce strength while omitting them risks premature frost damage [3]. High-performance concrete pavements can experience premature joint deterioration due to secondary ettringite growth that fills protective air voids, thereby destroying the durability system and accelerating degradation despite meeting initial freeze-thaw specifications [5].

State of the art — Current durability criteria for high-performance concrete blend traditional air-void specifications with a growing consensus that ultra-dense, low-permeability mixes can achieve freeze-thaw resistance without added air entrainment [3]. Material modifications increasingly utilize supplementary cementitious materials and very low water-to-binder ratios to create dense matrices, with emerging thresholds suggesting that air entrainment may be omitted for specific blended cement systems below w/b ≈ 0.26 [3]. For rapid-deployment overlays, durability is managed through meticulous surface preparation and the use of shrinkage-reducing admixtures to mitigate early-age cracking in high-heat latex-modified systems [11].

Findings from the reports

  • High-performance concrete without air entrainment met freeze-thaw durability criteria only under extremely restrictive mix conditions requiring very low water-to-binder ratios and high compressive strengths [3].
  • A latex-modified overlay on a high-performance concrete substrate demonstrated strong bonding that exceeded minimum thresholds for thin epoxy overlays [11].
  • The composite system of high-performance concrete and a latex-modified overlay exhibited rapid early strength development, permitting traffic reopening within hours [11].
  • High-performance concrete substrates exhibited lower chloride ingress than the applied latex-modified overlays during salt-ponding exposure tests [11].
  • The durability factor of high-performance concrete consistently exceeded 85% when air content was maintained at or above 6% with appropriate spacing factors, regardless of water-to-binder ratio or cement type [3].
  • Microscopic examination of high-performance concrete cores confirmed the presence of well-hydrated alite cement particles and abundant spherical fly-ash pozzolan particles, indicating typical supplementary cementitious material usage [5].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a very-early-strength latex-modified concrete overlay that utilizes a rapid-set calcium sulfoaluminate cement blended with polymer latex and citric acid to achieve high flowability and early strength gain without extensive formwork or curing delays [11]. A novel field measurement technique employing a saturated cloth between Wenner probe electrodes is established to stabilize surface resistivity readings, providing reliable durability data that overcomes the limitations of standard testing methods [11]. The study presents the first quantitative framework specifying when low-permeability high-performance concrete can dispense with air-entraining admixtures while still satisfying freeze-thaw durability criteria through empirical limits on water-to-binder ratios and strength thresholds [3]. This approach uniquely links detailed microstructural measurements of the air-void structure to freeze-thaw performance, deriving power-law expressions that enable prediction of durability solely from rapid chloride permeability, porosity, and water-to-binder data [3]. The investigation reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism where secondary ettringite precipitates fill fine entrained air voids adjacent to joints, destroying freeze-thaw protection and explaining premature joint failure in high-performance pavements [5]. A novel investigative protocol integrates an in-situ base-permeability testing device with laboratory experiments that vary mix parameters to demonstrate how deicing salts alter solution properties, thereby increasing concrete saturation and accelerating damage [5].

Implications — Designers must incorporate additional pore-refinement measures or surface treatments to mitigate freeze-thaw and sulfate attack, as the material’s inherent moisture uptake characteristics can compromise durability without these interventions [11]. Reliable freeze-thaw performance in practical field mixes requires the inclusion of an adequate air-void system rather than relying solely on low permeability or high compressive strength, which are often impractical to achieve consistently [3]. Selection of this concrete type must balance durability enhancements against economic considerations, applying moisture-resistant refinements where durability is paramount while reserving alternative overlays for high-volume corridors requiring rapid opening [11].

Air entrained concrete may have a relatively high chloride ion permeability but maintain high F-T durability.

Limitations — Durability data for latex-modified cementitious overlays remain sparse, with laboratory specimens exhibiting poor freeze-thaw resistance and unexplained early failure mechanisms [11]. Laboratory bond-strength results may be optimistic due to substrate preparation conditions that differ from field practice, while surface-resistivity measurements show seasonal instability and sorptivity trends suggest increasing water absorption at later ages [11]. The lowest practical water-to-binder ratios tested are higher than those achievable in typical field paving, making it difficult to consistently realize the durability benefits of air entrainment in such dry mixes [3]. Low permeability alone does not guarantee freeze-thaw durability without very high compressive strength, and empirical relationships derived for non-air-entrained mixes do not apply to air-entrained concretes [3].

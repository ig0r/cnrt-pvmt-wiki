Equivalent Temperature Difference

Part of: Curling and Warping
equivalent-temperaturepavement-analysispmedtemperature-gradient
Also known as: effective temperature difference, ΔT_eff
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

A unified measure expressing all slab deformation impacts—including the pavement slab’s original shape, settlement, and environmental effects—as an equivalent linear temperature difference across the slab depth. Used in PMED to quantify the combined effects of permanent curling/warping, hourly temperature variations, and shrinkage-induced warping. [1]

Components of Total Temperature Difference

The total equivalent temperature difference, which consists of both permanent and transient components [1], is calculated as the temperature at the top of the slab minus the temperature at the bottom of the slab [1]. This total difference is defined by the equation ΔT = ΔT_Permanent + ΔT_Hourly + ΔT_Shrinkage. The permanent components can be summed to form a single quantity, expressed as an effective linear temperature difference throughout a PCC slab [1]. Specifically, ΔT_Permanent represents the effective permanent curling/warping temperature difference locked into the pavement at zero temperature and moisture gradient, with a default value in PMED of -10°F based on national calibration.

The transient effects are based on hourly climatic data [1], specifically through ΔT_Hourly, which is the transient hourly temperature difference calculated by the Enhanced Integrated Climate Model (EICM) using 11 uniformly spaced temperature points through the slab. Additionally, the total includes ΔT_Shrinkage, representing the monthly equivalent temperature difference from reversible shrinkage induced by atmospheric relative humidity variations [1].

Typical temperature variation profile throughout a slab [1]

Directional Effects: Positive vs. Negative ΔT

The effective temperature difference at midday is always positive, whereas values in the morning and afternoon are always the opposite [1]. Pavement temperature generally exceeds ambient temperature and follows a similar pattern with a one- to two-hour lag, which influences the timing of positive versus negative temperature gradients throughout diurnal cycles [2]. A positive equivalent temperature difference indicates that the equivalent temperature at the top of the slab is more significant than at the bottom, while a negative number indicates the inverse [1]. Specifically, a positive ΔT means the top of the slab is warmer/moister than the bottom, leading to downward curling; the slab curls downwards as the equivalent temperature difference increases from zero to a positive number [1]. Conversely, a negative ΔT occurs when the bottom of the slab is warmer/moister than the top, causing upward curling [1]. This negative equivalent temperature difference decreases to a negative value when the drying shrinkage of the concrete occurs mainly near the top of the slab, indicating a higher equivalent temperature at the bottom of the slab than at the top [1]. As concrete ages, the equivalent temperature difference drops to more negative values, indicating an increased tendency for upward curling [1]. Loss of contact between the slab and subgrade occurs at the center during downward curling and at the corners during upward curling, which increases stresses and aggravates pavement deterioration through repeated loading [3].

Temp. at top > Temp. at bottom (during setting time) [2]

Modeling Methodologies and Theoretical Approaches

Finite element simulations using ISLAB 2000 and EverFE 2.24 demonstrate that the relationship between actual measured temperature difference and equivalent temperature difference is linear, allowing for back-estimation of equivalent values to match measured slab curvature profiles [2]. To avoid issues regarding the most appropriate input for curling analysis, the effective temperature differential is defined as the specific temperature difference that produces fatigue damage equivalent to the accumulated pavement fatigue damage induced by daily curling changes [3]. This effective built-in temperature difference (EBITD) concept integrates permanent curling from concrete setting with diurnal curling during service life, accounting for factors such as differential shrinkage, creep, wind, and moisture content that make precise estimation difficult [3]. Furthermore, in comparative studies evaluating internally cured concrete, an effective temperature gradient of -10°F is commonly assumed to simultaneously simulate the combined effects of curling and warping on pavements [4].

Regarding predictive modeling, Dempsey was among the first to use one-dimensional heat transfer models and explicit finite-difference approaches to formulate numerical simulation methods for pavement temperature prediction [1]. Theoretical models like the Climate-Material-Structure (CMS) program use one-dimensional forward finite-difference heat transfer analysis to estimate pavement temperatures based on atmospheric conditions such as air temperature, precipitation, and radiation [1]. The CMS model requires input in the form of pavement material parameters and climate data such as air temperature, precipitation, radiation, and so on [1]. While the theoretical approach is widely known for its time-consuming solution process and the high number of required inputs, it has been incorporated into the Enhanced Integrated Climate Model (EICM) [1].

Climate Modeling via EICM

To estimate pavement surface temperature and humidity conditions, the climate model uses hourly ambient climate data from meteorological stations across the United States [1].

Regression Coefficient Analysis

In these models, the coefficient of the linear regression equation relating measured to equivalent temperature difference is less than unity, which can be related to the transient component (temperature and moisture gradient variation), while the intercept corresponds to the permanent component (unrecoverable shrinkage, construction temperature conditions, and creep) [2].

Impact of Moisture Gradients and Shrinkage

For transient components, moisture impacts are based on monthly changes in atmospheric relative humidity [1], while temperature effects rely on hourly climatic data [1]. Changes in relative humidity can impact transient moisture shrinkage on the PCC slab’s top surface, influencing the overall equivalent temperature difference [1]. Typically, the top surface of a slab loses moisture to the air while the bottom remains in contact with soil or base layers, creating a negative moisture gradient where the top shrinks relative to the bottom [1]. This negative moisture gradient, characterized by lower moisture content at the top than at the bottom, results in slab warping [1]. Moisture gradients through the concrete slab depth cause warping and reversible contraction, with seasonal changes potentially having a greater impact than daily differences due to the limited hydraulic conductivity of concrete [1]. Furthermore, wetting of the slab bottom can significantly increase the equivalent temperature gradient, which consequently increases warping deformations in concrete pavements [3].

Note: the degree of curling and warping were measured using strain gages installed in the longitudinal direction at mid-panel. McCracken et al. 2008 Figure 7. Seasonal contributions to the degree of curling and warping in restrained and unrestrained slabs [3]

To assess slab deformation, moisture gradients can be converted into equivalent temperature gradients, allowing researchers to evaluate warping effects using thermal models [1]. Grasley et al. developed a moisture gradient model based on diffusion theory and drying stress gradient models that uses internal relative humidity readings directly related to stress development [1]. Instrumentation using Hygrochron iButtons® installed in PVC housings with vent holes reveals that moisture variations at the top of the slab are more sensitive to ambient moisture changes than those at the middle of the slab [2]. Additionally, shrinkage-reducing admixtures can reduce early-age shrinkage by approximately 30% to 40%, thereby decreasing the potential for early age cracking and influencing the permanent component of the equivalent temperature difference [3].

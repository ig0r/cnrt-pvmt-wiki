Fiber-Reinforced Concrete

Part of: Concrete Materials & Mixtures is-a-type-of
Fiber-Reinforced Concrete is a type of concrete mixture within the Concrete Materials & Mixtures domain.
concretefiber-reinforcementmaterial-properties
Also known as: FRC
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-19T20:01:32Z

Fiber-reinforced concrete is a cementitious composite material in which discrete, discontinuous fibers are uniformly dispersed throughout the matrix to modify its mechanical behavior and enhance durability. The primary mechanism of reinforcement involves the fibers bridging developing cracks within the hardened concrete, thereby limiting crack opening widths and preventing microcracks from propagating into larger pathways for aggressive agents [1] [2] [3]. By arresting crack propagation, the fiber network significantly improves the composite’s post-cracking toughness, ductility, and fatigue resistance while also mitigating early-age shrinkage cracking [1] [2] [3]. The specific performance characteristics of the composite are determined by the material properties and geometry of the fibers, which can be selected to provide benefits ranging from corrosion resistance to enhanced electrical conductivity through percolation networks [1] [2] [3] [4].

Fiber-Reinforced Concrete unifies diverse materials that introduce discontinuous reinforcement to improve the mechanical behavior of cementitious matrices. Alkali-Resistant Glass Fibers provide distributed tensile reinforcement within the cementitious matrix by using a zirconium dioxide-modified silica glass composition that withstands alkaline degradation, thereby enhancing post-cracking ductility and fracture resistance without requiring continuous rebar. Polyvinyl Alcohol Fibers enhance post-cracking ductility and fracture resistance by bridging microcracks within the cementitious matrix through their high tensile strength and strong bond with the alkaline environment. Synthetic Fiber Reinforcement integrates discrete polymeric fibers into the cementitious matrix to enhance post-cracking ductility and fracture resistance through distributed tensile reinforcement. Polypropylene Microfibers serve as discrete, randomly distributed synthetic filaments within the cementitious matrix to mitigate early-age plastic shrinkage cracking and enhance the composite’s toughness through improved energy absorption. Hybrid Fiber-Reinforced Concrete achieves synergistic mechanical performance by integrating two or more distinct types of discontinuous fibers within its cementitious matrix.

Key findings

  • Synthesized hybrid mixes combining polypropylene microfibers with alkali-resistant glass macrofibers produced the best balance of pre- and post-crack performance, delivering superior compressive strength, flexural capacity, and toughness compared to single-fiber systems [1].
  • Fiber reinforcement significantly reduced cracking rates in concrete overlays and enabled extended transverse joint spacings up to 40 feet by preventing mid-slab transverse cracking, whereas non-fibrous mixes suffered high distress levels at similar spacing [5].
  • Fiber reinforcement did not produce measurable improvements in key structural performance metrics such as joint load transfer efficiency, ride quality (IRI), or deformation characteristics, indicating that joint geometry and support conditions remain the dominant design factors [5].
  • Polypropylene microfibers excelled at early-age crack control and shrinkage mitigation, while macrofibers dominated post-crack ductility and macro-crack bridging, necessitating a hybrid approach to address both failure stages effectively [1].
  • Electrically conductive concrete required precise production controls, including staged fiber addition and batch size limitations, to prevent clumping and maintain electrical resistance within design limits, with an optimal dosage identified at approximately 0.40 vol% [4].
  • Substituting Portland cement with Class F fly ash improved plastic-shrinkage resistance, workability, and long-term durability in fiber-reinforced mixes, whereas expansive cement was deemed unsuitable due to increased capillary pressure outweighing crack-width reduction benefits [1].
  • Fibers restricted crack width and produced thinner reflective cracks, offering secondary benefits such as improved fatigue capacity and ductility, even when they carried little load until large strains developed due to their low modulus relative to the matrix [3].

Specialized Applications & Systems

Evidence: 9 reports (7 primary)

Specialized applications of fiber-reinforced concrete extend beyond standard structural reinforcement to exploit the material’s inherent electrical properties when conductive fibers are incorporated. These functional systems rely on a percolating network or tunneling effect within the cement matrix to enable current flow, facilitating uses such as electrically heated pavements for snow and ice management [4].

Research problem — The industry lacks comprehensive long-term performance data and clear design guidance for integrating structural synthetic fibers into thin, unbonded concrete overlays, preventing reliable prediction of service life and optimization of joint spacing [2]. Engineers face uncertainty regarding the actual field validation of assumed benefits such as enhanced fatigue life and load transfer, creating a gap in justifying added material costs against tangible durability gains for agencies [5]. Specialized applications like electrically heated pavements require precise control over fiber degradation and crack-related voltage to maintain designed electrical resistance, ensuring public safety and energy efficiency during service life [4].

State of the art — Fiber-reinforced concrete has transitioned from a niche material to a mainstream solution for thin pavement overlays, where structural synthetic macrofibers are primarily utilized as a crack-mitigation measure rather than a means to improve load transfer or ride quality [5].

Findings from the reports

  • Synthetic fiber reinforcement did not produce measurable improvements in load-transfer efficiency, ride quality (IRI), or joint activation rates compared to plain concrete controls [5] [3] [6].
  • The structural contribution of fibers was limited in thin overlays, with field data showing no significant effect on effective thickness or deformation characteristics despite minor increases in back-calculated stiffness [5] [3].
  • Identified that fiber reinforcement serves as a critical safeguard for thin overlays (4 inches or less), providing insurance against material loss and multiple cracking in the event of slab support failure [7] [6].
  • Fiber inclusion significantly reduced transverse and reflective cracking, allowing for extended joint spacings up to 40 feet without inducing mid-panel cracks [5].
  • Fiber reinforcement served as a critical safeguard for overlay depths of four inches or less, providing insurance against material loss and multiple cracking during slab support failures [6].
  • Durability parameters such as permeability and mix density were controlled primarily by cement content and compaction effort rather than fiber addition [8].
  • Short carbon fibers enabled the production of electrically conductive concrete suitable for heated pavements, provided precise dosage control and two-stage mixing were employed to reach the percolation threshold [4].
  • Fiber reinforcement did not alter early-age smoothness or curling/warping behavior in field-evaluated overlay sections [5].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a comprehensive field-based methodology that integrates large-scale factorial testing with advanced diagnostic tools, such as ultrasonic shear-wave tomography and falling-weight deflectometer analysis, to evaluate the structural performance of thin concrete overlays under diverse traffic and environmental conditions [5] [3]. A significant methodological innovation is the development of a validated three-dimensional finite-element modeling framework that explicitly simulates layer bonding, joint spacing, and interface behaviors using contact elements rather than simplified spring stiffnesses [9]. The study establishes a novel production-to-field workflow for electrically heated pavements by defining a staged mixing protocol for carbon fibers that ensures uniform conductive network formation and includes a fresh-stage electrical resistance test to predict long-term resistivity [4].

Across the reviewed reports, fiber reinforcement in concrete overlays is consistently shown to provide negligible improvements in primary structural metrics such as load transfer efficiency, ride quality, and joint activation rates compared to plain concrete controls. Conversely, the inclusion of fibers delivers a distinct advantage in crack mitigation by significantly reducing both the frequency and severity of transverse and reflective cracking, thereby enabling extended joint spacings that would otherwise induce mid-panel failure. The functional utility of fibers in overlay systems is further contextualized by their conditional necessity based on thickness, where they serve as a critical safeguard for thin sections to prevent material loss while remaining optional for thicker pavements. [5] [3] [7] [6]

Implications — Designers should treat fiber reinforcement as a secondary measure for thin overlays, reserving it primarily for crack mitigation and durability in sections where conventional concrete cannot meet performance targets without excessive thickness or joint density [5] [3] [6]. For specialized systems such as electrically heated pavements, mix design and construction protocols must be driven by electrical resistance requirements rather than mechanical strength alone, necessitating strict controls on fiber dosage and batch size to ensure uniform dispersion and long-term conductivity [4].

Limitations — The practical benefits of fiber reinforcement in thin overlays are restricted to specific thickness ranges, with no measurable performance advantage or clear justification for added cost observed in thicker sections [7]. Current field data and analytical models lack the robustness to definitively assess long-term efficacy, as studies are often limited by small sample sizes, early service periods, low fiber dosages, and an inability to predict performance for unusual joint spacing configurations [5] [3]. Deployment in electrically heated systems is hindered by inconsistent electrical resistance due to fiber degradation during mixing, the absence of specific regulatory criteria, and strict handling constraints required to prevent poor material distribution [4].

Hybridization Strategies and Mix Optimization

Evidence: 2 reports (1 primary)

Hybridization strategies involve combining distinct fiber types, such as micro- and macro-fibers or different material classes, to achieve synergistic mechanical performance that addresses the trade-offs between early-age shrinkage control and post-cracking toughness [1] [3]. Mix optimization is required because increasing fiber content generally raises the demand for water-reducing admixtures to maintain workability, reflecting a fundamental balance between reinforcement benefits and mix design requirements [1]. The effectiveness of these hybrid systems depends on selecting fibers with complementary properties, such as varying modulus of elasticity or bond mechanisms, to ensure continuous crack bridging from micro-crack initiation through macro-crack propagation [1] [3].

Research problem — Researchers seek to determine how hybridization strategies can effectively mitigate early-age shrinkage cracking in bridge deck concrete, where rapid water loss creates capillary tension that exceeds the tensile strength of fresh cement paste [1]. A key challenge is optimizing mix designs to leverage fiber crack-bridging behavior for reducing crack width and propagation, thereby addressing durability threats such as chloride ingress and reinforcement corrosion without relying on the brittle performance of conventional concrete [1].

State of the art — Current design practice treats fiber-reinforced concrete as an engineered composite where hybrid blends of high-aspect-ratio microfibers and longer macrofibers are dosed to synergistically balance early-age shrinkage control with post-cracking ductility [1]. To maintain acceptable workability while achieving mechanical performance, designers typically limit polymer microfiber content to approximately 0.125% by volume and supplement the binder with pozzolanic materials such as fly ash [1]. The field relies on standardized testing protocols like ASTM C1609 and C1579 to quantify trade-offs, while ongoing optimization efforts focus on multi-scale fiber geometry and mix sequencing to enhance post-peak toughness without compromising cost or workability [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Demonstrated that fiber-reinforced concrete effectively addresses early-age shrinkage cracking, chloride ingress durability, and post-cracking toughness requirements in bridge deck applications [1].
  • Established that the tensile capacity of fiber-reinforced concrete relies on a random, discontinuous fiber network where performance is dictated by fiber geometry, matrix stiffness relative to fibers, and bond chemistry [1].
  • Observed that microfibers excel at early-age crack control and shrinkage mitigation, whereas macrofibers dominate post-crack ductility and macro-crack bridging, necessitating a hybrid approach for optimal performance [1].
  • Rejected the use of Type K expansive cement for final mixes because its increase in capillary pressure outweighed any reduction in early-age tensile strain [1].
  • Found that polypropylene microfibers raised both tensile and compressive strengths while lowering cracking potential and chloride ingress, but higher dosages caused diminishing returns and increased water demand [1].
  • Recommended a practical dosage limit of approximately 0.125% by volume for polypropylene microfibers to balance mechanical benefits with workability constraints [1].
  • Showed that hybrid mixes combining alkali-resistant glass macrofibers with polypropylene microfibers delivered the best combination of pre-peak compressive strength, flexural performance, and post-peak toughness [1].
  • Reported that polyvinyl alcohol fibers reduced workability and that both polypropylene and polyvinyl alcohol macrofibers lowered compressive strength relative to the control mix [1].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a systematic three-stage experimental framework that simultaneously optimizes binder chemistry, fiber type selection, and dosage to address early-age shrinkage cracking while maintaining workability and durability in bridge-deck applications [1]. Methodological novelty is achieved through the integration of non-intrusive digital image correlation with chalk-based speckle patterns and continuous capillary-pressure sensing, enabling comprehensive strain mapping without impeding natural evaporation rates during restrained slab testing [1]. Reproducibility is ensured by adhering to a detailed protocol that specifies precise mixing sequences, casting geometries with modified restraint conditions, and standardized mechanical and durability tests linked directly to the sensor data for dosage-response assessment [1].

Shrinkage Control and Crack Mitigation

Evidence: 1 report (1 primary)

Research problem — The research addresses the persistent challenge of restrained shrinkage-induced cracking in fiber-reinforced concrete, which can severely undermine the durability and service life of pavements and other structures [11]. It seeks to determine why similar cracking-potential ratios yield different outcomes, specifically investigating how fiber reinforcement enables certain mixes to avoid cracking where others fail [11].

Findings from the reports

  • Fiber reinforcement prevented cracking in a concrete mix that exhibited high cracking potential, whereas an unreinforced counterpart cracked after approximately seven days [11].

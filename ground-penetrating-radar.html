A nondestructive testing (NDT) method that uses electromagnetic radio waves to detect subsurface features. A transmitter emits pulses of radio energy into the structure; a receiver collects waves reflected from material boundaries with different dielectric properties. [1]

Illustration of Directing NDT at Different Levels of Investigation [1]

Principles of GPR

GPR is analogous to stress-wave propagation techniques but substitutes electromagnetic waves for mechanical stress waves. The receiver-transmitter assembly is called an antenna, which can be ground-coupled or air-launched. Systems with air-launched antennas can collect data at highway speeds (50-60 mph). GPR signal interpretation relies on detecting differences in dielectric constant between materials — hyperbolic signatures indicate cylindrical objects (e.g., rebar). [1][2]

Slab containing crossed reinforcing bars (3/8-inch nominal diameter) [2]

GPR Equipment and Survey Methods

GPR data can be collected using both linear and three-dimensional survey procedures [2]. Various systems are utilized for these tasks, such as the Sensors & Software pulseEKKO 1000A, which employs 900 and 1,200 MHz ground-coupled antennas at a ~5 mph towing speed using Win EKKO Pro PC/EKKO 3D/EKKO Mapper software; this system utilizes a wheel odometer to automatically trigger the antenna during data collection [2]. The GSSI SIR-10 A+ system uses 400, 900, and 1,500 MHz ground-coupled antennas and is capable of collecting high-resolution data at highway speeds ranging from 55 to 60 mph [2].

Three-dimensional visualization of the GPR survey (note: rebar is colored red) [2]

Alternatively, the trailer-mounted GSSI SIR-20 features dual-channel, 900 and 1,500 MHz antennas and uses RADAN 5 software. However, FCC emission regulations for ultra-wideband transmitters restrict the maximum data collection speed of the GSSI SIR-20 system to approximately 10–15 mph when acquiring high-resolution data [2].

Applications in Concrete Pavement Detection

Ground penetrating radar (GPR) is utilized for the detection of subsurface voids, delaminations, and moisture gradients, as well as the measurement of pavement layer thickness (standardized under ASTM D 4748). It has been applied to detect voids and locate rebars in concrete, though further development is required to produce automated data evaluation for determining pavement thickness [3]. The technology enables the identification of aggregate-related distress (D-cracking) via phase change signals at the concrete-subgrade interface, and GPR surveys have successfully identified aggregate-induced distress, such as frost attack or durability cracking, even when surface symptoms like cracking and staining were not evident [2]. Furthermore, laboratory experiments on mortar and concrete specimens were conducted to evaluate GPR effectiveness in locating alkali-silica reaction (ASR)-related cracking [2], including the detection of moisture gradients resulting from ASR expansion (laboratory demonstration, Phase 2).

Mortar bar expansion versus time for ASR cracking experiments [2]

At a broader scale, GPR is used for network-level pavement evaluation in Florida, Louisiana, Missouri, and Texas. It can also verify the effectiveness of edge drains in preventing moisture accumulation beneath rubblized concrete pavements, as observed in Colorado DOT investigations where no distresses or settlement were found after six years [4].

Amount of cracking on the LTPP SPS-6 rubblized test sections [4]

Structural and Joint Evaluation

During construction, GPR is utilized to verify the correct placement of dowels within contraction joints, ensuring alignment with design specifications [5]. In a South Dakota DOT study on an overlay project, GPR surveys revealed that approximately 18% of transverse joints over a 2,000-foot section had misplaced dowel bars, representing a defect rate not detectable through visual inspection during paving [6].

– SD Route 50 Dowel Bar Configuration (note omitted dowels between the wheel paths) [6]

Additionally, joint load transfer efficiency is calculated by comparing deflections measured under the loading plate on the loaded slab against deflections of the unloaded slab recorded by a sensor positioned approximately 12 inches from the plate center [7].

Integration with FWD Testing

The combined use of FWD and GPR testing is effective for evaluating pavement condition, as GPR provides in-depth information on sublayer thickness, subgrade structure, depth to bedrock, and detection of voids or irregularities under the pavement [8]. To improve backcalculation accuracy for rigid pavements, GPR readings can be taken along test sections to determine PCC layer thickness at FWD test points more precisely, as predicted elastic moduli are highly sensitive to assumed slab thickness [8]. In rubblized concrete pavement evaluations, GPR data combined with Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) results can confirm effective drainage and structural integrity, as demonstrated in Louisiana projects where the rubblized base exhibited a modulus value over ten times higher than traditional flexible bases [4]. Furthermore, FWD deflection measurements taken in a dense grid pattern across pavement panels, potentially combined with ground penetrating radar scanning, can assist in assessing loss of support conditions [7].

Experimental plan developed for field testing and sampling [7]

Specific applications of Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing include collecting data at pavement joints to enable the determination of joint load transfer efficiency (LTE) and subsurface voids through analysis of ‘zero’ load intercept values [7], as well as performing tests at the center of concrete pavement panels to determine modulus of subgrade reaction (k) values and associated intercept parameters [7]. Mechanically, FWD tests utilize a loading plate with an 11.81-inch diameter to apply seating and four sequential loading drops, with applied forces typically ranging from approximately 5,000 to 15,000 lbs [7]. For example, the Iowa DOT’s JILS-20 FWD equipment applies a step loading sequence of 6,000, 9,000, 12,000, and 15,000 lbs to measure pavement deflection at three distinct locations (start, middle, and end point) within each test section [4].

Picture of Iowa DOT’s JILS-20 FWD equipment [4]

Limitations and Technical Constraints

Ground penetrating radar (GPR) utilizes an antenna that creates an irradiated region shaped like a cone directly below the device, though closer antenna coupling improves signal at the risk of hitting objects. The effectiveness of detection is influenced by vehicle speed, as slower speeds enhance detection ability, and deployment is constrained by FCC regulations on ultra-wideband technology. Because MRD typically produces only subtle GPR features since it manifests as moisture content differences, an experienced operator is required for equipment operation and result interpretation; indeed, GPR data interpretation is currently constrained by a reliance on expert knowledge, similar to the limitations of X-ray diffraction in the mid-20th century, though software-driven visualization and three-dimensional imaging are expected to reduce this dependency [1].

High-Resolution GPR Illustrating the Periodic Signal from the Base of US 63 [1]

Technical limitations include the fact that cracks are not easily detected unless moisture is present, and the presence of wet concrete can introduce variability into GPR results due to the additional factor of moisture affecting signal propagation [3]. This phenomenon, known as moisture masking, involves the presence of water in severely cracked concrete obscuring the GPR signal, which was the most plausible explanation for the P 73 failure (Phase 2 finding); consequently, GPR may not detect paste-related premature distress, as demonstrated at the P 73 site. Furthermore, ASR-induced uniform expansion may not be measurable with GPR (Phase 2 finding).

Photo from eddy current test of wet concrete [3]

Emerging Technologies

Phase-measurement laser radar (Ladar) technology has been integrated into pavement profile scanners to collect transverse rutting, longitudinal faulting, and distress profiles at highway speeds [1]. Additionally, neural networks have been applied to automate the interpretation of GPR data, specifically through feedforward models using backpropagation algorithms that are capable of identifying different surface-base interfaces in composite pavements without destructive coring [8].

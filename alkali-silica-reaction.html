Alkali-Silica Reaction

Part of: Materials-Related Distress (MRD) is-materials-related-distress-of
Alkali-Silica Reaction is a materials-related distress of Materials-Related Distress.
asrconcretedistressdurability
Also known as: ASR, alkali-aggregate reaction
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-22

Alkali-silica reaction is a deleterious chemical process in concrete where alkalis released from cementitious materials react with reactive silica minerals present in certain aggregates. This interaction produces an expansive gel that absorbs water, swells, and generates internal pressure within the concrete matrix [4] [17]. The resulting expansion creates internal stresses that cause cracking of both the aggregate particles and the surrounding cement paste, leading to a loss of structural integrity and durability [2] [6]. The reaction requires the simultaneous presence of an alkaline environment, reactive aggregates, and moisture, with its progression often accelerated by high humidity conditions [4] [6] [17].

Key findings

  • Substituting ordinary Portland cement with Class C fly ash lowered the risk of alkali-silica reaction despite high total alkali content, due to low soluble-alkali fraction and reduced concrete permeability [13].
  • Ground-penetrating radar failed to reliably detect alkali-silica reaction damage in laboratory slabs with reactive aggregate, as uniform expansion produced inconclusive signals [17].
  • Alkali-silica reaction was present in all tested pavement cores, with chert identified as the primary reactive constituent causing moderate-to-severe cracking and gel deposits [4].
  • Ternary pozzolan blends proved capable of controlling alkali-silica reaction at lower replacement levels than mandated by current specifications, whereas Class C fly ash required high dosages or pairing with other pozzolans [11].
  • Microcracking was observed around reactive shale fine aggregate particles but did not drive joint failure, while coarse aggregate showed no evidence of participation in distress [5].

Mitigation Strategies Using Supplementary Cementitious Materials and Recycled Aggregates

Research problem — Standard control mixes frequently exceed allowable expansion limits, creating a need for supplementary cementitious materials that can effectively suppress alkali-silica reaction swelling to ensure long-term durability and reduce maintenance costs [11]. Identifying optimal ternary blends and low-alkali supplementary materials is essential for mitigating deleterious expansion in infrastructure, yet the specific performance of these combinations under aggressive testing regimes requires further evaluation to guarantee structural integrity [14]. The use of recycled concrete aggregates that have previously exhibited alkali-silica reaction poses a significant barrier to sustainable construction, as these materials can transmit distress to new pavement mixes and hinder compliance with agency specifications [18].

The figure below illustrates the ASTM C1012 sulfate mortar expansions observed in mixtures containing silica fume.

ASTM C1012 sulfate mortar expansions of mixtures containing silica fume [11]

The figure displays a line chart plotting the percentage of mortar expansion over time for various concrete mixture formulations. The chart demonstrates how specific mixtures containing silica fume mitigate expansive behavior compared to standard controls, which is the primary mechanism for preventing the deleterious chemical process of ASR.

State of the art — Current practice mitigates alkali-silica reaction primarily by incorporating supplementary cementitious materials such as Class F fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, and silica fume to lower permeability and bind excess alkalis [4] [6] [13]. Recent advances focus on optimizing ternary blends of multiple supplementary cementitious materials to overcome the limitations of single-material approaches and meet performance-based durability requirements [1] [14].

Findings from the reports

  • Found that Class C fly ash provided inconsistent mitigation, with only about half of tested mixes remaining under the 0.10% expansion threshold and requiring higher replacement levels or combination with other pozzolans for reliable control [11] [12].
  • Revealed that adding silica fume or metakaolin enhanced the mitigation effect of ternary blends, allowing for effective ASR resistance at lower total SCM replacement levels than typically mandated by specifications [11] [12].
  • Demonstrated that supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as Class F fly ash, ground-granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS), silica fume, and metakaolin consistently suppressed alkali-silica reaction (ASR) expansion to low levels [11].
  • Observed that control mixtures lacking sufficient SCMs exhibited high ASR expansions, with nine of twelve tested mixes exceeding the 0.10% limit and limestone-blended cement reaching approximately 0.62% [11].
  • Showed that increasing fly ash replacement levels reduced ASR expansion, whereas low dosages of Class C fly ash combined with Class F fly ash failed to provide effective mitigation [12].
  • Reported that current specification assumptions regarding the independent action of SCMs were contradicted by test results, where many mixes passed accelerated testing criteria but failed stricter performance limits [11].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a distinctive approach by deliberately engineering ternary blends of supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash, slag, and silica fume, to synergistically suppress alkali-silica reaction expansion rather than relying on single additives [1] [4] [11]. Methodological rigor is established through the integration of accelerated testing protocols with large-scale experimental matrices and systematic chemical characterization to generate predictive tools for rational blend selection [1] [11] [14]. Reproducibility is ensured by standardizing raw material specifications and employing defined testing procedures that allow for the verification of mitigation performance under controlled laboratory conditions [1] [4] [14].

Across the reviewed reports, supplementary cementitious materials such as Class F fly ash, ground-granulated blast-furnace slag, silica fume, and metakaolin consistently suppress alkali-silica reaction expansion in ternary blends, whereas Class C fly ash requires significantly higher replacement levels or synergistic combinations to achieve comparable mitigation. While control mixtures lacking sufficient supplementary cementitious materials frequently exceed standard expansion limits, properly proportioned ternary systems utilizing these effective pozzolans reliably limit expansion to within acceptable thresholds. Implications — Designers should specify ternary pozzolan systems rather than relying on conventional binary blends or low-alkali cement alone, as synergistic combinations of supplementary cementitious materials provide superior ASR control in aggressive environments [11]. Specifications must mandate minimum thresholds for reactive SCMs, such as substantial slag content or the inclusion of silica fume and metakaolin, because low dosages of Class C fly ash are insufficient to mitigate expansion [12]. Current standards require revision to recognize the synergistic effects of blended cement systems, moving away from treating each supplementary material as acting independently when assessing durability performance [11].

Limitations — Mitigation strategies relying on supplementary cementitious materials may require impractically high replacement levels to be effective, and standard low dosages have proven insufficient for highly reactive aggregates [4]. Current performance predictions based on accelerated testing regimes are likely conservative compared to field conditions, and existing specification equations that assume independent additive effects do not align with experimental data for ternary blends [11]. Certain materials like silica fume act primarily as delay mechanisms rather than permanent solutions in exposed conditions, and the reliability of specific high-replacement blends requires further project-specific validation before widespread adoption [11].

ASR Mechanisms, Aggregate Reactivity and Detection

Alkali-silica reaction is a deleterious chemical process in concrete where alkalis from the cement paste react with reactive silica minerals present in certain aggregates to form an expansive gel [2] [4] [6] [17]. This gel absorbs moisture and swells, generating internal pressure that causes cracking and loss of durability within the concrete matrix [2] [4] [6] [17]. The reaction requires both an alkaline environment and alkali-sensitive aggregate, with reactivity levels commonly classified based on the aggregate’s tendency to participate in this process [11] [17]. Standardized laboratory testing methods are employed to assess the potential for ASR by measuring expansion under accelerated conditions [6] [11].

The following figure illustrates the characteristic cracking patterns resulting from this expansive gel formation.

Micrograph of concrete aggregate showing ASR gel filling cracks and rims. [4]

The image displays a cross-section of concrete containing various aggregates embedded in a cement matrix, with red arrows pointing to specific features. These arrows indicate the presence of gel-like material filling cracks and forming rims around the aggregate particles.

Research problem — There is a need to understand how sustainability-driven reductions in cementitious content affect the risk of alkali-silica reaction, particularly regarding whether lower cement levels require stricter mitigation of reactive aggregates [2]. Rapid or revised testing procedures are required to quickly assess aggregate susceptibility and avoid early-age durability problems that jeopardize intended service life [10]. Reliable detection methods are needed for alkali-silica reaction distress because moisture conditions can mask ground-penetrating radar signals, hindering early identification and timely repair [17].

The figure below illustrates how these distress mechanisms can manifest as detectable moisture gradients.

Ground-penetrating radar profiles showing moisture gradients in concrete slabs containing reactive aggregate. [17]

The figure displays two vertical ground-penetrating radar profiles, where the left panel shows a relatively uniform signal and the right panel exhibits significant color variation indicating internal heterogeneity. A black arrow in the right profile points to a specific region of disturbance within the concrete matrix, which corresponds to the location where reactive aggregate was placed.

Findings from the reports

  • Ground-penetrating radar failed to reliably detect alkali-silica reaction damage in laboratory slabs and mortar specimens, producing inconclusive results despite visible surface cracking [17].
  • Alkali-silica reaction was present in all examined pavement cores, with chert in the fine aggregate identified as the primary reactive constituent driving severity ranging from negligible to moderate-to-severe [4].
  • Low-alkali cement alone did not eliminate alkali-silica reaction, necessitating the use of supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash or slag to mitigate expansion [4].
  • Highly reactive fine shale aggregate particles generated significant alkali-silica gel and microcracking, but these features did not drive joint distress in the studied concrete [5].
  • Coarse aggregate showed no evidence of alkali-silica reaction and remained hard and sound, indicating it was not participating in the observed concrete distress [5].
  • Field observations identified map- or web-shaped cracking patterns as possible indicators of aggregate reactivity, though no quantitative performance data specific to alkali-silica reaction was provided [7].

Novelty & rigor — Research has introduced ground-penetrating radar as a novel nondestructive method to detect subtle internal signs of alkali-silica reaction, such as moisture gradients and hidden cracking, in specially fabricated concrete specimens [17]. A unique investigative approach combines field-scale permeability testing with enhanced microscopic detection techniques to assess how deicing salts and freeze-thaw cycles may exacerbate deterioration associated with alkali-silica reaction [5].

The following figure illustrates a three-dimensional visualization of this ground-penetrating radar survey.

Three-dimensional visualization of the GPR survey (note: rebar is colored red) [17]

The figure displays a three-dimensional volumetric rendering of a concrete slab, visualized using ground-penetrating radar. Internal features are highlighted in pink within the gray matrix, representing moisture gradients or hidden cracking associated with alkali-silica reaction.

Across the reviewed reports, petrographic analysis consistently identifies specific fine aggregate minerals—such as chert and shale—as the primary reactive constituents driving ASR gel formation and microcracking, while coarse aggregates are generally found to be non-reactive and not contributing to distress. [4] [5]

Implications — Engineers should not rely on ground-penetrating radar as a definitive diagnostic tool for alkali-silica reaction, given its inability to reliably distinguish ASR distress from moisture-related artifacts [17]. Petrographic analysis remains the essential method for confirming or ruling out alkali-silica reaction in concrete structures [5] [17]. When petrographic evidence confirms that reactive aggregate particles are present but not causing distress, mitigation efforts should be redirected toward other deterioration mechanisms such as freeze-thaw damage rather than alkali-silica reaction control [5].

Limitations — Ground-penetrating radar scans have not yet provided clear detection of ASR cracking, and it remains unconfirmed whether the technique can reliably measure ASR-induced expansion [17]. Current diagnostic methods like ground-penetrating radar and visual inspection only reveal symptoms rather than providing definitive diagnosis, which still requires coring and petrographic analysis [17].

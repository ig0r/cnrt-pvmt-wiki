Penetrating Sealers

Part of: Transport Properties & Permeability is-mitigation-method-for
Penetrating Sealers is a mitigation method for Transport Properties & Permeability.
conceptconcrete-durabilityjointsmoisture-protectionsurface-treatment
Also known as: sealer, topical treatment
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-11

Chemical treatments applied to concrete surfaces that penetrate into the pore structure to reduce water absorption, chloride ingress, and freeze-thaw damage. Sealers function through four distinct mechanisms depending on their chemical composition: pore blocking, pore lining, barrier coating, and water repellency. [1]

Contact Angle Measurement is a test method used to evaluate the hydrophobic properties of penetrating sealers and predict their ability to repel water. [1] It correlates well with early sorptivity but diminishes in utility for long-term absorption behavior or distinguishing between pore-blocking and pore-lining mechanisms. [1]

Penetrating sealers mitigate transport properties and permeability by blocking moisture ingress and limiting chloride ion penetration [1]. These materials function primarily through capillary suction mechanisms to reduce fluid transport into the concrete paste [1]. This reduction in ingress extends the time to critical saturation, thereby decreasing exposure to freeze-thaw conditions and reducing the risk of chemical attacks such as oxychloride formation [1].

Contact Angle Measurement

Mechanisms of Action

There are four general mechanisms by which sealers work on concrete [1]: pore blocking, which fills or narrows capillary pores to physically block fluid entry and is best suited for high permeability concrete; pore lining, which coats pore surfaces to change surface energy and reduce capillary suction without significantly reducing permeability but preventing water absorption; barrier coating, which forms a physical film on the concrete surface that can shed during freeze-thaw cycling and is best suited for impermeable concrete; and water repellency, which creates hydrophobic surface chemistry to prevent water from entering pores via capillary action. While air permeability testing serves as a diagnostic tool for gas transmission, products that are completely impenetrable to gases may be undesirable in applications where concrete breathability is required for moisture drying [1].

Air (gas) permeability index for concrete [1]

Sealer Families and Chemical Composition

| Family | Chemical Composition | Primary Mechanism | |--------|---------------------|-------------------| | Lithium silicate | Silicic acid, lithium salt, potassium methylsiliconate | Pore liner | | Silane (40%) | Reactive alkyltrialkoxysilane | Pore liner, hydrophobic | | Silane/siloxane combination | Colloidal silica | Surface densifier | | Sodium tartrate/silicate (SoTa) | Sodium tartrate, potassium methyl siliconate | Pore blocker/liner, hydrophobic | | Acrylic | Water-based acrylic polymer | Barrier coating | | Soy methyl ester (SME) | Fatty acids, soy methyl ester, polystyrene | Pore blocker, hydrophobic |

Surface densifiers such as LS and SC exhibit lower contact angles because they lack inherent hydrophobic properties compared to organic or hydrophobic sealers. [1]

Contact angle measurements using ImageJ [1]

Performance by Distress Mechanism

Because different sealer types are appropriate for different deterioration mechanisms [1], products must be matched to the specific distress mechanism, as no single sealer family is optimal for all conditions [1]. For a marginal air-void system, pore liners and hydrophobic sealers extend time to critical saturation to provide additional freeze-thaw protection, while barrier coatings are also effective but may shed during cycling [1]. In oxychloride-susceptible concrete, all sealer types reduced CaOXY formation potential, though densifiers and pore liners were most effective at reducing initial solution ingress [1].

Scaling and visual rating of specimens after 140 F-T cycles [1]

Regarding D-cracking susceptibility, pore liners and hydrophobic sealers performed best, whereas pure pore blockers (SME) may be detrimental to F-T performance on permeable concrete [1]. For permeable concrete specifically, both pore liners and pore blockers reduce absorption, and barrier coatings are also effective [1].

Evolution of absorption and desorption for concrete specimens: mAir (left), mOXY (center), and mDcrack (right) [1]

The contact angle correlates well with early water absorption (0–5 days) [1], where a higher contact angle generally indicates better resistance to initial water ingress.

Relationship between contact angles and water absorption of coated specimens at 5 hrs and at 15 days [1]

This relationship diminishes as exposure duration increases, meaning the contact angle is not useful for predicting long-term absorption behavior or performance under cyclic exposure [1].

The contact angle does not correlate with secondary absorption behavior and does not distinguish between pore-blocking and pore-lining mechanisms.

Pore-blocking sealers can trap internal moisture, resulting in higher saturation levels after drying compared to untreated controls [1].

Mitigating Specific Concrete Deterioration

To stabilize existing concrete against alkali-silica reaction (ASR) distress, lithium-based treatments can be applied via saturation or pressure injection [2]. These lithium saturation treatments aim to produce revitalized, ASR-stable concrete pavements without requiring traditional repair or replacement downtime [2]. Regarding material composition, Class F fly ash may improve durability in slag aggregate concrete, whereas Class C fly ash can have a negative impact on performance [2]. Furthermore, transverse cracking in jointed concrete pavements is significantly influenced by joint spacing, coarse aggregate type, and shoulder configuration [2], though concrete overlays are expected to last 20–25 years or more without major rehabilitation compared to the shorter lifespan of asphalt resurfacing [2]. Additionally, soybean oil treatments have been demonstrated to reduce water absorption and improve resistance to deicer scaling without significantly altering chloride ion penetration rates [1].

Chloride content percentage results [1]

Application Timing and Surface Preparation

Sealers are most effective when applied to relatively new concrete, as application timing affects penetration depth; typically, they are applied after the concrete has cured for 28 days to ensure proper development of engineering properties [2]. Because saw-cut joint faces present a different surface than broom-finished slabs, sealers may perform differently on these surfaces. Furthermore, field application requires careful surface preparation and proper application rates. Proper curing methods, such as wet curing and membrane curing, produce more durable and wear-resistant surfaces that sealers then protect [2].

Joint Management and Seal Integrity

To maintain seal integrity against water penetration, joint fillers must be placed with initial compression to accommodate joint opening [2]. While foam joint fillers provide effective sealing when joints close, they allow water penetration into the base if the joint opens [2].

Long-term Performance and Maintenance

Reapplication may be needed over time as sealers degrade or are worn away. Because performance degradation is time-dependent, field data from bridge decks showed that while silane-based sealers experienced no failures during the first 12 years, nearly half of the treated surfaces failed after 18 years of service [1].

Evolution of the degrees of saturation with time [1]

Testing And Evaluation Methods

Used to evaluate the hydrophobic properties of penetrating sealers and predict their ability to repel water. [1]

Degree of saturation for concrete specimens: mAir (top left), mOXY (top right), and mDcrack (bottom) [1]

Penetrating sealers are evaluated by measuring the water contact angle on treated concrete surfaces to determine their hydrophobicity [1]. This measurement method helps classify the surface properties and assess how effectively the sealer converts the naturally hydrophilic concrete into a hydrophobic state [1]. Consequently, these evaluations provide insight into the sealer’s ability to reduce water absorption and improve durability [1].

Secondary Absorption Phenomena

An elevated secondary absorption rate in treated concrete is attributed to the formation of a water bridge between the thin sealer layer and the underlying untreated concrete [1].

Water absorption and desorption development of concrete specimens [1]

The increase in water contact angle indicates that sealers reduce molecular attraction between water and pores, thereby decreasing capillary suction potential according to the Young-Laplace equation [1].

Limitations Of Testing Methods

It does not account for subsurface pore structure effects, making it more useful for comparing sealer products than for predicting field performance.

Unbonded Concrete Overlay on Asphalt (UBCOA)

Part of: Concrete Overlay

A concrete overlay type where a portland cement concrete layer is placed over an existing asphalt concrete pavement with a bond breaker layer (typically HMA) between them. UBCOA is the smallest category in Iowa’s overlay database, representing 18% of all overlay projects and 17% of total overlay lane-miles.

Overview of UBCOA Performance

  • 94% of UBCOA projects rated good to excellent (PCI ≥ 60) — the highest among all four overlay types
  • UBCOA achieved a PCI of 76 at year 20, tied with BCOA for the best performance among all types
  • UBCOA achieved an IRI of 128 in/mi at year 20 — the lowest (best) among all types

Unbonded concrete overlays on asphalt (UBCOAs) outperform bonded counterparts, with 94% of UBCOAs reaching good-to-excellent condition compared to 88% for bonded concrete overlays on asphalt (BCOAs) [1].

Comparative Performance Analysis

UBCOAs consistently outperformed overlays on concrete (BCOC and UBCOC) [1], as empirical data indicates that unbonded concrete overlays on asphalt generally exhibit superior performance compared to bonded concrete overlays on asphalt, with a higher percentage of projects maintaining good to excellent condition ratings over time [1].

Direct shear tests on UBCOA cores reveal that the system’s weak point typically occurs at an asphaltic concrete lift line located one inch or more below the surface, where insufficient asphalt depth prevents a valid shear test upon core removal [2]. Furthermore, empirical monitoring shows that UBCOA joint opening follows a sinusoidal pattern over time, with values shifting from positive to negative and back again across survey periods [2].

Tied transverse crack [2]

Material Components and Bond Breakers

In UBCOA pavements, the inclusion of synthetic fibers—such as polypropylene fibrillated, monofilament, or structural types at rates of one to three pounds per cubic yard—has no significant impact on the level of faulting [2]. For these systems, geotextile fabric interlayers, which are approximately 0.1 inches thick, can be used as a bond breaker to reduce reflective cracking while potentially improving drainage due to their high permeability compared to traditional asphalt concrete layers [3].

These geotextile materials may be utilized in concrete overlay projects as bond separators, having demonstrated effective performance in preventing bonding to the lower concrete layer while absorbing mortar from above [4]. Furthermore, these geotextile bond breakers, approximately 0.1 inches thick, can serve as effective separation layers between the asphalt and concrete, offering potential cost savings and reduced vertical clearance requirements compared to traditional one-inch HMA layers [4].

The gap in the HMA bond breaker where the geotextile material was placed [4]

Design Parameters and Slab Dimensions

For unbonded concrete overlays on asphalt, slab dimensions are typically designed at 6 feet by 6 feet to manage stress distribution and cracking patterns [5]. To ensure effective stress relief, a separation layer thickness of one and a half inches is specified for unbonded overlays placed over existing asphalt pavements [5]. Within Iowa’s pavement management database, UBCOA projects are typically classified as overlays thicker than six inches, which distinguishes them from bonded concrete overlays on asphalt (BCOA) that are designated at six inches or less [1]; this thickness threshold serves as a critical design parameter for categorizing the overlay type [1].

– Placing the 4” Thick Unbonded Concrete Overlay (existing concrete was milled and a geotextile separation layer used) [5]

Cyclic automated plate load testing of UBCOA sections with geotextile fabric interlayers showed core thicknesses ranging from 8.8 to 10.2 inches, whereas sections using asphalt concrete interlayers ranged from 7.3 to 7.6 inches [3].

Joint Spacing and Load Transfer Efficiency

The structural integrity and performance of unbonded concrete overlays are significantly influenced by the adequacy of system drainage, as inadequate drainage has been identified as a primary cause of premature deterioration and lower pavement condition index values [1]. For thin concrete overlays ranging from two to five inches in depth, transverse joints should be spaced at a maximum distance of five joints during the initial ‘skip’ sawing process to prevent premature cracking [4]. In travel lanes for unbonded overlays, transverse joints are typically doweled and spaced at approximately 15 feet center-to-center to maintain load transfer across cracks [5].

Concrete overlay transverse joint spacing based on number of projects [1]

The bond between concrete and asphalt is critical for load transfer, as unbonded overlays with geotextile separation layers exhibit significantly lower joint load transfer efficiency (LTE) compared to conventional COA overlays that may unintentionally bond [6]. For example, a 6-inch PCC overlay on HMA achieved an average LTE of 82.9% when bonded but dropped to 47.8% when deliberately separated by a geotextile layer [6]. Furthermore, shorter transverse joint spacing improves load transfer efficiency in unbonded concrete overlays; sections with 5.5 ft or 6 ft spacing demonstrated significantly higher LTE than those with spacings of 11 ft or greater [6]. Additionally, unbonded concrete overlays on asphalt contribute substantially to the structural system’s effective thickness through the underlying asphalt layer, which can add between 3.9 inches and 6.0 inches to the backcalculated effective thickness compared to deliberately debonded sections [6].

Single layer assumption for COA overlays [6]

Construction Methods and Traffic Management

Unbonded concrete overlays can be constructed with traffic continuing in adjacent lanes, or traffic can be shifted to one side of a median to facilitate full-width construction operations. [5]

In 2011, Iowa DOT constructed the first unbonded PCC overlay on a two-lane roadway under traffic (US-18, 18.82 miles), demonstrating stringless milling and paving with single-lane pilot car operations [5].

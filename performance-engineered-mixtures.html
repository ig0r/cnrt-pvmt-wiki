Performance-Engineered Mixtures

Performance-Engineered Mixtures (PEM) represent a shift from traditional prescriptive concrete pavement specifications to performance-based approaches that better predict long-term pavement durability. PEM focuses on measuring and specifying concrete properties directly linked to field performance rather than relying on indirect indicators like strength, slump, and air content.

Introduction to PEM Approach

The PEM approach identifies six critical performance properties for concrete pavements [1].

PPM = pore partitioning model; PPMC = pore partitioning model for concrete Jason Weiss Figure 34. Calculation framework used to predict concrete performance [1]

Six Critical Performance Properties

  1. Transport properties: Resistance to fluid penetration through the concrete
  2. Freeze-thaw durability: Resistance to damage from cyclic freezing and thawing
  3. Aggregate stability: Resistance to alkali-aggregate reactivity and aggregate freeze-thaw damage
  4. Strength: Adequate compressive and flexural strength for design requirements
  5. Shrinkage: Low risk of cracking and warping due to drying shrinkage
  6. Workability: Ease of mixing, placing, consolidating, and finishing to homogeneous condition

Innovative Test Methods and Procedures

PEM employs innovative test methods to measure various properties, including the Super Air Meter (SAM), which measures air-void system quality in fresh concrete (AASHTO T 395), and Surface Resistivity, a nondestructive electrical test for transport properties (AASHTO T 358). To normalize these electrical test results for pore connectivity, the Formation Factor is utilized. Other methods include the VKelly Test, which measures concrete response to vibration (AASHTO TP 129, approved as T 403), and the Box Test, which assesses consolidation ability and edge slumping (AASHTO TP 137). To ensure adequate consolidation ability in mix proportioning, the Box Test mandates an edge slump of less than 0.25 inches and a ranking of 2 or less; this test is required for mixture design in states like Colorado to verify constructability [1]. Additionally, the Phoenix Device measures moisture content of aggregates and fresh concrete [1]. The Phoenix device is utilized to assess the water-to-cementitious materials (w/cm) ratio in fresh concrete, providing a direct measurement of this critical durability parameter, which allows agencies to validate mix proportions against performance targets rather than relying solely on prescriptive limits [1].

Fresh air, hardened air, and SAM air [1]

Specification Frameworks and Standards

PEM is formalized in AASHTO R 101, “Standard Practice for Developing Performance Engineered Concrete Pavement Mixtures,” which provides both performance-based and prescriptive approaches, allowing agencies to choose methods best suited to their circumstances. This standard evolved from AASHTO PP 84 (2017) to R 101 (2022) through annual revisions based on field validation [1]. To ensure durability, performance specifications may mandate a maximum shrinkage limit of 500 microstrain and require minimum surface resistivity values, such as 10 kΩ/cm at 28 days [2]. Specific transport property thresholds include a Rapid Chloride Permeability Test (RCPT) result of less than 2,500 coulombs at less than 56 days or a surface resistivity greater than 12 kΩ-cm at 28 days, while unrestrained shrinkage requirements are specified as less than 0.05% at 28 days [1].

AASHTO R 101 timeline [1]

In terms of regional implementation, New York specifications now require specific sieve sizes in the sieve analysis to provide clarity regarding optimized aggregate gradation when using the Tarantula curve, and the state has also moved toward exclusive use of 4 in. x 8 in. cylinders for resistivity testing to simplify conversion factors [1]. Similarly, North Carolina’s structural concrete pilot project includes specification provisions and performance targets for resistivity, SAM number, and shrinkage; furthermore, the state is considering a reduction of current prescriptive w/cm ratio limits and cement contents based on PEM validation [1].

Sustainability Benefits of PEM

PEM approaches demonstrate improvements across all sustainability dimensions, including Economic benefits such as reduced maintenance costs, minimized cement contents, and extended pavement life; Environmental advantages through lower CO₂ emissions via cement optimization and reduced shrinkage/cracking; and Social improvements through smoother pavements and improved safety due to fewer lane closures. To achieve these goals, the approach encourages reduced cement content, increased use of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), and optimized aggregate gradations to lower carbon footprints while maintaining performance [1].

After U.S. Department of Energy Office of Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy’s Bioenergy Technologies Office (BETO) vision diagram (https://www.energy.gov/eere/bioenergy/sustainability) Figure 1. PEM sustainability [1]

Furthermore, optimized aggregate gradations allow for improvements to mixture workability with a parallel reduction in paste volume, which historically addressed performance issues by adding cement that increases CO2 emissions and shrinkage potential. Consequently, the PEM approach encourages judicious use of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) to reduce the carbon footprint while maintaining durability [1].

Implementation and State Adoption

As of 2022, multiple states had adopted or were developing PEM specifications, including New York, which maintains an active PEM specification with a SAM requirement ≤0.20 for mixture design. Colorado removed cementitious content requirements and instead adopted Box Test and transport requirements, while North Carolina has seen successful demonstration projects with contractor adoption. Minnesota implemented a shadow specification and was the first state to try the Phoenix device, and Kansas started its SAM program with specification development currently underway.

In Michigan, the use of optimized aggregate gradation mixtures is mandated on all high-performance concrete mixtures for pavements and structures, and the state has advanced special provisions for the use of SCMs, contractor-provided mixes, and optimized aggregate gradation into standard specifications [1]. Additionally, field validation on the I-80 rigid pavement reconstruction in California utilized a cementitious system composed of Type II-V cement, Grade 120 slag cement, and Class F fly ash [2].

Ternary Cementitious Mixtures

Ternary mixtures, which combine portland cement with two or more supplementary cementitious materials such as slag, fly ash, and silica fume, are specifically designed to enhance concrete durability rather than merely increasing compressive strength [3]. To improve concrete pavement sustainability, Iowa has utilized ternary cementitious materials, using blended cements and fly ash, on paving projects [1]. Consequently, the state is considering updating specifications to include PEM testing such as the SAM, Box Test, and resistivity to allow for reduced cement content [1].

Bulk chemical composition ranges for some common supplementary cementitious materials (from reference 5) [3]

Mix Design Optimization Techniques

Optimizing these complex cementitious systems requires balancing chemical admixture dosages with supplementary material levels to manage setting times, strength gain rates, and workability across varying ambient weather conditions [3]. For instance, ternary mixtures with pozzolans comprising between 40 and 50 percent of total cementitious content have demonstrated effective performance in cool to hot weather environments without significant technical barriers for pavement construction [2].

Temperature data for ternary mixtures with 35% slag cement [2]

The Tarantula curve, an optimized aggregate gradation tool incorporated into proportioning spreadsheets, enables suppliers to reduce paste volume and cement content while maintaining workability [1]. This optimization facilitates the construction of smoother pavements that significantly lower vehicle fuel consumption over their service life [1]. Consequently, states such as West Virginia have adopted the Tarantula curve for optimizing aggregate gradation and lowered cementitious materials content by 40 lb/yd³ for all concrete as a contractor option; this reduction is achieved through optimized gradations that improve workability with parallel reductions in paste volume [1].

Constructability Challenges

The integration of supplementary cementitious materials can introduce various constructability challenges, such as rapid slump loss, unstable air content retention, uncontrolled cracking during late-season paving, and scaling at high material dosages [3]. Furthermore, mixtures containing Class F fly ash may exhibit an unexpected decrease in setting time due to increased fineness, while finer grind slag cements like grade 120 tend to reduce flow and set time compared to coarser grades [2].

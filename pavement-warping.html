Pavement Warping

Part of: Curling and Warping is-specific-phenomenon-of
Pavement Warping is a specific moisture-driven phenomenon of Curling and Warping.
jpcpmoisture-gradientpavement-engineeringslab-deflectionwarping
Also known as: PCC warping, concrete pavement warping, slab warping
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-20

Pavement warping is an out-of-plane bending deformation of a concrete slab that occurs when non-uniform temperature or moisture conditions develop through its thickness. These environmental gradients generate differential expansion, contraction, or shrinkage between the surface and interior layers, causing the slab to curve upward or downward depending on which side experiences greater dimensional change [1] [5] [6]. The resulting curvature induces internal tensile stresses that are amplified by structural restraints and traffic loads, potentially leading to cracking if they exceed the concrete’s strength [3] [6] [12]. The magnitude of this deformation is governed by material properties such as thermal expansion and stiffness, slab geometry, subgrade support conditions, and the specific environmental history during construction [6] [11] [12].

Key findings

  • Pavement warping is primarily driven by non-uniform temperature and moisture gradients across the concrete slab depth, which generate differential strains that cause curvature [1] [5] [6].
  • Skewed slabs produce larger warping than rectangular ones because skewed joints effectively increase slab dimensions and raise tensile stress ratios at corners [1] [12].
  • Internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate markedly cuts temperature and moisture gradients, which dramatically lessens slab warping and curling while enhancing ride quality [9] [10].
  • Seasonal moisture differences exert a stronger influence on warping than daily fluctuations, producing upward warp when the bottom of the slab retains more moisture than the top [5].
  • Greater upward curling and warping are linked to poorer service performance, resulting in lower Pavement Condition Index (PCI) values, higher International Roughness Index (IRI) scores, and increased cracking [1].
  • Mix designs with increased cement content and reduced fly ash amplify warping, whereas reducing cement and adding fly ash limits it [1].
  • Warping peaks during the fall season and is greatest during early-morning measurements, whereas paving in spring or fall tends to reduce these metrics compared to other seasons [9].
  • Rectangular slabs paired with tied PCC shoulders consistently lower curvature IRI and deflection ratios compared with alternative geometries or untied shoulders [2].

Mechanisms and Prediction of Early-Age Warping

Pavement warping is an out-of-plane deformation of concrete slabs caused by non-uniform temperature or moisture gradients through the slab thickness, which generate differential expansion and shrinkage between the top and bottom surfaces [3] [5] [6]. These internal stresses are most pronounced during early-age concrete when rapid drying and diurnal temperature cycles create significant gradients that can lead to upward or downward curvature depending on the direction of the moisture or thermal difference [5] [7] [12]. The magnitude and behavior of warping are governed by material properties such as the coefficient of thermal expansion and modulus of elasticity, slab geometry including thickness and span, and the interaction between slab rigidity and subgrade support [6] [11].

Research problem — Early-age temperature and moisture gradients generate tensile stresses that cause concrete slabs to curl or warp before sufficient strength is gained, creating a high risk of premature cracking and structural failure [3] [5] [6]. Warping-induced deformations significantly degrade pavement smoothness and ride quality immediately after construction, complicating compliance with performance specifications and increasing long-term maintenance costs [2] [7]. Design decisions regarding joint spacing and slab geometry must balance cost savings against the potential for excessive warping stresses that lead to mid-panel cracking and accelerated deterioration under traffic loads [11] [12].

State of the art — Current practice treats pavement warping as a combined mechanical-thermal phenomenon that is now fully integrated into mechanistic-empirical design tools, such as the MEPDG, which utilize permanent effective curling/warping temperature offsets to predict long-term performance [1] [5]. Prediction and mitigation rely on high-resolution finite-element models that account for nonlinear temperature profiles and moisture transport, alongside maturity-based scheduling tools like HIPERPAV to manage early-age stress-strength margins [3] [5] [12]. Field quantification has advanced from single-line profilometers to high-resolution LiDAR point-cloud surveys and inertial profiling, enabling the extraction of multi-directional curvature metrics that feed into mechanistic models for comprehensive warping assessment [1] [2] [5].

Findings from the reports

  • Pavement warping is driven primarily by non-uniform temperature and moisture gradients through the slab depth, which generate differential strains that cause upward or downward curling [1] [5] [6].
  • Skewed joints effectively increase effective slab dimensions, producing higher curling and warping stresses and corner deflections compared with rectangular joints [2] [12].
  • Longer joint spacing increases shrinkage- and temperature-induced stresses, accelerating joint activation and elevating curling stress relative to shorter slab sections [2] [11].
  • Heavy axle loads straddling adjacent slabs significantly amplify warping stresses, with tensile stress ratios rising sharply when combined with negative temperature gradients [1] [12].
  • Tied PCC shoulders mitigate warping stresses and reduce curvature indicators compared to untied HMA or granular shoulders [2] [12].
  • Quality-management concrete (QMC) mix designs curb curling and warping more effectively than high-strength mixes, while increased cement content and reduced fly ash amplify warping [1] [2].
  • Seasonal moisture changes exert a stronger influence on early-age warping than daily temperature fluctuations, with fall conditions producing the highest curvature-related roughness indices [2] [5].
  • Longitudinal cracking associated with warping distress appears mainly near slab edges and runs parallel to traffic, particularly at sites characterized by granular shoulders and high truck volumes [1] [12].
  • Evaluated thermal-moisture stresses in concrete slabs with high-volume fly ash and slag replacements, demonstrating that stress levels remained below early-age strength for at least the first three days across typical seasonal temperature curves [3].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a dimensionless slab-length-to-relative-stiffness ratio as a practical design metric for predicting joint activation rates linked to curling-induced warping, offering a quantitative indicator that simplifies spacing decisions compared to complex mechanistic models [11]. A distinctive analytical framework couples the HIPERPAV computer program with experimentally measured properties of ternary cements and season-specific temperature profiles, enabling the prediction of early-age thermal-moisture stresses that lead to warping under realistic curing conditions [3]. The study provides a comprehensive field-based quantification of early-age warping by translating measured slab movements into an equivalent temperature difference, which allows moisture-shrinkage effects to be represented in mechanistic finite-element simulations for model calibration [5].

Across the reviewed reports, early-age pavement warping is collectively established as a phenomenon driven by non-uniform thermal and moisture gradients through the slab depth, which generate differential strains that cause upward or downward curvature depending on whether the top or bottom layer expands more. These environmental drivers are significantly amplified by specific geometric and material factors, including skewed joints, longer slab lengths, untied shoulders, and high-strength or high-cement mixes, all of which increase curling stresses and longitudinal cracking potential. While seasonal variations influence the magnitude of warping with fall conditions often producing higher curvature indices than spring or summer, finite-element models and field measurements validate that these effects can be quantified using an equivalent temperature difference to predict slab deflection and smoothness degradation. [2] [5] [6] [11] [12]

Implications — Designers must prioritize geometric controls, such as shorter joint spacing and rectangular slab shapes, to mitigate the increased warping stresses associated with skewed joints and larger slab dimensions [12]. Construction specifications should mandate environmental management during placement, including scheduling for cooler periods and implementing rigorous curing protocols, to minimize the thermal and moisture gradients that drive early-age deformation [1] [5] [6]. Mechanistic-empirical design models should incorporate specific effective temperature differences for warping to accurately predict long-term serviceability and roughness performance under varying traffic loads [2] [5].

Limitations — Conclusions regarding pavement warping remain tentative due to limited investigation of skewed joints and a lack of clear correlation between slab geometry or construction practices and observed distress [12]. The absence of standardized techniques for describing environmental effects hampers consistent quantification, while insufficient data collection prevents full validation of literature-based recommendations [2]. Predictive models exhibit weaker-than-expected correlations with low explanatory power, and the omission of significant variables reduces confidence in the statistical findings [2].

Material Properties & Mix Design

Research problem — Low water-to-cement ratios in concrete mixtures limit internal moisture availability, leading to autogenous and differential drying shrinkage that induces upward warping and tensile stresses [4] [10]. Existing mixture design guidelines struggle to adequately control early-age volume changes in thin overlays, resulting in compromised ride quality, accelerated distress, and reduced service life [4] [9]. There is a need for mix designs that effectively mitigate moisture gradients through internal curing strategies to prevent warping-related cracking and maintain long-term pavement durability [9] [10].

The figure below illustrates the physical manifestation of these stresses as concrete slab warping and curling.

Concrete slab warping and curling [9]

The figure illustrates two distinct modes of out-of-plane deformation in concrete slabs: upward curling, where the surface is cooler and drier than the base, and downward curling, where the surface is at a higher temperature and moisture level.

State of the art — The prevailing mitigation strategy for pavement warping is the use of internally cured concrete incorporating pre-saturated lightweight fine aggregate, which buffers internal moisture movement and significantly reduces warping amplitude compared to conventional mixes [9]. Performance-engineered mix designs now impose explicit shrinkage limits, such as capping paste volume at 25% and restricting unrestrained drying shrinkage to under 420 µε, rather than relying solely on strength or slump criteria [4]. Recent advances include the development of regression-based predictive models that combine measured temperature and moisture differentials with LiDAR-derived deformation maps to validate warping behavior and support design decisions [9].

Findings from the reports

  • Internally cured concrete significantly reduced pavement warping compared with conventional control slabs under similar temperature-moisture conditions [9] [10].
  • Internal curing lowered the concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion, modulus of elasticity, and ultimate shrinkage, which collectively reduced curvature caused by temperature gradients [10].
  • The use of lightweight fine aggregate for internal curing markedly cut temperature and moisture gradients in the concrete system [9].
  • Field measurements demonstrated that internally cured slabs exhibited smaller deflections than conventional slabs during both winter morning and noon periods [9].
  • Reduced warping and curling from internal curing improved ride quality and lowered the likelihood of corner breaks [9].
  • The performance benefits of internal curing allowed for thinner slabs or wider joint spacing without sacrificing structural performance [10].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces internal curing as a mix design strategy that replaces normal-weight fine aggregate with saturated lightweight aggregates to create distributed water reservoirs, thereby reducing autogenous shrinkage and thermal expansion coefficients to mitigate warping [9] [10]. A performance-engineered specification approach uniquely addresses warping by imposing strict limits on paste volume and unrestrained drying shrinkage strain, linking early-age material properties directly to field durability outcomes [4]. The methodologies are reproducible through detailed mix proportioning protocols for internal curing and standardized performance testing regimes that verify shrinkage limits before production [4] [9] [10].

Across the reviewed reports, internally cured concrete utilizing lightweight fine aggregate collectively establishes a reduction in pavement warping by lowering the material’s coefficient of thermal expansion and modulus of elasticity while simultaneously mitigating temperature and moisture gradients within the slab. This modified mix design results in significantly smaller predicted deflections under extreme environmental differentials compared to conventional concrete, as evidenced by field measurements showing substantially reduced warping magnitudes. [9] [10]

The figure below illustrates the modulus of elasticity test results that contribute to these improved performance characteristics.

Modulus of elasticity test results (psi) [9]

The figure plots the modulus of elasticity in ksi against age in days for concrete mixtures containing Washington and Winneshiek aggregates, comparing conventional curing (CC) with internally cured (IC) conditions. The data shows that the internally cured samples exhibit a lower modulus of elasticity compared to their conventionally cured counterparts at later ages. This reduction in stiffness is relevant to pavement warping because a lower modulus results in reduced stress under strain, thereby mitigating the risk of cracking associated with environmental gradients.

Implications — Incorporating internal curing techniques reduces thermal expansion and shrinkage, allowing designers to specify thinner slabs or wider joint spacing while maintaining structural performance [10]. Using lightweight fine aggregates for internal curing minimizes warping stresses in overlays, enabling larger slab dimensions without saw-cuts and extending the functional service life of the pavement [9].

Limitations — The study’s applicability is constrained by its reliance on data from specific highway designs that differ from thinner county and city road pavements [1]. Validation of literature-based relationships regarding material properties is limited by a lack of detailed information on the water absorption characteristics of the coarse aggregate used in the investigated pavements [1]. The suggestion to extend slab sizes for thinner sections remains speculative as it is based on low-traffic test sections, and practical barriers exist regarding the storage and preconditioning of lightweight fine aggregate for larger projects [9].

Monitoring, Measurement, and Effects on Pavement Performance

State of the art — Current practice monitors pavement warping by deploying embedded sensor arrays to capture non-uniform temperature and moisture gradients, which are then processed through concrete maturity models to predict early-age deformation and guide mitigation strategies [6]. Recent technological advances have introduced rugged wireless MEMS networks that enable real-time monitoring of thermal and humidity conditions at multiple slab depths over extended distances, although long-term sensor survivability remains a significant barrier to widespread adoption [6].

The following figure illustrates the moisture sensor packaging used in these monitoring systems.

Moisture sensor packaging [6]

The image displays a cylindrical bamboo tube assembly equipped with multiple sensors, which is inserted into the asphalt pavement surface. This apparatus represents a field packaging method developed for monitoring moisture conditions in concrete pavements during construction.

Findings from the reports

  • Field measurements revealed consistent upward curling and warping of concrete slabs, with diagonal directions exhibiting the largest magnitudes [1].
  • Deflection magnitudes varied significantly based on slab orientation and specific site conditions [1].
  • A modest diurnal effect associated with small temperature changes influenced deflection levels between morning and afternoon scans [1].
  • Adjacent slabs exhibited minimal differences in warping behavior, differing by no more than a narrow range of mils [1].
  • Upward curling occurred when the slab bottom warmed faster than the top, while downward curling resulted from surface heating [7].
  • Warping behaviors produced measurable changes in slab curvature that directly affected pavement smoothness metrics [7].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a comprehensive early-age monitoring framework that simultaneously tracks slab temperature gradients, joint opening, vertical movement, and surface profile to directly link curling and warping with pavement smoothness metrics [7]. Methodological distinctiveness is achieved through the deployment of an unprecedented suite of real-time field instrumentation, including MEMS sensors integrated with wireless transmission to eliminate permanent cabling and high-resolution inclinometer profiling adapted for rolling measurement patterns [5] [6]. The approach establishes a reproducible computational workflow that automates the conversion of raw profiler traces into quantitative curvature indicators, providing an objective alternative to traditional hand-calculated assessments and enabling direct calibration of finite-element models [2] [5].

Across the reviewed reports, field instrumentation confirms that early-age concrete slabs undergo significant upward and downward warping driven by diurnal temperature gradients, with measurable vertical movements directly influencing pavement smoothness metrics. Quantitative measurements reveal substantial variability in deflection magnitudes across different slab orientations and sites, with diagonal directions exhibiting the largest warping effects while adjacent slabs show relatively minor differences. Implications — Designers should prioritize the use of high-speed profiling equipment over slower inclinometer profilers to accurately capture early-age warping effects on pavement smoothness [7]. Construction monitoring protocols can be optimized by reducing temperature instrumentation to a single sensor per section while integrating LVDTs in adjacent slabs and humidity sensors to enhance the fidelity of finite-element models [7].

Limitations — Early-age observations are provisional due to limited pilot data and instrumentation that was inadequate for the planned schedule, resulting in truncated profiles and gaps in temperature monitoring [5]. Extensive sensor failures significantly reduced long-term data coverage, preventing robust inference of warping behavior over time [6]. Modeling limitations and the use of simple smoothness metrics may mask subtle curvature effects, leaving environmental correlations difficult to validate [5].

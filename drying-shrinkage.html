Drying Shrinkage

Part of: Shrinkage & Early-Age Cracking is-shrinkage-mechanism-of
Drying Shrinkage is a shrinkage mechanism categorized under Shrinkage & Early-Age Cracking.
concretecrack-mitigationdurabilityshrinkage
Also known as: long-term shrinkage
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-17T15:56:38Z

Drying shrinkage is a volumetric reduction in hardened cementitious materials that occurs as moisture evaporates from the pore structure into the surrounding environment. The fundamental mechanism involves the loss of water from capillary pores, which generates internal tensile stresses through capillary tension that pulls the solid skeleton inward [1] [7] [26]. Because evaporation typically occurs faster at exposed surfaces than within the interior, a moisture gradient develops that causes differential contraction between layers [3] [6] [20]. When these shrinkage-induced tensile stresses exceed the material’s low tensile strength or are restrained by external boundaries, they result in cracking, warping, or curling [2] [10] [17]. The magnitude of this phenomenon is governed by mixture proportions such as cement paste content and water-to-cement ratio, as well as environmental conditions including ambient humidity and curing practices [12] [19] [26].

Key findings

  • Supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash and slag generally reduce drying shrinkage compared to pure Type I Portland cement, whereas blends containing Type I/II cement often exhibit higher shrinkage [12] [13] [14] [20].
  • Internal curing techniques using lightweight fine aggregates or superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) lower ultimate drying shrinkage and mitigate early-age cracking risk [23] [25] [26].
  • Drying shrinkage is the dominant driver of concrete pavement curling and warping, exerting a far greater influence than thermal gradients alone [3] [6].
  • Fiber reinforcement effectively suppresses crack initiation and reduces the number and width of drying-shrinkage cracks without altering the final magnitude of shrinkage strain [25].
  • Recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) increases drying shrinkage due to its higher porosity and mortar content compared to conventional aggregates [18].
  • Highly absorptive blast-furnace-slag aggregates promote excessive plastic and drying shrinkage, leading to significant cracking in jointed sections [17].
  • Self-consolidating concrete (SFSCC) exhibits markedly greater drying shrinkage than conventional pavement concrete under standard laboratory conditions [22].
  • Shrinkage-reducing admixtures effectively mitigate elevated shrinkage in specific mix designs, with higher dosages further lowering shrinkage and eliminating cracking susceptibility [22].

Mechanisms and Fundamental Effects of Drying Shrinkage

Drying shrinkage is a volumetric reduction in hardened concrete that occurs as moisture evaporates from the cement paste into the surrounding environment [3] [17] [26]. The loss of water generates capillary tension within the pore system, which pulls the solid skeleton inward and creates internal tensile stresses [1] [4] [20]. Because evaporation typically proceeds faster at exposed surfaces than in the interior, a moisture gradient develops that causes differential contraction and induces warping or curling [3] [6] [21]. When these shrinkage-induced tensile stresses exceed the material’s low tensile capacity or are restrained by external boundaries, they result in cracking and reduced durability [2] [10] [19].

Research problem — The inability to reliably predict how moisture loss translates into shrinkage-related cracking or joint opening, compounded by excessive autogenous shrinkage and self-desiccation, leaves current mix designs and models inadequate for ensuring pavement durability [15]. Differential drying shrinkage between the slab surface and interior generates irreversible volume loss and permanent warping that degrade ride quality and shift performance specifications, yet the mechanisms driving this early-age deformation remain insufficiently understood for reliable design [3] [10]. Excessive drying shrinkage creates tensile stresses that exceed the low strength of fresh concrete or restrained overlays, leading to premature cracking and joint activation failures that accelerate deterioration and increase lifecycle maintenance costs [2] [17] [22] [30].

State of the art — Drying shrinkage is currently understood as a surface-driven volume loss occurring primarily within the upper few inches of concrete slabs, resulting from moisture evaporation that induces upward warping and early-age cracking [4]. In mechanistic-empirical pavement design, this phenomenon is modeled as part of transient slab curvature using finite-element tools to predict permanent deformation effects [3]. Recent research characterizes drying shrinkage as a complex moisture-transport process significantly altered by deicing salts, which modify solution properties and shift equilibrium relative humidity thresholds within the pore structure [8].

Findings from the reports

  • The use of dry, highly absorptive slag aggregate was associated with increased drying shrinkage cracking because the aggregate absorbed water from the cement paste and interfered with air-entrainment systems [9] [17].
  • Drying shrinkage was identified as the dominant early-age factor in concrete pavements, with moisture loss driving most of the initial shrinkage and causing irreversible deformation that leads to permanent slab curvature [3].
  • The rapid surface drying created a depthwise moisture gradient that induced non-uniform shrinkage, resulting in upward warping of slab corners during the first two days after paving [3].
  • Drying shrinkage, expressed as a shrinkage-equivalent temperature difference, was found to be the primary driver of concrete pavement curling and warping, exerting a greater influence than hourly or total equivalent temperature differences [6].
  • As the shrinkage-equivalent temperature difference approached zero, curvature, IRI, deflection, and deflection ratio declined, producing flatter slabs with no curling or warping [6].
  • Drying-shrinkage cracks acted as conduits for carbonation, allowing carbonation fronts to advance several millimeters along these microcracks [8].
  • The presence of deicing salts altered moisture diffusion by raising equilibrium relative humidity and delaying drying, which shifted the moisture-diffusivity curve toward lower humidities [8].
  • Embedded strain gages confirmed that shrinkage-induced stresses were responsible for joint crack initiation, while subsequent temperature cycles produced only modest slab length changes [10].

Novelty & rigor — Novel field-based investigations have empirically linked early-age drying shrinkage to slab curvature by simultaneously measuring depth-wise moisture gradients and vertical movements, distinguishing these effects from thermal influences during the critical pre-traffic period [3]. Unprecedented methodological frameworks integrate high-resolution terrestrial laser scanning with bespoke data-reduction algorithms to isolate pure shrinkage-induced deformation from underlying grade profiles, providing a quantitative metric for moisture loss effects [4] [6].

Across the reviewed reports, drying shrinkage is established as the dominant early-age mechanism driving irreversible deformation and permanent slab curvature in concrete pavements. This shrinkage manifests physically as fine, sub-vertical microcracks that typically penetrate only a few millimeters into the surface layer, with depths varying based on aggregate properties and environmental conditions. The use of highly absorptive slag aggregates exacerbates these effects by promoting excessive plastic and drying shrinkage cracks through water absorption from the cement paste. While rapid surface moisture loss creates non-uniform shrinkage strains that warp slab corners upward, the resulting microcracks serve as conduits for carbonation and are influenced by factors such as deicing salts. [3] [6] [8] [9] [17]

Implications — Designers must prioritize mix proportioning strategies that minimize water demand and utilize low-expansion aggregates to mitigate the dominant role of drying shrinkage in inducing early-age slab curvature [3] [6]. Construction specifications should mandate uniform curing protocols over the entire surface during the critical initial period to arrest rapid moisture loss and prevent irreversible geometric defects [3]. Practitioners must integrate shrinkage control measures, such as internal curing and optimized joint configurations, into design standards to limit tensile stress buildup and reduce the potential for longitudinal cracking in widened slabs [16].

Limitations — The relative importance of drying shrinkage cannot be definitively quantified due to the reliance on a modest data set that limits statistical robustness [3]. Uncertainties regarding how early-age environmental conditions influence initial pavement smoothness constrain the ability to draw firm conclusions about drying shrinkage effects [3]. The findings may not generalize to other concrete mixes or shrinkage conditions because the research was confined to a single mix without assessing behavior prior to crack formation [10].

Mix Design Strategies for Shrinkage Control

Drying shrinkage in cementitious materials results from moisture loss during hardening, with the magnitude largely determined by the volume of cement paste present [12]. The inclusion of supplementary cementitious materials allows for mix design adjustments to control this behavior, as different additives like fly ash and slag interact with the hydration process in distinct ways that can either mitigate or exacerbate dimensional changes [12].

Research problem — The use of supplementary cementitious materials in ternary mixes creates unpredictable variations in drying shrinkage, requiring engineers to identify specific blend combinations that minimize dimensional instability and cracking [13] [14] [20]. Incorporating recycled concrete aggregates increases the potential for excessive drying shrinkage, necessitating targeted adjustments to mix proportions and admixture dosages to restore durability [18]. High cementitious content, particularly with slag, elevates shrinkage risks while latex-modified overlays suffer from pronounced cracking, highlighting the need for optimized mix designs that balance strength and volume stability [19] [27].

State of the art — The prevailing mix design strategy for controlling drying shrinkage relies on ternary blends of Portland cement with supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash and slag, to balance opposing effects on the cementitious matrix [12] [13]. Practitioners routinely target pozzolan levels of 40–50% of the total cementitious content to limit free-shrinkage strains below 500 µε at 28 days [14] [20]. For specialized applications like latex-modified or self-consolidating concretes, mitigation strategies are refined through extended curing regimes, internal-curing agents, and the incorporation of shrinkage-reducing admixtures [22] [27].

Findings from the reports

  • Revealed that incorporating supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) into ordinary Type I Portland cement consistently lowers 28-day drying shrinkage compared to pure cement mixes [14] [20].
  • Demonstrated that self-consolidating concrete (SFSCC) exhibits substantially greater drying shrinkage than conventional pavement concrete of comparable compressive strength [22].
  • Found that slag increases drying shrinkage while fly ash reduces it, allowing ternary blends to balance these opposing effects for controlled shrinkage performance [12].
  • Showed that the type of cement base strongly influences shrinkage in ternary blends, with Type I/II cements tending to increase shrinkage relative to controls while many Type I or blended cement mixes reduce it [13].
  • Measured an ultimate drying shrinkage strain of approximately 454 µε for typical Iowa pavement concrete, with half this value attained after about 32 days [29].
  • Observed that concrete incorporating recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) shows noticeably higher drying shrinkage than conventional mixes due to the RCA’s greater porosity and mortar content [18].
  • Reported that nano-clay additives modify SFSCC shrinkage behavior, with Actigel increasing strain and Metamax reducing it in proportion to dosage [22].
  • Demonstrated that incorporating a shrinkage-reducing admixture (SRA) effectively mitigates the elevated drying shrinkage of SFSCC, with higher dosages further reducing strain and eliminating cracking [22].
  • Found that early-age cracking in LMC-VE overlays was attributed to shrinkage under traffic loading combined with rapid heat evolution and thermal stresses, despite overall strain levels being comparable to conventional concrete [27].

Novelty & rigor — Novel mix design strategies for drying-shrinkage control include the use of ternary cementitious blends to neutralize shrinkage effects, performance-based proportioning that treats shrinkage as a primary target rather than an afterthought, and the integration of specific admixtures like latex or nano-clays to modulate deformation [12] [22] [27]. These approaches are distinguished by their use of innovative testing frameworks, such as coupling free-prism measurements with restrained-ring tests to derive cracking-potential ratios, and the application of large-scale quantitative databases to map chemical compositions directly against shrinkage performance [13] [20] [22]. The reproducibility of these methods relies on strict adherence to standardized material characterization and mixing protocols, alongside specific curing regimes that may deviate from conventional norms to better simulate field conditions or accelerate testing timelines [12] [14] [20] [27].

Across the reviewed reports, mix design strategies for shrinkage control primarily involve optimizing cementitious constituents, where supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash and slag generally reduce drying shrinkage relative to pure Portland cement, although specific cement types (e.g., Type I/II) or high slag replacement levels can increase it. [12] [13] [14] [18] [20] [27] [29]

Implications — Mix designers should prioritize the careful selection and proportioning of supplementary cementitious materials, such as incorporating fly ash or balancing slag with fly ash in ternary blends, to effectively offset shrinkage introduced by cementitious content [12]. Designers must account for the specific chemistry of the base cement when using supplementary materials, as certain blends can increase shrinkage rather than reduce it, necessitating testing to ensure dimensional stability [13] [14] [20]. To mitigate excessive shrinkage and cracking risks associated with specific materials like highly absorptive aggregates or recycled concrete aggregates, practitioners should employ optimized water content, limit absorbent sources, and utilize chemical admixtures [17] [18]. For specialized mixtures such as semi-flowable self-consolidating concrete, engineers must deliberately select low-shrinkage fine materials and apply sufficient dosages of shrinkage-reducing admixtures to counteract inherently higher deformation tendencies [22].

Limitations — Laboratory results may not accurately predict field performance, as discrepancies between controlled curing and real-world conditions like rapid heat evolution or early traffic loading can lead to unexpected cracking [27]. Statistical correlations between mix chemistry and shrinkage are often weak due to narrow material ranges, requiring further testing before definitive conclusions about mix design adjustments can be drawn [13] [27]. Measurement reliability is compromised by early specimen cracking in restrained tests, inconsistent behavior across different cement types, and sparse data collection that limits the ability to make universal predictions for ternary blends or specific regional mixes [14] [20] [22] [29].

Advanced Mitigation Techniques: Internal Curing and SAPs

Research problem — Shrinkage-induced cracking in concrete bridge decks compromises durability, accelerates deterioration, and increases lifecycle costs [23]. There is a need to investigate internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate as a method to supply water from within the mix, thereby reducing dimensional change due to shrinkage and lowering the stress rate that drives crack formation [23].

State of the art — In broader practice, internal curing using superabsorbent polymers is the prevailing strategy for reducing drying-shrinkage rates, with international guidelines endorsing their use as admixtures at dosages of 0.3%–0.6% of binder mass [26]. While SAP-modified concretes can achieve significant shrinkage reductions, the effectiveness of this technique is highly sensitive to mix design parameters such as water-to-binder ratio and ambient conditions, necessitating careful specification of internal curing regimes [26].

Findings from the reports

  • Internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate reduced the risk of early-age cracking by lowering ultimate drying-shrinkage values compared to conventionally cured concrete [23] [25].
  • Incorporating superabsorbent polymers into concrete mixes reduced early-age drying shrinkage during the first week of curing by retaining water within the matrix [26].
  • Internal curing with lightweight fine aggregate improved permeability and surface resistivity, which extended the estimated service life of the concrete [23].
  • The use of superabsorbent polymers decreased compressive strength and electrical resistivity due to incomplete absorption of extra mixing water [26].
  • Internal curing mitigated the adverse impact of wider joint spacing on long-term distress performance by reducing the coefficient of thermal expansion, modulus of elasticity, and ultimate shrinkage [25].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a field-scale validation of internal curing using pre-soaked lightweight fine aggregate, demonstrating that this method effectively lowers shrinkage strain rates and cracking potential in bridge decks without altering the water-to-cement ratio [23]. A distinct contribution is the systematic quantification of superabsorbent polymers as internal curing agents, where specific absorption kinetics and particle sizes are used to calibrate dosages that mitigate early-age shrinkage while maintaining workability [26]. The methodologies for both approaches are highly reproducible, relying on standardized restrained ring testing protocols and precise mix preparation sequences involving specific aggregate replacement ratios or polymer dosage calculations based on measured hydration properties [23] [26].

Across the reviewed reports, internal curing techniques using lightweight fine aggregates and super-absorbent polymers consistently demonstrate a capacity to mitigate early-age drying shrinkage, with SAPs specifically reducing shrinkage during the first week of curing by retaining water within the matrix. While these mitigation strategies effectively lower ultimate shrinkage values and stress rates to reduce cracking potential, they introduce trade-offs in mechanical properties, such as slightly reduced stiffness or compressive strength, depending on the specific internal curing agent employed. Implications — Internal curing significantly reduces cracking potential and extends service life, allowing for durable performance in lightweight concrete decks [23]. Reduced shrinkage strain enables designers to increase joint spacing or use thinner slabs without compromising structural integrity, resulting in material savings and lower maintenance needs [25]. While super-absorbent polymers effectively mitigate early-age cracking by retaining water, their application requires careful dosage control to balance shrinkage benefits against potential reductions in compressive strength [26].

Limitations — Economic evaluations of internally cured concrete often exclude savings from reduced shrinkage and durability improvements, relying on conservative assumptions about maintenance costs that may understate actual benefits [25]. The effectiveness of internal curing in reducing long-term drying shrinkage remains conditional on variable construction costs, discount rates, and maintenance expenses used in financial models [25]. Superabsorbent polymers may compromise compressive strength and resistivity if their water absorption and release are not reliably controlled during mix design [26].

Fiber Reinforcement for Shrinkage Mitigation

State of the art — Current practice mitigates drying shrinkage stresses by incorporating low dosages of high-aspect-ratio microfibers, such as glass, polypropylene, PVA, or aramid, to increase tensile capacity and bridge early strains [1]. Evaluation relies on restrained ring tests per ASTM C1581, often supplemented by digital image correlation, with evidence showing that specific fiber volume fractions can maintain cracking potential below critical thresholds to prevent crack formation [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Incorporating polypropylene microfibers into fiber-reinforced concrete markedly reduced the propensity for drying-shrinkage cracking without significantly altering the rate or ultimate magnitude of shrinkage strain [1].
  • Fiber reinforcement effectively suppressed crack initiation in restrained-shrinkage ring tests, with specimens exhibiting no sudden drop in strain and maintaining cracking potential below the critical threshold of 1.0 throughout the testing period [1].
  • Higher fiber contents produced fewer and narrower cracks, demonstrating that sufficient reinforcement could eliminate visible drying-shrinkage cracks entirely [1].
  • Shorter fibers demonstrated greater effectiveness than longer ones at equal volume fractions in mitigating shrinkage damage [1].
  • High-aspect-ratio microfibers outperformed macrofibers in reducing drying-shrinkage effects [1].
  • Material type significantly influenced shrinkage mitigation performance, with aramid fibers reducing drying-shrinkage strain and PAN fibers achieving substantial reductions in autogenous shrinkage strain at specific dosages [1].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a quantitative cracking-potential metric that evaluates the ratio of maximum shrinkage-induced stress to splitting tensile strength, providing a rigorous threshold for crack-free performance [1]. This approach is distinct because it replaces conventional chemical admixtures with polypropylene microfibers and employs a specialized chalk-based speckle pattern for digital image correlation to ensure accurate strain mapping during drying [1]. The methodology ensures high reproducibility through detailed specifications for mix proportions, curing regimes, restrained-ring testing standards, and precise calculation procedures using companion cylinder data [1].

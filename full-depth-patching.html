Full-Depth Patching

Part of: Repair & Recycling is-repair-method-of
Full-Depth Patching is a repair method grouped under Repair & Recycling.
concrete-pavementconstructionmaintenancerehabilitation
Also known as: PCC patching, concrete patch, concrete pavement patching, full-depth patch
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Full-depth patching is a concrete repair technique that involves removing the entire thickness of a deteriorated slab section and replacing it with new material. The process requires preparing the underlying subgrade and ensuring proper drainage to support the structural integrity of the replacement [1]. Load-transfer devices, such as dowels extending from the existing pavement into the new patch, are installed to maintain load continuity across the joint [1]. These measures facilitate adequate early strength development and allow for earlier reopening of the repaired section to traffic [1].

Key findings

  • Deeper full-depth patches accelerated early strength development due to increased heat generation from cement hydration, as evidenced by higher maturity values [1].
  • Maturity testing proved reliable for predicting when target flexural or compressive strengths are reached, providing a useful tool for determining opening-to-traffic times [1].
  • Patch thickness, concrete mix type, and early traffic loading duration produced no measurable difference in load-transfer deflection or visual distress [1].
  • Slab thickness and bond condition dominated the fatigue performance of full-depth patching systems [2].
  • A bonded design with a thinner slab demonstrated that anticipated loads remained well within design limits, resulting in zero concrete and asphalt fatigue percentages [2].
  • An unbonded whitetopping design predicted essentially no concrete fatigue contribution while consuming approximately thirty percent of the allowable asphalt fatigue over a twenty-year traffic forecast [2].
  • Stiffness assessments revealed wide variability in dynamic and static values for unbonded layers, with radius of relative stiffness spanning from the low thirties to over one hundred [2].
  • Sections of existing concrete pavement exhibiting vertical movement required identification, marking, and treatment via full-depth patching before placing a concrete overlay [3].

Application Contexts in Pavement Systems

Full-depth patching involves removing the entire thickness of a deteriorated concrete slab and replacing it with new material to restore structural integrity [1]. The process requires preparing the underlying subgrade and installing load-transfer devices, such as dowels, to ensure continuity across the joint between old and new pavement sections [1].

Research problem — There is a need to systematically assess the condition of existing full-depth patches to determine if they have failed, as their status dictates whether a thin concrete overlay is viable or if more extensive reconstruction is required [2]. Research must address how to select an appropriate whitetopping thickness that satisfies long-term traffic forecasts while ensuring concrete stresses and asphalt strains remain within allowable limits to prevent premature failure [2]. It is necessary to identify and repair sections of existing pavement exhibiting vertical movement, such as severely tented joints, before overlay placement to prevent undermining the structural integrity of the new layer [3].

State of the art — Full-depth patching is currently implemented as a targeted repair step within concrete overlay projects, where sections exhibiting vertical movement or severely tented joints are identified through coring and falling-weight deflectometer testing before being marked for repair during milling [3]. Standard practice employs bonded or unbonded overlays with dowel bars restricted to wheel paths, while recent advancements incorporate geotextile fabrics, accelerated high-early-strength mixes, and ground-penetrating radar to verify dowel placement [3].

Findings from the reports

  • Identified that existing concrete pavement sections exhibiting vertical movement, such as severely tented joints, required full-depth patching prior to the placement of a concrete overlay [3].

Novelty & rigor — The approach introduces a novel integration of full-depth patching as a mandatory pre-overlay step, ensuring that vertical movement and joint issues are resolved within the same project scope rather than deferred [3]. Reproducibility is achieved through a distinct procedural sequence involving milling, targeted inspection for vertical displacement, and the use of separate contract items with specific pay quantities to detail repairs prior to overlay placement [3].

Implications — Designers must explicitly identify vertical movement in existing concrete as a condition requiring full-depth patching prior to overlay placement [3]. Contract documents should include specific details and pay items for these repairs to ensure proper budgeting and scheduling [3]. Construction crews must inspect and flag affected locations after milling to guarantee that joint replacements or other full-depth repairs are executed according to standard design procedures [3].

Limitations — The available guidance does not explicitly identify specific limitations or reservations regarding the application of full-depth patching in pavement systems [3].

Structural Performance and Mechanical Behavior

Research problem — Full-depth concrete patches frequently experience premature failure when opened to traffic before achieving adequate strength, resulting in cracking and settlement into the subgrade [1]. There is a need to address this early deterioration to mitigate increased construction costs, extended traffic delays, and compromised long-term pavement durability [1].

Rebound number measurements for concrete patches of varying thicknesses over time. [1]

The figure plots rebound numbers against the time after placing (days) to evaluate the strength development of full-depth concrete patches with different thicknesses.

State of the art — Current full-depth patching practice relies on engineered dowel load transfer systems, specific mix designs balancing water-cement ratios with optional accelerators, and maturity-based monitoring to ensure early strength development [1]. Field performance data indicates that these methods consistently achieve load transfer efficiencies exceeding 90 percent of the applied load [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Increased full-depth patch thickness accelerated early strength development because the larger mass generated more heat of hydration, resulting in higher maturity values [1].
  • Maturity testing reliably predicted the flexural strength required for traffic opening, with deeper patches reaching target maturity thresholds sooner than thinner ones [1].
  • Load transfer remained consistently high (above 90%) across all tested patch depths and mix types, showing no discernible performance differences based on depth or opening time [1].
  • Visual inspections revealed no measurable difference in distress among patches of varying depth, concrete mix type, or traffic opening times [1].
  • Unbonded whitetopping designs consumed approximately thirty percent of the allowable asphalt fatigue life over a twenty-year forecast while producing essentially zero concrete fatigue accumulation [2].
  • Bonded whitetopping configurations with thinner slabs demonstrated zero percent fatigue accumulation for both concrete and asphalt layers under anticipated loads [2].
  • Tensile stress in the concrete slab remained negligible, with low microstrain values indicating that the slab carried essentially no tensile load [2].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a design methodology that treats whitetopping overlay thickness as an optimization variable, linking substrate stiffness metrics to fatigue-based service life predictions for both bonded and unbonded slab configurations [2]. A distinct approach utilizes maturity testing to correlate patch depth with heat of hydration, enabling early traffic opening based on time-temperature factors rather than fixed curing periods or chemical accelerants [1].

Implications — Designers should prioritize bonded full-depth patching configurations to effectively eliminate fatigue damage in both the concrete overlay and underlying asphalt layers, thereby allowing for reduced structural thickness without compromising long-term durability [2]. Construction specifications can leverage maturity-based testing protocols to determine safe traffic opening times based on actual strength development rather than fixed curing periods, which helps mitigate construction delays despite the lack of performance sensitivity to patch depth or mix type [1].

Comparison of deflections for bonded layers and unbonded layers [2]

Limitations — The durability of the unbonded whitetopping design is limited by projected asphalt fatigue consumption over a twenty-year service life [2]. General adoption of the approach is constrained by a lack of established lower limits for acceptable concrete maturity values required to ensure reliable patch performance [1].

Air Entraining Agent

Part of: Chemical Admixtures for Concrete is-type-of
Air Entraining Agent is a type of Chemical Admixture for Concrete.
admixtureair-entrainmentconceptconcretefreeze-thaw
Also known as: AEA, air-entraining admixture
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Air-entraining agents are chemical admixtures added to concrete mixtures to generate and stabilize a controlled system of microscopic air bubbles within the hardened cement paste. These discrete voids function as internal relief chambers that accommodate the volumetric expansion of water during freezing, thereby reducing hydraulic and osmotic pressures that would otherwise cause cracking and structural damage [1] [2] [3]. The agents operate by lowering the surface tension of mixing water and adsorbing at the air-water interface to prevent bubble coalescence, resulting in a uniform distribution of spherical voids that are typically small enough to provide effective protection [1] [3]. Beyond enhancing freeze-thaw durability and resistance to deicing chemicals, the entrained air network improves workability by reducing mixture viscosity and bleeding while interrupting continuous water pathways [4] [5] [6]. The effectiveness of an air-entraining agent is determined by the quality of the resulting void system, specifically characterized by total air content, specific surface area, and spacing factor, which must be carefully balanced against potential reductions in compressive strength [7] [2] [8].

Air Entraining Agent refers to chemical admixtures that improve concrete’s resistance to freezing and thawing cycles by stabilizing internal void structures. Micro Air® achieves this performance by introducing microscopic air voids into the mixture, while Vinsol Resin delivers similar durability benefits through the introduction of microscopic air bubbles.

Key findings

  • Achieving a target total-air percentage is insufficient for durability; controlling bubble size, distribution, and spacing factor is critical because high air content does not guarantee acceptable void structure [9] [2] [10].
  • Air-entraining agents are indispensable for freeze-thaw durability, as performance is governed almost entirely by the resulting air-void system rather than the specific binder type [7] [11].
  • Conventional fresh-concrete tests that measure only total air are inadequate for quality control; real-time monitoring tools like the Air Void Analyzer (AVA) enable dosage adjustments to improve bubble spacing and durability [2] [10].
  • Air entrainment reduces compressive strength, but finer, well-dispersed voids mitigate this penalty while enhancing freeze-thaw resistance and scaling durability [5] [8].
  • Secondary sulfate deposits or ettringite growth can fill entrained voids during exposure to deicing solutions, effectively nullifying the protective air-void system and reducing durability [2] [12].
  • Air-entraining agents interact with other mix components in ways that affect workability and setting; for example, they can reduce thixotropy in self-consolidating concrete or cause compatibility issues with low water-to-cement ratios and high water-reducer doses [13] [6].
  • Different air-entraining agents exhibit distinct performance characteristics; synthetic detergent-based agents are potent for roller-compacted concrete but lack retarding properties, while Vinsol resin avoids adverse reactions with low-alkali cements compared to some synthetic alternatives [1] [2].
  • The presence of supplementary cementitious materials like slag or fly ash affects air-entrainment requirements; slag often necessitates higher AEA dosages, while modest replacements with Class C fly ash do not noticeably alter hardened-air characteristics [2] [8].

Air-Void System Characteristics and Durability Performance

Evidence: 24 reports (18 primary)

Air-entraining agents function by introducing a stable network of microscopic, discrete bubbles into the cement paste, which act as internal relief chambers to accommodate water expansion during freezing and thereby mitigate hydraulic pressures that cause cracking [2] [3] [11]. The durability performance of this system is governed by specific geometric characteristics, including total air volume, bubble size distribution, and the spacing factor, which defines the maximum distance between voids to ensure adequate protection throughout the matrix [7] [2]. While these voids significantly enhance resistance to freeze-thaw cycles and improve workability by reducing paste viscosity, their presence inherently reduces compressive strength, requiring a careful balance between air content criteria and structural performance [7] [6].

The relationship between these durability metrics and permeability is illustrated in the figure below.

Durability factor as a function of rapid chloride ion permeability [7]

The figure plots the Durability Factor against Charge Passed for concrete mixtures containing fly ash and/or inert particles, comparing samples with and without an air-entraining agent (AEA). Concrete without AEA exhibits a strong inverse relationship where durability drops precipitously as chloride permeability increases. In contrast, concrete with AEA maintains high durability factors regardless of the charge passed, demonstrating that “Air entrained concrete may have a relatively high chloride ion permeability but maintain high F-T durability.”

Research problem — Achieving a stable, uniformly spaced microscopic air-void system is critically undermined by incompatibilities between air-entraining agents and supplementary cementitious materials or chemical admixtures, leading to unstable air content and compromised freeze-thaw durability [14] [2] [3] [15] [16]. Current quality-control practices fail to ensure long-term performance because they rely on total air content measurements that poorly correlate with the actual void geometry required for durability and lack real-time verification of critical parameters like spacing factor [17] [9] [2] [15] [10]. The industry lacks a comprehensive understanding of how air-entraining agents interact with fresh-state properties and specialized mix designs, creating significant trade-offs between maintaining structural strength, ensuring workability, and securing freeze-thaw resistance [7] [1] [15] [18] [6].

The figure below illustrates how the durability factor varies with the water-to-binder ratio.

Durability factor as a function of w/b ratio for concrete with and without air-entraining agents. [7]

The figure plots the durability factor against the water-to-binder (w/b) ratio, showing that concrete mixes containing an air-entraining agent (AEA) maintain high durability factors regardless of the w/b ratio. In contrast, concrete without AEA exhibits a sharp decline in durability as the w/b ratio increases, with specific limiting ratios identified for different mix types to achieve a target durability factor of 85%. This demonstrates that for air-entrained concrete, freeze-thaw durability depends more on the entrained air system than on the water-to-binder ratio.

State of the art — The industry has shifted from relying solely on total air content to performance-based specifications that mandate a uniform microscopic void system, typically targeting an entrained air volume of 5% to 8% alongside strict limits on the spacing factor and specific surface to ensure freeze-thaw durability [2] [3] [10]. Verification practices now prioritize real-time or rapid assessment of void characteristics using tools like the Air Void Analyzer and Surface Air Meter to monitor bubble spacing and stability during construction, allowing for immediate mix adjustments that traditional core testing cannot provide [2] [15] [10]. Current research focuses on mitigating the inherent trade-off between durability and strength by refining admixture chemistry and process control to maintain fine void distribution in complex mixes containing supplementary cementitious materials or synthetic surfactants [19] [3] [8].

Findings from the reports

  • Achieving target freeze-thaw durability required a minimum entrained air content of approximately 6% combined with a spacing factor below roughly 0.28 mm (AVA) or 0.22 mm (RapidAir), as these parameters decoupled durability from the need for ultra-high strength and low permeability [7].
  • Conventional fresh-concrete air tests that measured only total air content proved inadequate for predicting durability, whereas real-time monitoring with an Air Void Analyzer (AVA) enabled dosage adjustments that improved bubble spacing and met strict spacing-factor limits [2].
  • Air-entraining agents generated a finer air-void system when combined with water reducers, which lowered bleeding rates and produced specific surface values that indicated enhanced resistance to freeze-thaw damage in ternary cementitious blends [5].
  • Synthetic detergent-based air-entraining agents achieved high entrained-air levels and significant water reduction in roller-compacted concrete but lacked retarding properties, causing early strength gains followed by declines as compactibility was lost within two hours [1].
  • Secondary ettringite deposits frequently filled the finer entrained pores during exposure to sulfate-bearing deicing solutions, reducing effective air content and pushing spacing factors beyond acceptable limits even when initial void quality met severe exposure criteria [12].
  • Air-entraining agents reduced the thixotropy of fresh cement paste in semi-flowable self-consolidating concrete, which diminished shape-holding ability but allowed faster viscosity recovery after shearing while maintaining acceptable freeze-thaw durability [6].
  • Air-void measurements obtained with the Air Void Analyzer exhibited considerably higher variability than standard laboratory tests, indicating that high total-air values did not guarantee acceptable spacing factors and highlighting the need for calibrated acceptance limits [9].
  • The choice of air-entraining agent governed void quality and durability outcomes, with fine-bubble agents producing smaller, closely spaced voids that mitigated strength penalties and improved resistance to deicer scaling compared to traditional resin-based agents [8].
  • Field investigations demonstrated that air-entraining performance was highly inconsistent across pavement cores, with only a small fraction of specimens developing adequate void structures due to formulation choices and execution errors that led to uneven bubble distribution [3].
  • Superabsorbent polymers generated a more stable air-void structure than traditional chemical agents and mitigated the negative impact of extra mixing water on mechanical performance, offering an alternative physical mechanism for air entrainment [20].

Novelty & rigor — Research has shifted from isolated admixture testing to systematic frameworks that quantify how air-entraining agents interact with complex ternary cementitious systems and water reducers, revealing critical trade-offs in void structure and set time [4]. Methodological innovations now emphasize real-time, on-site monitoring of the air-void system using tools like the Air Void Analyzer and Sequential Pressure Method to detect compatibility issues and air loss during placement that traditional laboratory tests miss [2] [10]. The field is advancing by establishing performance-based criteria that link fresh-state void metrics directly to long-term durability, challenging the conventional reliance on fixed dosage or total air content in favor of measurable spacing and specific surface targets [17] [10].

While conventional fresh-air tests often fail to predict hardened performance due to placement losses and measurement variability, real-time monitoring tools like the Air Void Analyzer (AVA) or Surface Air Meter (SAM) enable dosage adjustments that improve bubble spacing and mitigate the risk of inadequate void systems. The choice of air-entraining agent significantly influences void quality and durability outcomes, as synthetic detergent-based or fine-bubble agents generally produce superior spacing factors and scaling resistance compared to traditional Vinsol resin, which tends to yield coarser bubbles and requires higher dosages. Despite the benefits of air entrainment, increasing air content consistently reduces compressive strength, creating a trade-off where durability gains must be balanced against mechanical performance losses that can reach up to 40% at higher void volumes. Durability is further compromised by secondary ettringite formation and sulfate ingress, which can fill fine air voids and nullify the protective effects of entrainment even when initial spacing factors meet design criteria. [1] [2] [12] [10] [8] [6]

Implications — Specifications must shift from relying on simple total air content targets to enforcing performance-based criteria that verify the micro-void structure, such as spacing factor and specific surface, through hardened-state testing [2] [3] [10] [22] [23]. Designers must balance the durability benefits of air entrainment against its inevitable reduction in compressive strength and rheological stability by carefully selecting agents that produce fine, uniformly spaced voids [7] [5] [1] [8] [6]. Successful implementation requires holistic quality control that integrates real-time monitoring of the air-void system with rigorous compatibility testing to account for interactions between agents, cement chemistry, and supplementary materials [19] [2] [12] [3] [11] [25].

Limitations — Achieving the target spacing factor does not guarantee freeze-thaw durability, as high specific surface values are often required and may be difficult to attain with certain supplementary cementitious materials [4] [5]. Standard laboratory scaling tests can be overly aggressive for slag-containing concretes, leading to poor correlation with actual field performance and deicer-scaling resistance [8] [22]. Measurement inconsistencies between different air-void analysis methods and the inability of fresh-mix tests to predict hardened-system quality limit confidence in durability assessments [7] [2] [10].

Advanced Application Challenges

Evidence: 3 reports (3 primary)

Research problem — Research must address how excessive dosing of air-entraining agents interacts with high-range water reducers in low water-to-cement systems to cause severe setting retardation and delayed strength development [13]. Investigation is needed into the compatibility challenges arising from incorporating recycled asphalt, which alters entrapped air volumes and complicates the precise control of air-void content required for pavement durability [26].

The following figure illustrates isothermal test results of mortar containing normal and overdosed chemical admixtures.

Isothermal test results of mortar with normal and overdosed chemical admixtures [13]

The figure displays isothermal calorimetry curves comparing the rate of heat generation over time for two different water-to-cement ratios. A mixture with a w/c ratio of 0.45 exhibits an early hydration peak, whereas a low w/c mixture of 0.30 containing overdosed AEA and WR shows a significantly delayed primary peak. The overdosed low w/c sample also exhibits a secondary major hydration peak at approximately 60 hours, indicating severe setting retardation.

Findings from the reports

  • Demonstrated that combining low water-to-cement ratios with heavily overdosed air-entraining agents and excessive water reducers caused significant incompatibility, shifting hydration peaks and prolonging set times and early strength development [13].
  • Observed that using air-entraining agents within normal dosage levels in standard mixes did not produce the compatibility issues seen with overdosing and low water-to-cement ratios [13].
  • Found that incorporating reclaimed asphalt into lower pavement lifts increased air-entrainment levels, requiring only minimal additional air-entraining admixture to mitigate the effect [26].
  • Identified inadequate or improper air entrainment as a key durability risk for concrete overlays, linking it to material-related distress in poorer-performing sections [27].
  • Established that proper air entrainment systems are essential cross-cutting factors for extending pavement service life when combined with high-quality materials and controlled water-to-cement ratios [27].

Novelty & rigor — Research indicates that managing air-entrainment in complex pavement structures, such as those incorporating recycled asphalt in lower lifts, requires only minimal adjustments to admixture dosages rather than significant increases [26].

Across the reviewed reports, advanced application challenges manifest as distinct compatibility and control issues depending on mix complexity, ranging from severe hydration disruption under extreme dosage conditions to subtle air-void fluctuations when incorporating recycled materials. While standard mixing proportions generally avoid incompatibility, combining low water-to-cement ratios with overdosed agents and high superplasticizer levels significantly delays hydration peaks and strength development, whereas the presence of reclaimed asphalt in base layers necessitates precise, minimal adjustments to maintain target air-entrainment levels. These operational sensitivities underscore that maintaining proper air entrainment is a critical cross-cutting factor for durability, as inadequate control can lead to material-related distress even in otherwise high-performing pavement systems. [13] [26] [27]

Implications — Practitioners must evaluate air-entraining agent performance under site-specific thermal conditions, as excessive dosage or low temperatures can disrupt hydration kinetics and compromise early strength development [13]. Designers should recognize that incorporating recycled materials may require only modest admixture additions to maintain durability, thereby reducing cost and mix-design constraints without sacrificing freeze-thaw resistance [26]. Transportation agencies must treat air entrainment as a critical quality-control prerequisite throughout the entire lifecycle of concrete overlays, since precise selection and dosage management are essential for achieving extended service lives beyond historical benchmarks [27].

Limitations — The incorporation of recycled asphalt into lower concrete lifts complicates the control of air-entrainment by altering the required dosage of the admixture [26]. Achieving acceptable performance in these modified mixtures may require only minimal adjustments to the agent content, indicating a high sensitivity to material composition changes [26].

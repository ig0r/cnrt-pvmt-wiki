Thin Whitetopping

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-rehabilitation-method-of
Thin Whitetopping is a rehabilitation method categorized under Concrete Overlay.
concrete-overlaypavementrehabilitation
Also known as: UTW, ultrathin whitetopping
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-11

Thin Whitetopping is a specific type of concrete overlay rehabilitation method distinguished by its relatively thin profile, typically ranging from 2 to 4 inches [4]. This thickness range serves as a key differentiator from thicker conventional concrete overlays, which are often characterized by dimensions exceeding 6 inches [3]. By utilizing these thinner sections, the technique offers an alternative approach for rehabilitating deteriorated pavements while managing structural characteristics such as joint spacing and activation rates differently than standard thick overlays [4], [3]. It involves placing a thin portland cement concrete (PCC) overlay on an existing asphalt cement concrete (ACC) pavement, typically 2–4.5 inches thick. The first modern ultrathin whitetopping (UTW) project was built in Louisville, Kentucky in 1991. Key design variables include surface preparation (scarification, HMA stress relief layer, broomed/patch), PCC thickness, synthetic fiber reinforcement, joint spacing, and joint sealing. [1]

Design Procedures and Mechanistic-Empirical Models

The PCA/ACPA design procedure for thin whitetopping utilizes a correction term multiplier to account for partial bonding between the PCC and HMA layers when predicting corner stresses; however, because this constant stress multiplier is derived from a single field test location, it can lead to errors during extrapolation [2]. In contrast, the Colorado mechanistic design procedure incorporates equations that adjust theoretical fully bonded concrete stresses using calibration factors derived from field instrumentation and finite element analysis, which account for partial bonding phenomena and temperature curling effects to determine critical flexural stresses [2]. Regarding specific dimensions, the Pavement ME software does not recommend a thin 4-inch thick concrete overlay for joint spacings of 12 feet or larger, as indicated by large increases in predicted IRI values around year 20 to 22 [3].

Comparison of deflections for bonded layers and unbonded layers [2]

The Chang-Majidzadeh model aligns with NCHRP proposed design criteria, indicating that crack spacings smaller than five feet or greater than eight feet are not desirable [4].

Material Enhancements: Fibers and Internal Curing

Internal curing using lightweight fine aggregates (LWFA) can be applied to thin whitetopping and ultra-thin whitetopping (UTW) to reduce built-in stress caused by restrained shrinkage, a technique that may also decrease curling and associated stresses within the overlay [5]. In concrete overlays, structural macrofibers have become the predominant type of structural fiber used rather than steel fibers, as they increase fatigue capacity and fracture toughness while helping to control differential slab movement caused by curling and warping [6]. These fibers also hold cracks tightly together, which can reduce overlay design thickness [6]. Typically dosed at a volume of 0.2% to 0.5%, fiber reinforcement targets a residual strength equivalent to approximately 20% to 30% of the design flexural strength [7].

Design benefits of using FRC [7]

Furthermore, fibers enhance aggregate interlock across joints in overlays too thin for dowel or tie bars, which improves load transfer efficiency and reduces faulting [7]. This reinforcement can be used to extend typical joint spacing designs beyond standard limits, potentially balancing the benefits of shorter slabs while mitigating issues with unactivated joints [7]. Consequently, fiber-reinforced overlays are often constructed without transverse sawcut joints, relying on synthetic macrofibers to provide residual strength and hold cracks tightly together [7].

View of typical transverse crack in joint-free FRC overlay two weeks postconstruction [7]

For ultrathin overlays less than 3.5 inches in depth, the inclusion of poly fibers is recommended to increase performance reliability due to strict construction depth control requirements [8].

Stress Relief Layer Placement

To manage interface conditions, a 1-inch hot mix asphalt (HMA) stress relief layer can be placed over the existing ACC pavement prior to PCC placement [1]. Alternatively, geotextile bond breakers provide a thinner alternative to traditional hot mix asphalt layers, measuring approximately 0.1 inches thick compared to the standard 1-inch HMA layer while achieving equivalent long-term performance [9]. In areas with severe transverse cracking and faulting, bridging techniques using #4 epoxy-coated rebars stapled to the existing slab at 30-inch intervals can be employed as an alternative surface preparation method [1].

Tied Transverse Crack [1]

Temperature differentials can induce interface shear and normal tensile stresses that exceed the partial bond strength of the composite pavement, potentially leading to debonding [2]. Specifically, the maximum interface shear stress for negative temperature differentials could surpass the bond strength range of 155 to 302 psi, depending on surface preparation [2]. Regarding reinforcement placement, the placement of steel reinforcement below mid-depth results in excessive crack opening, while placing it above mid-depth increases crack spacing [4]. Furthermore, optimum crack spacing, crack opening, and steel stress are highly dependent on environmental conditions during curing, particularly air temperature and radiation flux from cloud cover [4].

Temperature differentials applied to the composite pavement [2]

Joint Design and Crack Control

Longitudinal joint forming knives attached to slip-form pavers can separate aggregates to create a weakened concrete cream joint approximately 2 inches deep, eliminating the need for subsequent sawing operations [1]. However, if sawing is delayed, random transverse cracking outside of designated joints can occur, particularly when rapid setting conditions such as high temperatures and wind are present during construction [9]; therefore, early entry sawing for joint establishment should occur as soon as pavement conditions allow, cutting to a depth of T/3 or 1.5 inches [8]. Regarding crack patterns, the Chang-Majidzadeh design criteria can be used to predict crack spacing in continuously reinforced concrete (CRC) pavement structures, with an optimum average crack spacing identified as five feet [4]. Crack spacing tends to be more uniform in thicker continuously reinforced concrete pavements, such as nine-inch slabs, compared to thinner six-inch pavements [4].

Pavement where a crack has formed along the longitudinal joint, but remains tight [9]

Field observations have shown that not all contraction joints activate initially in thin concrete overlays (4-inch to 6-inch), with some joints failing to activate until many years after construction [3]. Furthermore, for shorter slab sizes of 5.5- to 6-foot spacing, MIRA test method sensitivity is affected by temperature, as slabs expand in warmer temperatures causing cracks to close and become undetectable [3].

Percentage of joints activated for different joint spacing of 6-inch thick concrete overlays [3]

Phase III Findings: Iowa Highway 13 Project

The cable-2006-composite-pavement-unbonded-overlays-phase3 project on Iowa Highway 13 (2002–2006) tested 91 sections utilizing three fiber types, three panel sizes, and three surface preparations with 3.5 and 4.5 in. overlays. The project performed well over four years with only minor distress, demonstrating that overlay depths of 3.5 in. or greater can be built without fiber inclusion, though structural fibers enable larger slab sizes (up to 9 ft.) without loss of load transfer [10]. The Iowa Highway 13 project utilized a 28-foot-wide roadway configuration featuring an 8-inch-thickened edge on the outer 5 feet of each side, with the thin concrete surface applied only to the middle 18 feet [1].

Typical Cross Section for the Iowa Highway 13 Project [1]

To monitor strength development, the maturity method is used by inserting temperature probes at specific depths and locations within the overlay; under this method, local traffic can be permitted once flexural strength reaches 350 psi, while construction traffic requires a minimum of 500 psi [1].

Maturity Device Installation [1]

Brick Street Whitetopping Applications

The cable-2006-unbonded-ultrathin-whitetopping-brick-streets project evaluated unbonded ultrathin PCC overlays (3–4.5 in.) on historic brick streets in two Iowa cities. The Oskaloosa project demonstrated that PCC overlays on brick perform well under heavy truck traffic over five years with minimal cracking. No debonding agents were needed. Iowa DOT C-4WR mix with polypropylene fibers (1.5–2.5 lb/cu yd) was recommended for depths under 3.5 in. Joint spacing should not exceed twice the overlay depth in feet for normal mixes. [8]

For brick street applications, a deeper transition section of 6–8 inches is recommended at the interface between the thin overlay and existing pavement to contain the overlay and manage transitions in jointing and depth. [8]

Existing pavement and bull-curb [8]

When constructing thin whitetopping over brick surfaces, concrete loads should be reduced to 6 cubic yards during placement to protect the underlying brick structure from damage. [8]

In single-lane construction scenarios, traffic must be prohibited on the remaining unbuilt brick surface during the strength gain period of the first side. [8]

Southbound lane paving completed [8]

The maturity concept serves as a monitoring tool for tracking concrete strength gain to facilitate early opening of the pavement to public or construction vehicles. [8]

Construction Logistics and Traffic Control

In single-lane construction, adequate strength gain is required before placing the second side, and it is recommended to reduce concrete truck loads to 6 cubic yards to protect the underlying brick surface [8].

Concrete delivery method [8]

Curing Methods for Thin Overlays

Proper curing of thinner concrete overlay sections requires application of curing compound before any surface evaporation occurs; this may necessitate multiple coats in areas with steep profile grades or super elevated cross-slopes to prevent saws from sliding [11].

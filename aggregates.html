Aggregates

Part of: Concrete Materials & Mixtures is-component-of
Aggregates is a structural component of Concrete Materials & Mixtures.
aggregatedurabilityinternal-curinglightweight-aggregatepore-structurerecycled-concrete-aggregate
Also known as: Aggregate Types, Concrete Aggregates, Construction Aggregates, Pavement Aggregates
Created 2026-07-28 · Updated 2026-08-20

Aggregates are granular mineral materials that form the coarse or fine fraction of concrete and road surfaces, providing bulk, strength, and stability to the composite structure. Their performance is governed primarily by petrophysical characteristics such as specific gravity, absorption, mineralogy, particle size distribution, and pore structure, which collectively influence durability under environmental stressors like freeze-thaw cycling [1]. The internal pore architecture, particularly the connectivity of capillary pores, dictates resistance to deterioration mechanisms such as D-crack formation in pavements subjected to repetitive thermal and hydraulic pressures [2]. Classification schemes typically categorize these materials by rock type or by specification limits on absorption and carbonate content to ensure suitability for specific construction applications [1].

Aggregates form the structural backbone of concrete, providing volume, stability, and mechanical strength through a variety of material sources and assessment methods. Carbonate Aggregate serves as the primary filler in mixtures, using crushed limestone or dolostone to minimize shrinkage and lower costs while making up most of the pavement’s weight. Lightweight Aggregate also acts as a structural filler, but employs expanded minerals with lower specific gravity to cut overall density without sacrificing its role as the main aggregate component. Recycled Concrete Aggregate fulfills this same primary filler function by repurposing demolition debris, which reduces material expenses and shrinkage while occupying the bulk of the pavement volume. Beyond material selection, Rock Typing ensures performance by classifying the lithological components of coarse sources to guarantee consistency. Finally, the Iowa Pore Index assesses durability by quantifying pore structure connectivity and suction parameters within coarse aggregates.

Fundamentals

Aggregates function as the granular skeleton in construction composites, providing essential bulk and mechanical stability to the final material. The long-term durability of these materials is fundamentally governed by intrinsic properties such as specific gravity, absorption capacity, pore structure, and mineralogy, which collectively determine resistance to environmental degradation like freeze-thaw cycling [1]. Standard classification systems organize aggregates based on geological rock types or specification limits regarding absorption and carbonate content to ensure suitability for specific engineering applications [1].

Key findings

  • Confirmed that lightweight fine aggregate used for internal curing reduces autogenous and drying shrinkage, thereby improving durability indicators such as permeability and surface resistivity without compromising compressive strength [3] [4].
  • Revealed that the traditional Iowa Pore Index method mischaracterizes pore structure by relying on a fixed macro/micropore split, whereas high-resolution testing shows macropore filling finishes within seconds and varies significantly by lithology [2].
  • Established that the Iowa Pore Index serves as a rapid, nondestructive proxy for mercury porosimetry when calibrated against fundamental geological attributes such as matrix type and mineralogy [5].
  • Identified that recycled concrete aggregate exhibits markedly slower hydraulic conductivity than virgin limestone, creating a trade-off between material reuse and effective moisture management in pavement subgrades [6].
  • Showed that internally cured concrete enables slab thickness reductions while maintaining performance criteria, offsetting the higher material costs through reduced construction volume and deferred maintenance [7].
  • Observed that aggregate porosity and sorptivity drive early-age cracking in low-modulus concrete overlays, requiring careful management of surface texture to ensure adequate bond strength with the substrate [8].
  • Concluded that recycled concrete aggregate can become a mainstream substitute for virgin materials if the industry addresses variability in quality and production logistics through coordinated education and clear guidelines [9].

Durability Assessment and Performance Metrics

Evidence: 6 reports (5 primary)

The durability of aggregates in concrete and pavement applications is fundamentally governed by their petrophysical characteristics, particularly the size and distribution of capillary pores [2]. Pore structures within specific diameter ranges significantly influence susceptibility to premature deterioration mechanisms such as freeze-thaw damage [2]. Performance metrics for durability assessment rely on standardized tests that quantify porosity through pressurized water intrusion to distinguish between durable and non-durable material sources [2].

Research problem — Current bulk testing methods fail to capture the true pore-structure and lithological variability of coarse aggregates, leading to mischaracterization of durability potential and premature pavement failure [5]. The prevailing Iowa Pore Index testing regime contains methodological flaws that cannot reliably capture petrophysical heterogeneity, causing index values to drift from historical benchmarks and resulting in the misclassification of aggregate durability [2]. Existing specifications are likely overly restrictive, rejecting aggregates that could meet performance requirements while failing to account for the uncertain effects of sub-micron pores on freeze-thaw durability in carbonate materials [1].

Cumulative Intrusion versus Pore Size (Specimen 56192-Carbonate) [1]

The figure displays a cumulative intrusion curve plotted against pore size diameter, specifically for a carbonate aggregate specimen. This data represents the results of mercury intrusion porosimetry tests conducted to identify features of the pore structure and relate Iowa pore index test parameters to aggregate pore size distributions.

State of the art — The field has shifted from composition-based legacy limits to integrated rapid testing workflows that combine the Iowa Pore Index with petrographic rock typing and chemical analyses to address source heterogeneity [1] [2] [5]. Current practice utilizes automated IPI systems with lithology-specific transition points rather than a universal time rule, supported by calibration against helium and mercury porosimetry to predict freeze-thaw durability [2] [5]. Emerging consensus favors data-driven composite indices based on measured pore-structure metrics over fixed carbonate-content ceilings, with ongoing research refining secondary-pore index thresholds to improve predictive accuracy [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Demonstrated strong statistical correlations between water absorption and combined Iowa Pore Index metrics (PPI + SPI), establishing that the sum of these indices serves as a robust predictor for aggregate durability across mixed lithologies [1].
  • Observed that visually uniform aggregate sources contain at least three distinct rock types with differing porosities, demonstrating that non-representative sampling can mischaracterize durability and necessitating weighted averaging of rock-type contributions for accurate assessment [5].
  • Evaluated the third-generation Iowa Pore Index apparatus and found that macropore intrusion completes within seconds rather than the traditional minute-long assumption, with transition times varying by lithology to allow for more precise detection of problematic rock fractions [2].
  • Reported that automation of pore-index testing eliminates operator error and reduces soak times, while noting that reducing chamber size increases measured intrusion but also widens sample-to-sample variability, highlighting a trade-off between historical comparability and resolution [2].
  • Measured significantly lower in-situ stiffness and hydraulic conductivity for aggregate subbases than design manuals assume, with recycled concrete aggregate showing markedly slower drainage times compared to virgin limestone despite similar modulus values [6].
  • Found that the surface microtexture of coarse aggregates critically controls bond strength in overlays, where insufficient roughness leads to interface failure while proper preparation ensures durability, illustrating a trade-off between rapid construction and long-term service life [8].

Novelty & rigor — Research has shifted durability assessment from rigid, mineralogy-based acceptance limits toward a performance-driven framework that utilizes rapid pore-structure metrics to predict freeze-thaw resilience across diverse aggregate sources [1]. Methodological innovations include the calibration of bulk Iowa Pore Index measurements against high-resolution porosimetry to quantify total and critical pore volumes, as well as the deployment of automated third-generation platforms that capture sub-second intrusion dynamics for higher sensitivity [2] [5]. Reproducibility is supported by standardized protocols for bulk sample preparation, specific pressure settings during water intrusion, and complementary petrophysical testing, enabling consistent evaluation of pore networks across heterogeneous material types [2] [5].

Across the reviewed reports, aggregate durability is fundamentally governed by pore structure and petrophysical heterogeneity rather than bulk composition alone, with the Iowa Pore Index emerging as a robust proxy for helium porosity that captures critical pore-throat distributions linked to freeze-thaw performance. While carbonate aggregates generally exhibit higher water absorption and finer pore networks than non-carbonates, their durability is determined by specific lithological components within the source, necessitating weighted averaging of rock-type properties to accurately predict field performance. Advanced testing protocols that resolve rapid macropore intrusion dynamics and isolate individual rock types provide higher-resolution durability assessments than traditional fixed-time methods, though they reveal significant variability in hydraulic conductivity and stiffness depending on aggregate gradation and source history. The interplay between aggregate porosity, surface texture, and bond strength creates a trade-off where materials enabling rapid construction or reuse may suffer from increased permeability and reduced freeze-thaw resistance unless substrate preparation is rigorously controlled. Implications — Specifications should transition from prescriptive compositional limits to performance-based criteria that utilize rapid pore-structure screening, such as the Iowa Pore Index, alongside simple physical indicators like specific gravity and absorption [1]. Agencies must adopt lithology-specific testing protocols and weighting methods to account for rock-type heterogeneity, ensuring that durability predictions reflect actual pore-throat distributions rather than relying on single universal thresholds [2] [5]. Designers must balance structural stiffness against hydraulic conductivity when selecting aggregates for pavement layers, implementing supplemental drainage measures where low-permeability materials risk prolonged water retention and frost susceptibility [6].

Limitations — Current acceptance practices rely on metrics that lack universal predictive power, as laboratory tests often fail to replicate field behavior and correlations break down when specific lithologies are isolated [1] [2]. Methodological constraints in pore structure analysis, such as fixed transition points and single-modal values, obscure the heterogeneity of aggregates and limit the accuracy of durability assessments across diverse rock types [1] [2] [5]. Performance evaluations are frequently compromised by incomplete laboratory datasets and modeling assumptions that omit unquantified durability benefits or rely on narrow sample sets that preclude broader generalization [2] [7] [6].

Advanced Aggregate Technologies for Shrinkage Control

Evidence: 4 reports (3 primary)

Advanced aggregate technologies for shrinkage control utilize specialized materials, such as lightweight fine aggregates, to mitigate volume changes in concrete through internal curing mechanisms. These porous particles absorb mixing water and subsequently release it slowly during cement hydration, thereby supplying additional moisture without altering the initial water-to-cement ratio [4]. This internal moisture supply enhances the degree of hydration, which reduces autogenous shrinkage and permeability while maintaining structural integrity [4].

The following figure illustrates calorimetry results for laboratory and field samples.

Calorimetry results for laboratory and field samples [4]

The figure displays temperature profiles over time for various concrete mixtures, including a Field Control Mix, Lab Control Mix, and mixes containing internal curing (IC) agents. The data shows that the mixes containing IC agents exhibit a lower peak temperature and a slower rate of heat evolution compared to the control mixtures.

Research problem — Low water-to-cement concrete mixes frequently suffer from autogenous shrinkage and cracking due to insufficient internal moisture for complete cement hydration, necessitating effective strategies to supply curing water throughout the mix volume [4]. While lightweight aggregates can serve as internal reservoirs to mitigate early-age shrinkage, accurately characterizing and controlling their moisture absorption properties is challenging because unmanaged surface moisture can inadvertently increase the water-to-cement ratio and compromise strength [3]. The widespread use of pre-saturated lightweight fine aggregate for internal curing imposes significant economic, environmental, and mechanical penalties, driving the need to identify alternative internal-curing technologies that maintain durability without these drawbacks [10].

State of the art — Pre-wetted lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) remains the dominant technology for internal curing, with practice codified in standards such as ASTM C1761 and supported by mechanistic-equipment design workflows that balance modest upfront cost premiums against long-term reductions in shrinkage and maintenance [3] [7]. Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are emerging as a tunable alternative to mitigate the handling costs and stiffness reduction associated with LWFA, though their adoption is currently limited by the lack of universal specifications and ongoing challenges regarding early-age strength and workability trade-offs [10].

Findings from the reports

  • Lightweight fine aggregate used for internal curing reduced concrete unit weight, stiffness, coefficient of thermal expansion, and ultimate shrinkage while maintaining compressive strength comparable to conventional mixes [3] [7] [4].
  • Internal curing with pre-wetted lightweight aggregate improved durability indicators by lowering rapid-chloride permeability and increasing surface resistivity [3] [4].
  • The use of internally cured concrete lowered stress development and cracking potential, leading to an estimated service life extension of approximately two decades for bridge decks [3] [4].
  • Internally cured concrete enabled a reduction in pavement slab thickness while meeting performance criteria, offsetting the higher material costs associated with lightweight aggregates [7].
  • Internal curing boosted cement hydration and reduced autogenous, plastic, and drying shrinkage compared to conventional concrete mixes [3].
  • Live-load testing demonstrated that the structural behavior of lightweight aggregate internal curing decks remained indistinguishable from conventional concrete controls in terms of load distribution and composite action [4].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a standardized mixture-design methodology that treats lightweight fine aggregate as a managed internal-curing reservoir, replacing normal-weight fine aggregate to mitigate chemical shrinkage without altering the overall water-to-cement ratio [3]. This approach is distinguished by a reproducible conditioning protocol that ensures consistent moisture release through specific wetting and draining regimes, supported by automated calculation tools for precise saturated-surface-dry accounting [3]. Field validation demonstrates that this aggregate substitution yields comparable mechanical properties while significantly reducing cracking potential and extending service life in structural applications like bridge decks [4].

Implications — Designers can leverage lightweight fine aggregates to significantly extend the service life of concrete structures by mitigating shrinkage-related cracking, provided they account for the modest increase in initial material costs against long-term durability gains [4]. Specifying these materials requires rigorous quality control and adherence to specific absorption thresholds and mix design limits to ensure internal curing functions effectively without compromising workability or strength [3]. Pavement agencies should evaluate aggregate selection through a lifecycle cost lens, recognizing that the capacity of these materials to store water and reduce stiffness can justify higher upfront expenses by enabling thinner sections and reduced maintenance frequency [7].

Limitations — Field performance data indicates that durability benefits from lightweight aggregate internal curing may remain theoretical, as long-term mechanical advantages and service-life extensions have not been empirically validated beyond model assumptions [4]. The technology is currently limited by a lack of standardized specifications that explicitly address safety and curing requirements, necessitating rigorous quality control to ensure aggregates meet specific absorption criteria for effective shrinkage mitigation [3].

Sustainability and Implementation Challenges in Recycled Aggregates

Evidence: 1 report (1 primary)

Research problem — The widespread adoption of recycled concrete aggregate in new paving mixtures is hindered by a complex set of barriers related to compliance, quality consistency, and production logistics [9]. Research must address how agency specifications that often exclude recycled materials, combined with inconsistent material properties and storage challenges, can be overcome to realize the economic and environmental benefits of using these aggregates [9].

State of the art — The industry is actively shifting toward substituting virgin rock with recycled concrete aggregate, yet widespread adoption in new Portland cement concrete remains constrained by variable material quality and logistical hurdles [9]. Current efforts to overcome these barriers focus on refining testing protocols for properties like absorption and soundness while developing performance-based specifications to replace rigid gradation requirements [9].

Findings from the reports

  • Identified compliance with existing specifications for soundness, abrasion, gradation, and strength as a primary barrier to the widespread adoption of recycled concrete aggregate [9].
  • Observed that variability in material quality and the perception of recycled concrete aggregate as waste hinder its acceptance by state departments of transportation [9].
  • Noted that production obstacles, including storage constraints, workability issues, limited supply, and contaminant removal requirements, impede the integration of recycled concrete aggregate into new pavement mixes [9].
  • Demonstrated that coordinated education, clear guidelines, and demonstration projects led by technical working groups can surmount barriers to using recycled concrete aggregate as a mainstream substitute for virgin aggregates [9].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a systematic technology-deployment framework rather than novel materials, aiming to transition recycled concrete aggregates from experimental stages into routine highway paving applications [9]. Methodological rigor is established through a standardized processing protocol that includes contaminant removal, specific crushing techniques, and moisture management strategies to ensure reproducible performance in new concrete mixes [9].

Implications — Recycled aggregates can transition from niche materials to mainstream construction components only through a coordinated, standards-driven industry approach that addresses compliance and quality barriers [9]. Establishing technical working groups and performance-based specifications will enable reliable production methods that unlock sustainability benefits such as reduced landfill waste and lower material costs across all aggregate categories [9].

Limitations — Existing specifications often impose performance criteria on recycled concrete aggregate that are identical to those for virgin materials, creating unnecessary compliance barriers [9]. The material frequently exhibits greater variability in quality and fails standard durability tests, complicating its use in applications requiring consistent structural integrity [9]. Broader adoption is hindered by contractor concerns regarding workability, strength assurance, and warranty risks due to limited experience with the material [9].

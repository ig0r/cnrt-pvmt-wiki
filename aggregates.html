Aggregates

Part of: Concrete Materials & Mixtures is-component-of
Aggregates is a structural component of Concrete Materials & Mixtures.
aggregatedurabilityinternal-curinglightweight-aggregatepore-structurerecycled-concrete-aggregate
Also known as: Aggregate Types, Concrete Aggregates, Construction Aggregates, Pavement Aggregates
Created 2026-07-28 · Updated 2026-08-11

Aggregates constitute a major structural component of concrete materials and mixtures, serving as the primary filler that reduces shrinkage and cost while comprising 75% to 85% of portland cement concrete by weight [5]. This significant proportion highlights their role as the largest line item in pavement construction costs [5]. As granular materials used as structural components in construction, they are distinguished by their mineral composition, physical density, porosity characteristics, or origin. [1]–[3] Specific types include Carbonate Aggregate, which relies on calcite and dolomite content for concrete pavement, and Lightweight Aggregate, which utilizes lower-density materials to reduce weight and facilitate internal curing. [1]–[3] Recycled Concrete Aggregate is derived from crushed existing structures, whereas Rock Typing serves as a petrographic methodology to classify lithological components within coarse sources. [2], [4] The Iowa Pore Index evaluates pore structure connectivity and suction parameters to assess durability, complemented by Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry, which provides detailed reference data on pore size distribution and total porosity through high-pressure mercury injection. [1], [5]

Pore Structure And Durability

Internal curing is a concrete technology designed to maintain internal moisture levels above 90% during early-age hydration, primarily by replacing 20% to 30% of fine aggregate with saturated lightweight fine aggregates (LWFA) or superabsorbent polymers (SAPs). [3], [6] This process mitigates autogenous shrinkage and plastic shrinkage cracking by providing a continuous water reservoir that compensates for chemical shrinkage and capillary pressure buildup. [3], [7] LWFA is generally superior to coarse lightweight aggregates for this purpose because its finer particle size allows for improved spacing at lower substitution rates, enabling more rapid fluid draw and uniform water distribution throughout the paste. [3], [6] This mechanism significantly enhances freeze-thaw durability, reduces permeability, and increases calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) content by approximately 20% at 28 days compared to conventional concrete. [3], [8] Additionally, internal curing alters moisture gradients in pavement slabs, reducing differential drying that causes warping and curling stresses, thereby improving ride quality and extending service life by up to two decades. [9], [10] Superabsorbent polymers offer an alternative mechanism through physico-chemical sorption, functioning as chemical admixtures rather than physical aggregates. [6] SAPs can be mixed directly into concrete without the extensive pre-saturation timelines required for LWFA, with dosages typically ranging from 0.3% to 0.6% of binder mass. [6] The performance of both systems relies on controlled water release; for LWFA, this requires a minimum 48-hour sprinkling period followed by drainage to achieve stable absorption, while SAP desorption rates must be carefully matched to hydration kinetics. [3], [6] Internal curing also improves the efficiency of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), allowing for reduced cement content and lower carbon footprints. [10] The selection and quality of aggregates are critical to durability, particularly regarding freeze-thaw resistance and D-cracking susceptibility. [1], [2] Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (MIP) is used to characterize pore structures, identifying that carbonate aggregates typically have finer pores (median diameter 0.01–0.5 μm) compared to non-carbonate aggregates (0.5–4.5 μm). [1], [2] The critical pore-throat range for deterioration has been refined to 0.02–0.1 μm, with rock typing revealing that heterogeneous sources may contain specific lithologies prone to failure even if the bulk sample appears durable. [2], [5] Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA) presents challenges due to higher porosity and absorption but can be successfully utilized in base layers or new concrete when properly engineered, often requiring quality control measures such as separation by origin and strength to mitigate issues like alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and sulfate soundness failures. [4], [11]

Carbonate aggregate is a type of aggregate characterized by specific gravity values that are typically lower than those of siliceous aggregates [1]. This difference arises because carbonate minerals, such as calcite, possess a lighter density compared to the minerals found in siliceous aggregates [1]. Consequently, carbonates generally exhibit lower specific gravity alongside higher absorption and pore index values when compared to non-carbonate fractions [1].

Aggregates are evaluated for soundness using the Iowa Pore Index test to quantify their resistance to freeze-thaw deterioration [1]. This method serves as a simple estimation of aggregate pore structure, providing an indicator for durability against freezing water pressures that can cause D-cracking in concrete [1]. Developed by the Iowa Department of Transportation and accepted as an AASHTO standard, the test rapidly determines the macropore-to-micropore ratio to help grade coarse aggregates [5].

The inclusion of Lightweight Aggregate within the parent category of Aggregates enables the production of structural lightweight concrete with a hardened density ranging from approximately 90 to 115 pounds per cubic foot. In North America, this water-filled inclusion is typically an expanded lightweight aggregate [3]. This material facilitates internal curing by dispersing curing water throughout the depth of the concrete, which helps prevent autogenous shrinkage in high-strength mixtures [10]. Furthermore, it influences the interfacial transition zone and chloride ingress characteristics in mortars [10].

Aggregates is a classification that encompasses Recycled Concrete Aggregate based on physical properties including density, water absorption, and soundness. The relative density of recycled concrete aggregate is typically lower than that of virgin aggregates due to the presence of attached mortar [4]. Standards for these materials often set primary limits on relative density and absorption, while also requiring compliance with additional criteria such as abrasion resistance and soundness in freeze-thaw environments [4].

Fly Ash

Part of: Supplementary Cementitious Materials is-instance-of
Fly Ash is an instance of Supplementary Cementitious Materials.
fly-ashmaterialspozzolanscm
Also known as: FA, pulverized fuel ash
Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-20

Fly ash is a fine, spherical by-product of coal combustion captured from flue gases that serves as a supplementary cementitious material in concrete. It is classified into Class F and Class C based on its chemical composition, with the former being low-calcium and primarily pozzolanic, while the latter contains higher calcium levels that impart self-cementing or hydraulic properties [1] [4] [18]. In concrete mixtures, fly ash partially replaces Portland cement to improve workability and reduce water demand through its spherical particle morphology [2] [35] [36]. It reacts pozzolanically with calcium hydroxide released during cement hydration to form additional calcium silicate hydrate gel, which refines the pore structure and enhances long-term strength [11] [23] [24]. The incorporation of fly ash modifies the heat of hydration, retards early-age setting, and mitigates deleterious reactions such as alkali-silica expansion while improving overall durability [15] [17] [19].

Key findings

  • Low-calcium Class F fly ash significantly lowers the heat of hydration and delays setting times, whereas high-calcium Class C fly ash can generate excess heat and behave more like ordinary Portland cement [4] [17] [18].
  • Incorporating fly ash reduces the peak temperature of hydration and delays its occurrence, with the magnitude of heat reduction proportional to the calcium oxide content [4] [16] [17].
  • Fly ash enhances fresh-state workability by reducing viscosity and yield stress, allowing for lower water demand due to the spherical shape of its particles [2] [14] [35].
  • Low-calcium Class F fly ash effectively mitigates alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and improves sulfate resistance, while high-calcium Class C fly ash often exhibits poor sulfate resistance and may exacerbate ASR unless used at higher replacement levels with other pozzolans [5] [19].
  • Early-age strength development is retarded by fly ash, particularly with Class C mixes, although strengths often converge or exceed control values at later ages (e.g., 28 days) [4] [18].
  • Fly ash reduces concrete warping and curling compared to plain cement mixes, though excessive replacement levels may increase water demand and counteract these benefits [12] [22].
  • Fly ash refines the pore structure of concrete, resulting in more hydrophobic surfaces and improved resistance to chloride ion penetration and deicing salt damage [15] [25].
  • Fly ash mitigates premature stiffening (false set) and improves plastic behavior by acting as a low-range water reducer, while also reducing drying shrinkage [11].

Fly Ash Applications & Usage

Fly ash is a fine, spherical by-product of coal combustion that serves as a supplementary cementitious material in concrete, partially replacing Portland cement to improve workability and reduce water demand [1] [2] [4] [9] [36]. The material is classified into low-calcium Class F and high-calcium Class C based on its chemical composition, with the former acting primarily as a pozzolan and the latter exhibiting both pozzolanic and hydraulic properties [1] [4] [18] [23]. Through pozzolanic reactions that consume calcium hydroxide to form additional cementitious gel, fly ash refines the pore structure, enhances long-term strength and durability, and mitigates deleterious phenomena such as alkali-silica reaction [11] [23] [24]. Incorporating fly ash modifies the hydration kinetics by lowering heat evolution and retarding early-age setting while improving rheological properties through the lubrication effect of its spherical particles [18] [19] [36].

Research problem — The inherent chemical variability of fly ash, particularly the distinct behaviors of Class C versus Class F materials, creates classification and sourcing challenges that can lead to unpredictable concrete performance and premature durability failures [1] [5] [19]. Incompatibilities between fly ash and other mix components often result in adverse fresh-state properties, such as false set, rapid slump loss, or excessive air content instability, which jeopardize placement quality and early-age strength development [4] [11] [18]. Specific application contexts, including self-consolidating concrete, pervious overlays, and ternary blends, face unique performance gaps regarding rheological control, early strength gain, and the accurate assessment of environmental impacts [9] [14] [34] [35].

State of the art — Fly ash is the dominant supplementary cementitious material in concrete, with modern practice typically replacing 15–30% of Portland cement to enhance durability and reduce carbon emissions [1] [4] [24]. Current specifications classify fly ash into Class F and Class C based on calcium content, with selection driven by performance requirements such as sulfate resistance or early strength development [1] [4] [19]. The field is shifting toward ternary blends incorporating slag and silica fume, supported by high-shear mixing techniques and performance-based specifications that prioritize durability over prescriptive limits [6] [14] [19].

Findings from the reports

  • Incorporating fly ash reduced the viscosity and yield stress of concrete mixes, thereby improving workability and flowability relative to plain cement or slag blends [4] [9] [14] [35].
  • Class F fly ash consistently mitigated alkali-silica reaction (ASR), keeping expansion well below the 0.10% limit, whereas Class C fly ash generally failed to control ASR and produced expansions exceeding this threshold [5] [19] [23].
  • Fly ash delayed the setting time of concrete and mortar, with Class C causing more pronounced retardation than Class F at equivalent replacement levels [4] [19] [24].
  • Early-age compressive strength was often modestly reduced or delayed by fly ash, but long-term strength increased significantly, with some ternary blends exceeding control strengths at later ages [4] [11] [14].
  • Fly ash reduced the heat of hydration and lowered drying shrinkage relative to plain Portland cement mixes [4] [11] [35].
  • Class F fly ash significantly improved sulfate resistance by limiting expansion or inducing net shrinkage, while Class C fly ash markedly weakened durability and caused severe expansion and cracking over extended periods [5] [19].
  • Class C fly ash exhibited broader chemical variability than Portland cement but remained within normal compositional bounds, with no unexpected elemental levels detected in the samples analyzed [5].
  • Replacing approximately one-fifth of Portland cement with Class F fly ash improved resistance to plastic shrinkage and reduced crack widths in bridge-deck concrete applications [2].
  • Detected a mismatch between the designed mix composition and the actual placed material, as no fly ash was found in a core that was specified to contain approximately 15% Class C fly ash [6].

Novelty & rigor — Research has shifted from viewing fly ash as a simple binary replacement to treating it as an integral component of systematically engineered ternary cementitious systems that combine slag and silica fume [1] [19]. Novel methodologies include the development of rapid screening protocols using small paste and mortar specimens to optimize dosages before full-scale casting, as well as high-shear two-stage mixing regimes that reduce viscosity and improve early-age strength [11] [14]. These approaches are distinguished by their rigorous experimental frameworks, which utilize full factorial matrices and advanced instrumentation to quantify performance thresholds for durability, shrinkage control, and pollutant attenuation [2] [24].

Across the reviewed reports, fly ash is consistently utilized as a supplementary cementitious material to enhance fresh-state properties such as workability and flowability while reducing water demand and heat of hydration. While Class F fly ash is widely established for its ability to mitigate alkali-silica reaction and sulfate expansion, Class C fly ash demonstrates variable durability performance, often exhibiting poor resistance to these deleterious reactions unless used at higher replacement levels or in combination with other pozzolans. Incorporating fly ash generally delays early-age strength development and extends setting times, yet it contributes to reduced drying shrinkage and improved long-term compressive strength, particularly when blended with slag in ternary systems. Field and laboratory applications confirm that fly ash can replace approximately 15 to 30 percent of Portland cement without compromising key performance metrics such as permeability and air-void structure, although chemical variability between sources requires careful quality control. Implications — Specifications should mandate batch-by-batch testing and tighter limits for fly ash to manage its inherent chemical variability, ensuring consistent performance across different sources [5]. Designers must carefully select between Class F and Class C ashes based on environmental exposure, utilizing Class F for superior durability against ASR and sulfate attack while restricting Class C in aggressive conditions due to its potential to weaken resistance [5] [19]. Mix designs should incorporate fly ash to enhance workability and reduce early-age thermal cracking risks, but must balance these benefits by adjusting admixture dosages or using supplementary fine materials to offset reduced green strength and potential setting delays [4] [35]. Industry practice should advance the use of ternary blends containing fly ash in performance-based mix design systems to isolate its specific effects on durability and scaling outcomes [2] [6].

Limitations — The practical impact of fly ash composition variations at modest replacement levels remains uncertain, and seasonal source switching may alter concrete behavior in ways not captured by limited laboratory data [11]. Field performance may differ from laboratory results due to climatic conditions, and specific classes of fly ash can exhibit poor durability or incompatibility with certain admixtures, requiring careful screening and project-specific testing [4] [19]. Construction-related variables often dominate observed behavior, making it difficult to isolate the specific influence of fly ash on durability, while certain applications may suffer from reduced early-age strength or ineffective air entrainment [6] [14] [35].

Durability Performance and Freeze-Thaw Resistance

Research problem — It is unclear whether the lower drying shrinkage associated with fly-ash-rich ternary blends delays joint cracking, which complicates early-entry sawing specifications and pavement durability [20]. The necessity of air entrainment for freeze-thaw durability in low-permeability concrete containing fly ash remains uncertain due to the limited freezable water in dense mixes [13]. Standard deicer-scaling tests designed for plain cement produce misleading results for fly-ash blends, while field performance indicates that moderate fly-ash substitution may increase surface scaling under freeze-thaw conditions [26]. Low water-to-cement ratios limit the hydration of supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash, restricting strength and durability gains unless internal curing strategies are employed to mitigate autogenous shrinkage and cracking [27]. The specific impact of Class C fly ash on the resistance of concrete joints to freeze-thaw cycles and deterioration requires clarification to ensure long-term pavement performance in cold climates [28]. It is unknown whether the use of Class C fly ash contributes to extensive longitudinal cracking in widened jointed plain-concrete slabs, affecting service life and maintenance costs [21]. Calcium-oxychloride formation triggered by deicing salts causes expansive paste cracking and premature joint deterioration, necessitating mix designs that suppress this chemical attack to extend pavement durability [15] [25].

State of the art — Class C fly ash is commonly incorporated as a supplementary cementitious material at replacement levels ranging from approximately 10% to 30%, where it refines pore structure and mitigates chemical degradation such as oxychloride formation [25] [28]. Despite these benefits, fly ash is not considered a primary factor in preventing freeze-thaw deterioration, with performance instead dominated by the water-cement ratio, air-void quality, and exposure to deicing salts [28]. Because fly ash alone may not fully protect concrete in aggressive freeze-thaw environments, current practice often combines it with sealers or other admixtures to achieve the desired durability performance [25].

Findings from the reports

  • Field evaluations found no discernible link between the quantity of fly ash in the mix and the frequency or severity of longitudinal cracking in pavements [20] [21].
  • Concrete containing Class C fly ash achieved high freeze-thaw durability (≥85%) when proper air entrainment was used, overriding the need for extremely low water-to-binder ratios [13].
  • Fly ash significantly reduced the formation of expansive calcium oxychloride and slowed paste expansion in concrete exposed to deicing salts [15].
  • The inclusion of Class C fly ash refined the pore structure, resulting in higher water contact angles and lower water absorption rates compared to plain Portland cement concrete [25].
  • Concrete mixes with approximately 33% Class C fly ash replacement exhibited low chloride ion penetrability, reaching a low classification by 56 days [15].
  • Laboratory freeze-thaw tests showed that adding Class C fly ash had little effect on visual damage ratings compared to other variables such as water-to-cement ratio and curing method [28].
  • Concrete mixes containing fly ash showed slightly higher cumulative mass loss after freeze-thaw cycles compared to plain Portland cement controls in inter-laboratory scaling tests [26].

Novelty & rigor — Research has established quantitative limits for water-to-binder ratios and compressive strength that are specific to fly-ash concrete, identifying thresholds below which air entrainment is unnecessary for acceptable freeze-thaw durability [13]. A systematic laboratory matrix isolates the influence of Class C fly ash by varying its replacement level alongside different air-entraining admixtures and deicing-salt exposures to determine its specific impact on freeze-thaw distress [28]. The application of a rigorous de-icing salt freezing protocol generates comparative scaling loss data for high-volume fly ash concretes under conditions that were previously undocumented in inter-laboratory studies [26].

Rheological Properties and Hydration Kinetics

Fly ash functions as a supplementary cementitious material that modifies the thermal profile and reaction rates of cement hydration [17]. Calcium oxide within the ash acts as a sink for calcium ions, thereby slowing the nucleation of hydration products and altering the overall kinetics [17].

Research problem — The research addresses the challenge of maintaining consistent flowability and viscosity in 3D printable concrete mixtures over time to prevent nozzle blockage and ensure reliable layer deposition [36]. It seeks to understand how fly ash incorporation alters the heat-of-hydration profile, specifically regarding early-age reaction retardation and its impact on set times, strength development, and thermal cracking risks [16] [17]. The work investigates the sensitivity of hydration kinetics to variations in fly ash dosage and evaluates the reliability of isothermal calorimetry for detecting these shifts during quality control [16].

The following figure illustrates how curing temperature influences these hydration processes.

Effect of curing temperature on hydration (adapted from Escalante-Garcia et al. 2000) [17]

Higher initial reaction temperatures result in a higher hydration rate at early age, whereas lower initial reaction temperatures lead to slower early reactions but potentially higher cumulative hydration later on.

State of the art — Fly ash is widely utilized as a supplementary cementitious material to enhance rheological stability and extend open time in 3D-printable concrete, with its spherical morphology effectively maintaining viscosity during the printing process [36]. The hydration kinetics of fly ash are characterized by a reduction in peak heat generation and a delay in the main exothermic peak, effects that vary significantly between low-calcium Class F and high-calcium Class C sources [17] [18]. Current industry practice relies on isothermal or semi-adiabatic calorimetry to quantify these thermal effects, using heat-index parameters and derivative curves to predict set times, assess strength development, and mitigate thermal cracking risks in pavement concrete [16] [18].

The impact of Class F fly ash on these thermal profiles is illustrated in the following figure.

Effect of class F fly ash on heat generation (adapted from Kishi and Maekawa 1995) [17]

The figure plots the heat generation rate against time for pure Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) and mixtures containing increasing proportions of Class F fly ash. As the ratio of fly ash increases, the peak heat generation rate is significantly reduced and the time to reach this maximum is delayed compared to pure OPC.

Findings from the reports

  • Incorporating fly ash into cement mixtures consistently lowers the heat released during hydration, shifts the temperature peak to later times, and widens the heat-generation curve [17] [18].
  • Fly ash lengthens both initial and final set times, with the retardation effect becoming more pronounced at higher replacement levels and lower curing temperatures [16] [18].
  • Simple isothermal calorimetry revealed a distinct secondary heat-evolution peak attributable to fly ash hydration that was not observed in semi-adiabatic or IQ-drum tests [16] [18].
  • Different fly ash sources produced distinct early-age hydration curves, with some sources hydrating significantly faster than others and altering the prominence of secondary heat peaks [17] [18].
  • Varying the fly ash content by ±50% did not change the shape of the heat-generation curves but merely shifted them in time, indicating no incompatibility [16].
  • Early-age strength development decreased as fly ash content rose, but strengths converged by 28 days with differences narrowing to less than 10% [18].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a low-cost semi-adiabatic calorimetry approach that replaces expensive laboratory equipment, enabling rapid field interpretation of thermal fingerprints for pavement performance predictions [17]. A novel data-driven framework utilizes weighted average calcium-oxide content as a linear predictor to model heat-signature parameters across ternary cementitious blends, isolating the specific influence of fly ash on hydration kinetics and setting times [4]. The methodology employs newly defined heat-index metrics derived from first-derivative calorimetry curves to quantitatively monitor set development and detect source-specific incompatibilities, offering a fast and repeatable alternative to traditional long-duration testing routines [18].

Quality control protocols should incorporate calorimetric screening to verify compatibility and predict performance, ensuring that variations in fly ash dosage do not compromise the thermal stability or schedule of mass concrete placements [16] [18].

Structural Performance and Subgrade Stabilization

Research problem — The long-term structural integrity of subgrade soils stabilized with fly ash is compromised by expansive mineral formation from sulfur reactions, persistent frost-heave potential despite stabilization, and poor hydraulic drainage that traps moisture [33]. There is a lack of systematic dosage guidelines for incorporating industrial by-products into concrete without sacrificing performance, alongside an operational gap where existing batching systems cannot automatically adjust mix proportions in response to environmental changes during paving [29]. Precise control of fly ash content is critical for mitigating excessive curling and warping in concrete pavements, yet the absence of documented mix-design details hinders the ability to optimize replacement rates for minimizing distress mechanisms [12] [22].

State of the art — Higher dosages are increasingly recognized for delivering performance approaching conventional granular bases, particularly in reducing frost-heave rates and increasing California Bearing Ratio values [33]. Long-term durability remains contingent on managing sulfur content to prevent expansive mineral formation that degrades strength [33].

Findings from the reports

  • Incorporating Class C fly ash to replace approximately 20% of cementitious material improved workability and durability while significantly reducing upward curling and warping compared to mixes with higher cement content [12].
  • Pavement sections utilizing higher fly-ash contents exhibited noticeably lower deflection ranges than those relying on higher cement and less fly ash, indicating reduced structural deformation under load [12].
  • A higher proportion of fly ash relative to cement consistently produced lower deflection values, whereas mixes with more cement and less fly ash exhibited the greatest curling and warping tendencies [12].
  • Concrete slabs constructed with newer mix designs using about 20% fly-ash replacement experienced less curling and warping than those built with older pre-QMC mixes containing approximately 15% fly ash [22].
  • The C-SUD mix design, which permitted up to 35% fly-ash replacement, showed the highest propensity for curling and warping among county roads and city streets, suggesting that other design factors can outweigh the benefits of higher fly-ash content [22].
  • Seven-year deflection data for slabs with documented fly-ash quantities showed no clear correlation between specific fly-ash dosages and slab performance, indicating that the impact of fly ash on deformation is nuanced and dependent on broader mix design factors [22].
  • Static Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) moduli measurements revealed significant variability in subgrade stiffness across different sites, ranging from fair to excellent performance levels [33].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a field-based evaluation framework that treats fly-ash-stabilized subgrade as a distinct support condition, utilizing an integrated suite of in-situ tests to simultaneously characterize mechanical stiffness and hydraulic conductivity [33]. Methodological innovation is achieved through the deployment of a newly developed core-hole permeameter alongside conventional deflection and penetration devices, enabling direct derivation of design inputs such as drainage coefficients from field measurements [33]. Reproducibility relies on blending self-cementing fly ash with fine-grained subgrade soils at specific proportions, followed by controlled compaction and curing protocols that account for moisture optimization and sulfur content monitoring [33].

Life-Cycle Cost Analysis

Part of: Concrete Pavement Sustainability
cost-analysiseconomicslccapavement-engineering
Also known as: LCCA
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-29 02:56:42+00:00

Life-cycle cost analysis (LCCA) is an economic evaluation method that compares the total cost of ownership for different pavement alternatives over their service life, including initial construction, maintenance, rehabilitation, and user costs. In the internal curing study, LCCA was used to assess the value proposition of using IC technology in concrete overlays. [1]

Project Cost Components

Economic evaluations of RCA in new concrete paving mixtures require assessing the costs associated with production and verification testing against alternative applications, as contractors prioritize uses that minimize overall project expenses while managing risks related to supply availability and material processing [2]. These evaluations must account for specific material and logistical expenses, such as sand at $24/yd³ and LWFA at $37.35/yd³, alongside additional handling costs of $2,350 per site [2]. Furthermore, transportation costs vary by location, reaching $27.50/yd³ in Washington County and $35.00/yd³ in Winneshiek County [2].

Contractor perspective when considering RCA [2]

Rehabilitation and Maintenance Assumptions

The economic assessment for internally cured concrete overlays relies on predicted maintenance cost reductions derived from laboratory-measured permeability improvements rather than structural performance gains, as mechanistic modeling shows no significant difference in structural capacity under low traffic loads [1]. This approach isolates the durability benefits of internal curing to justify long-term savings despite higher initial material costs [1]. Rehabilitation assumptions include a patch area of 2% of surface at regular intervals, with rehabilitation costs consisting of $3/yd² (patching) + $170/yd² (grinding). While the conventional (CC) rehabilitation interval is 20 years, the IC rehabilitation interval is 27 years (extended due to improved permeability).

Maintenance thresholds are defined by International Roughness Index (IRI) values, with major maintenance triggered when IRI reaches 130 or 140 inches per mile depending on traffic volume, after which smoothness is restored to half that threshold value [3].

IRI of conventionally cured pavements over design life [3]

Economic Indicators and Metrics

Economic Indicators

  • Net Present Value (NPV): Sum of discounted future costs
  • Equivalent Annual Annuity (EAA): Annualized cost over pavement life
  • Discount rate: 4% (per Sompura & Avetisyan 2017)

Economic analyses distinguish between microeconomic determinations, which estimate the impact of engineering details like longitudinal tie-bars and drainable bases on materials and labor costs, and macroeconomic determinations that compare long-life solutions against short-life options such as UTW or rehabilitation using recycled materials. [4]

The US Office of Management and Budget (OMB) recommends a real discount rate of 0.7% for 30-year cost-effectiveness analysis, though sensitivity analyses often test variations to account for uncertainty in long-term economic evaluations. [3]

Sensitivity analyses reveal that variations in total construction costs significantly impact the net present value savings of thinner pavements, with potential NPV savings increasing by two to five times if construction costs vary by ±20%. [3]

Residual serviceable life serves as the primary component of salvage value to account for differences in remaining pavement life between alternative design strategies at the end of an analysis period, whereas residual value from material recycling typically has little effect on results. [3]

Cost stream over the lifecycle of a pavement [3]

Different 18 Kip axle equivalency factors (ESALs) must be applied for flexible and rigid pavements because each pavement type experiences a different loss of serviceability from identical vehicles. [5]

Economic evaluations establish a specific annual average daily traffic (AADT) threshold of 3,300 vehicles to determine the viability of high-cost polymer overlays. Above this traffic volume, the total life-cycle cost of the LMC-VE overlay is projected to be lower than that of alternative concrete designs due to accumulated user savings outweighing initial construction premiums. [6]

Determining AADT threshold for LMC-VE overlay [6]

Analytical Methods for Life-Cycle Cost Analysis

Life-cycle cost analysis for whitetopping overlays requires precise predictions of loading stresses, which can be modeled using three-dimensional finite element programs like ABAQUS or ANSYS to account for the viscoelastic behavior of asphalt subbases and interface conditions between layers [7]. In mechanistic-based pavement management systems, these life-cycle cost calculations are performed at the end of each simulation cycle to compare alternative strategies against terminating distress limits [7]. Advanced life-cycle cost procedures incorporate user costs derived from specific performance metrics such as smoothness, skid resistance, noise levels, traffic shutdowns, and rolling resistance to more accurately reflect the interrelationships between product cost and pavement performance [4]. To evaluate these relationships, optimization tools for concrete pavement life-cycle decisions integrate structural design, material selection, construction, preservation, rehabilitation, and user impacts into high-speed software programs to evaluate the cost-to-performance relationship of various solutions [4]. Furthermore, life-cycle cost procedures can integrate protocols for assessing risk based on life-cycle analysis (LCA) tools, such as PaLATE or FHWA-funded comprehensive sustainability models, to evaluate the social, environmental, and economic benefits of recycled materials over their service life [2]. Specific modeling approaches include the Life-365 Service Life Prediction Model, which is utilized to generate lifecycle cost projections by integrating four distinct modules: project parameters, exposure conditions, concrete mixture properties, and individual repair costs [8].

Sample composite pavement model [7]

Regarding specific pavement configurations and selection methods, numerical analysis using ISLAB2005 and EverFE 2.25 Finite Element Analysis (FEA) software validated that widened jointed plain concrete pavements with skewed joints have a higher potential for developing longitudinal cracking compared to those with rectangular joints [9]. Additionally, statistical crosstabulation analysis indicates that panel width selection is influenced by multiple factors beyond thickness, including traffic volume, truck percentage, ease of construction, and combined construction and maintenance costs [9]. For comparing reconstruction alternatives, agencies such as MDOT utilize the Equivalent Uniform Annual Cost (EUAC) calculation method, where the pavement alternative with the lowest EUAC is selected based on department policy [5]. Deterministic analysis of polymer concrete overlays reveals that while the LMC-VE alternative incurs the highest agency cost due to elevated initial construction expenses, it achieves the lowest user costs by enabling a rapid traffic opening time of approximately three to four hours; this reduction in work zone duration significantly lowers traffic control expenditures compared to conventional low-slump PCC and HPC overlays [6].

Costs of alternatives obtained from the probabilistic analysis: (a) agency costs and (b) user costs [6]

Material and Design Variables

Mechanistic design models for doweled joints require inputs such as slab thickness, modulus of subgrade reaction, and the radius of the loaded area to calculate load transfer efficiency [10], noting that GFRP dowels with a 38 mm diameter provide higher shear stiffness and lower bearing stresses on concrete compared to standard 32 mm epoxy-coated steel dowels [10]. Furthermore, faulting prediction models are significantly influenced by site conditions including traffic, climate, and subgrade, along with design features such as dowel diameter, subdrainage, joint spacing, base type, and slab welding [10]. Regarding material composition, the integration of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash, GGBFS, and silica fume can significantly alter the heat signature and hydration kinetics of concrete mixtures, which directly impacts thermal cracking risks and long-term durability [11]; consequently, regression models utilizing parameters like fly ash calcium oxide content and GGBFS fineness allow engineers to predict key engineering properties including time to maximum temperature and area under the curve during hydration [11]. Additionally, internal curing using superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) can be implemented by adding additional mix water that is absorbed by the SAP before setting, a method distinct from substituting prewetted lightweight fine aggregate [12].

In terms of application and structural design, life-cycle cost analysis for recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) applications must account for variable quality and consistency, as lack of uniformity increases the risk of concrete rejection or repair costs due to failures in meeting slump, strength, and air content specifications [2]. For whitetopping overlays, structural design relies on backcalculating layer moduli from Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) data, specifically using maximum deflection and deflection basin area measurements standardized for a 9000-pound load [7]. Within these overlays, structural macrofibers in concrete increase fatigue capacity and fracture toughness, which reduces overlay design thickness requirements while controlling differential slab movement caused by curling and warping; these fibers hold cracks tightly together, thereby minimizing maintenance costs associated with crack sealing and patching over the life cycle [13].

Key Results: IHRB TR-746 Study

Economic evaluations of pavement designs reveal significant variations in costs and longevity. For Washington, the CC section has an initial cost of $523,545/mile, an NPV of $551,878/mile, and an EAA of $24,394/mile, while the IC section has an initial cost of $535,526/mile, an NPV of $555,437/mile, and an EAA of $23,185/mile. In Winneshiek, the CC section features an initial cost of $578,022/mile, an NPV of $607,704/mile, and an EAA of $25,734/mile, whereas the IC section has an initial cost of $595,652/mile, an NPV of $616,512/mile, and an EAA of $24,608/mile. Despite initial cost increases of 2.3% (Washington) and 3.1% (Winneshiek), EAA calculations showed annual savings of ~$1,100/mile for IC sections due to extended maintenance intervals [1]. Regarding reinforcement, the use of dowel bars increases initial concrete pavement cost by approximately 7.8%, yet over a 25-year service life, non-doweled pavements incur costs that are approximately 13.1% higher than doweled ones due to the latter’s extended lifespan [10]. Furthermore, the life of doweled pavement is estimated to last approximately 2.5 times longer than non-doweled pavement prior to any maintenance or rehabilitation [10].

Internally cured concrete offers substantial durability benefits; service life predictions indicate a significant extension of approximately 18.7 years compared to control sections, though initial cumulative present values remain higher until approximately the year 2060 [8]. The use of internally cured concrete in continuously reinforced pavements leads to tighter crack openings and reduced punch-out failures, while also decreasing upward slab curling caused by long-term drying shrinkage [12]. Additionally, internal curing can reduce early age autogenous shrinkage by more than 80% compared to noninternally cured concretes, potentially tripling the service life of bridge decks [12]. For widened concrete pavements, economic evaluations reveal that while 9- and 10-inch thick 12-foot panels exhibit the highest specific maintenance costs, their overall life-cycle maintenance cost is lower than wider 15-foot panels, which incur average costs 1.7 times higher [9]. Life-cycle cost analyses of widened concrete pavements identify three principal advantages: extended service life, reduced shoulder maintenance expenses, and enhanced safety resulting from the elimination of hazardous edge dropoffs [9].

Lifecycle cost [8]

Optimization of joint spacing and thickness also impacts performance. Optimizing transverse joint spacing to 20 feet can extend concrete overlay service life by nearly 20 years compared to shorter spacings that exhibit premature deterioration, achieving a Pavement Condition Index (PCI) of 60 at year 45; this improvement is attributed to reduced maintenance frequency and minimized distress associated with ineffective crack deployment in narrower panels [13]. Increasing bonded concrete overlay thickness from 4 inches to 6 inches extends the service life before reaching a PCI threshold of 60 from approximately 25 years to 37 years, while maintaining International Roughness Index (IRI) values below 170 in/mi throughout that period, demonstrating that thicker overlays provide superior long-term performance metrics [13]. Conversely, joint spacing designs significantly influence life-cycle costs, as shorter spacings (5.5 to 6 ft) may fail to deploy cracks effectively under saw cuts, leading to dominant joint behavior, additional faulting, and increased maintenance expenses on low-volume roadways; thus, determining minimum optimal joint spacing is critical for thin bonded concrete overlays to avoid these distress mechanisms [13].

Assumptions and Limitations

The analytical framework assumes a propagation period of 20 years, defined as the duration between the onset of initial steel corrosion and the point at which concrete requires structural repair [8]. Within this model, little structural benefit is expected from IC mixtures, so savings are based on reduced maintenance resulting from improved permeability. These permeability improvements lead to a service life extension from 20 to 27 years.

The framework assumes the unit rehabilitation cost is the same per yd² for all sections, though it does not account for user costs or traffic delay costs.

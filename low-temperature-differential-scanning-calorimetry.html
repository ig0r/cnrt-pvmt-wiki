Low-Temperature Differential Scanning Calorimetry (LT-DSC)

Part of: Premature Joint Deterioration
analytical-techniquecalcium-oxychlorideconcrete-durabilitytest-method
Also known as: LT-DSC, differential scanning calorimetry, low-temperature DSC
Created 2026-07-22 00:00:00+00:00 · Updated 2026-07-30 00:04:50+00:00

Analytical method proposed by Monical et al. (2016) to quantify calcium oxychloride formation for cementitious materials in the presence of calcium chloride. The method uses differential scanning calorimetry at low temperatures to measure heat absorption during the calcium oxychloride phase transformation.

Overview and Standards

The LT-DSC testing protocol follows the AASHTO T 365 Standard Method of Test for Quantifying Calcium Oxychloride Formation Potential of Cementitious Pastes Exposed to Deicing Salts, utilizing a DSC 25 instrument equipped with a low-temperature kit. [1]

presents the amount of potential CaOXY formation in the treated concrete discs immersed in the salt solutions as determined by LT-DSC in accordance with AASHTO T 365. Figure 12. Potential CaOXY formation results [1]

Sample Preparation

  1. Sample preparation: Grind concrete to powder passing through 75-um sieve (No. 200)
  2. Store in sealed container to prevent carbonation until testing
  3. Prepare 20% CaCl2 solution (75.49% CaCl2 and 24.51% water by mass)
  4. Mix 10 +/- 1 mg of powder specimen with solution (1:1 powder-to-solution mass ratio)
  5. Hold at room temperature for approximately 1 hour to dissipate hydration heat

Testing Protocol (LT-DSC)

  1. Reduce temperature to -90 deg C at 3 deg C/min
  2. Cycle temperature from -90 deg C to -70 deg C back to -90 deg C at 3 deg C/min until frozen
  3. Heat sample at 0.25 deg C/min to 50 deg C, then return to room temperature

Quantitative Analysis and Calculation

The energy associated with calcium oxychloride formation is estimated by measuring the magnitude of the shift in cumulative heat slopes before and after the phase transformation, specifically observing the drop in the cumulative heat curve. To evaluate the heat absorbed during the calcium oxychloride melting phase, the heat flow versus time curve is integrated to determine the energy associated with the phase transformation [2].

The drop associated with calcium oxychloride formation in cumulative heat curve [2]

The amount of calcium oxychloride is calculated using the formula m_oxy = Delta H_oxy / L_oxy, where m_oxy represents grams of calcium oxychloride per gram of cementitious binder, Delta H_oxy is the latent heat absorbed during phase transformation (J/g of cementitious material), and L_oxy is the specific latent heat of calcium oxychloride phase transformation = 186 J/g.

Experimental Results

The QMC mixture contains 30.5% calcium oxychloride and absorbs 11.36 J/g of heat, whereas the M-QMC mixture contains 8.4% calcium oxychloride and absorbs 3.36 J/g of heat. Compared to QMC, the M-QMC mixture showed a 72% reduction in calcium oxychloride formation, and these results are consistent with paste expansion test observations. Further research is needed to correlate calcium oxychloride amounts with field performance.

Practical Significance and Applications

This technique provides a quantitative measurement of calcium oxychloride formation potential, complementing qualitative paste expansion test results [1]. It can quantify calcium oxychloride potential in samples exposed to 20% CaCl2 salt solution at 4℃ for extended durations, such as one year [1]. The method is applicable to evaluating the effectiveness of surface sealants, as it clearly indicates benefits in reducing oxychloride formation risk by measuring potential formation in treated concrete discs immersed in salt solutions [1].

Scaling and visual rating of specimens after 140 F-T cycles [1]

Furthermore, the technique can be used to evaluate effectiveness of fly ash dosage in mitigating joint deterioration and is useful for developing performance-based specifications for concrete mixtures [1]. It also serves as a practical screening tool for selecting specific portland cement and supplementary cementitious material (SCM) systems to optimize composition under anticipated deicing conditions [3].

Regulatory Requirements

AASHTO R 101-22 mandates that calcium oxychloride content must remain below 0.15 g per 100 g of cementitious paste as determined by AASHTO T 365. [3]

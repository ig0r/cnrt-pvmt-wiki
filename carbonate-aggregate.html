Carbonate Aggregate

Part of: Aggregates is-type-of
Carbonate Aggregate is a type of Aggregates.
aggregateconceptconcretedurabilityspecifications
Also known as: Carbonate Content, Dolomitic Aggregate
Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T05:31:25Z

Carbonate aggregate refers to crushed sedimentary rock materials, primarily composed of limestone or dolostone, that serve as the coarse fraction in concrete and granular road surfaces. These aggregates are characterized by their mineralogical composition of calcite or dolomite, which establishes a characteristic grain density and influences overall porosity compared to siliceous alternatives [1] [3]. The internal pore structure originates from the sedimentary fabric and diagenetic history of the source rock, resulting in textures that range from coarse-grained to fine-grained microporous networks dominated by sub-micron pores [2] [3]. Due to higher water absorption and a significant volume of capillary-sized micropores, carbonate aggregates are more susceptible to internal stress buildup during freeze-thaw cycles, which can lead to durability issues such as D-cracking [1] [4].

Key findings

  • Ground-penetrating radar reliably located aggregate-induced cracking in concrete pavements using crushed limestone, though it missed distress caused by paste-related issues [7].
  • Concrete made with crushed-limestone aggregate consistently underperformed river-gravel mixes, exhibiting lower abrasion resistance and significantly reduced seven-day compressive strength [6].
  • Limestone concrete demonstrated a relatively low coefficient of thermal expansion but possessed roughly half the thermal conductivity of standard MEPDG default values for such materials [14].
  • Most carbonate aggregates exhibited lower absorption and pore index values than natural gravel, with a combination of low absorption and high specific gravity reliably signaling good freeze-thaw durability [1].
  • Carbonate aggregates consistently lost more mass and showed inferior frost resistance compared to non-carbonate aggregates in multiple durability tests, a performance tied to higher volumes of micropores [4].
  • Concrete mixtures using crushed limestone performed markedly better in freeze-thaw tests than those with non-carbonate gravel, showing less salt buildup and fewer signs of interfacial-zone attack [9].
  • Carbonate aggregate sources are never uniform but contain distinct rock types whose textures govern suitability, with specific porosity thresholds identified for limestone and dolostone via helium porosimetry [2].
  • The third-generation Iowa Pore Index successfully separated dolostone and limestone samples based on macroporosity, identifying lithology-specific transition times for water intrusion [3].

Pore Structure Characterization and Durability Implications

Carbonate aggregates are sedimentary rocks, primarily limestone or dolostone, whose internal pore structure is dictated by their depositional texture and subsequent diagenetic alterations [2]. These materials typically exhibit higher porosity than siliceous counterparts, with a pore network dominated by sub-micron capillary pores that form during deposition or secondary processes like dissolution [1] [3]. The abundance of these fine micropores facilitates water retention and creates internal hydraulic pressures during freezing, which significantly reduces resistance to freeze-thaw damage compared to coarser-pored aggregates [4].

Research problem — Current specifications for coarse aggregate often rely on restrictive limits that may unnecessarily reject usable carbonate sources, raising concerns about whether these materials can meet durability requirements despite their finer pore structures [1]. The inherent heterogeneity of carbonate aggregates and the limitations of standard testing methods make it difficult to reliably distinguish between durable and non-durable materials, leading to potential misclassification and premature pavement failure [2] [3]. There is a critical need for quick and reliable screening tools that accurately correlate pore structure characteristics with freeze-thaw performance to prevent costly repairs and ensure long-term pavement safety [4] [10].

State of the art — The current state of the art relies on a composite testing regime that combines rapid, automated Iowa Pore Index (IPI) screening with complementary petrophysical and mineralogical analyses to assess pore structure and predict freeze-thaw durability [1] [2] [3]. While traditional specifications use fixed absorption limits, recent consensus favors a nuanced acceptance framework that correlates low secondary IPI values with high specific gravity and low absorption to identify durable aggregates [1] [4]. Mercury intrusion porosimetry remains the standard for detailed pore-size characterization, particularly for identifying critical sub-micron pores that govern damage susceptibility in carbonate-rich fractions [1] [2] [3].

Findings from the reports

  • Evaluated 21 carbonate-aggregate sources using the automated 3-G Iowa Pore Index (IPI) device, confirming that it produces primary and secondary load indices essentially identical to those from the legacy system [3].
  • Observed that macropore intrusion terminates rapidly within a lithology-dependent window of approximately 3 seconds for limestone, 5 seconds for intermediate carbonate, and 8 seconds for dolostone [3].
  • Found that carbonate aggregates exhibit higher water absorption and lower specific gravity than non-carbonate rocks, with a very strong statistical correlation between water absorption and the combined primary and secondary pore index values [1].
  • Demonstrated that low water absorption (below 1.75%) combined with high specific gravity (above 2.60) reliably predicts low secondary pore index values and acceptable freeze-thaw durability [1].
  • Established that carbonate aggregate sources are never uniform but contain multiple rock types, where specific textures such as sparite matrices in limestone or porous intercrystalline zones in dolostone govern their suitability for road construction [2].
  • Identified a critical pore-throat diameter range of 0.02–0.1 µm, noting that aggregates with modal pore-throat diameters exceeding this range tend to perform better in durability applications [2].
  • Measured higher primary load indices for dolostone samples compared to limestone when tested in smaller chambers, while secondary load indices remained essentially unchanged across varying intrusion pressures [3].

Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a geological calibration of the Iowa Pore Index by integrating systematic rock-typing with linear models that translate primary and secondary intrusion values into quantitative helium porosity for distinct carbonate lithologies [2] [9]. Methodological innovation is achieved through the development of a third-generation automated testing system that utilizes high-frequency data capture and lithology-specific transition times to replace legacy manual protocols [3]. The approach establishes a rigorous, multi-method validation framework by correlating rapid pore-index screening with detailed mercury intrusion porosimetry and standard durability conditioning tests to define critical pore-thresholds for freeze-thaw performance [1] [4].

Implications — Specifications should replace single carbonate-percentage limits with combined criteria based on specific gravity and absorption to better predict frost resistance [1]. The Iowa Pore Index should be adopted as a rapid screening tool, using distinct cutoffs for limestone versus dolostone to identify materials prone to D-cracking and ice-segregation damage [2] [4]. Aggregates must be pre-qualified by separating heterogeneous sources into constituent lithologies for individual evaluation, with conflicting results triggering supplemental petrophysical analysis to isolate nondurable fractions [2] [3].

Limitations — The specific pore size ranges that most significantly influence freeze-thaw durability remain unclear, and the correlation between absorption and secondary pore index weakens when carbonate aggregates are analyzed separately [1]. Experimental results may not extrapolate to larger aggregate sizes because testing was confined to a narrow size fraction, and definitive safe limits for carbonate content cannot be established without field performance data [1]. The Iowa Pore Index is limited by its operation at a single pressure and fixed time intervals, which may fail to capture macroporosity differences or misclassify carbonates due to lithology-dependent intrusion times [2] [3]. Sampling bias from excluding small fractions and using only active quarry sites with low microporosity limits the ability to explore less favorable property combinations or assess the influence of minor rock types [2]. Test system artifacts, such as inconsistent chamber expansion volumes and variable soak times, along with the masking effect of dominant high-quality material in mixed sources, reduce confidence in predictive durability assessments [3] [4].

Thermal Properties and General Performance Assessment

Research problem — The default thermal property inputs in pavement design guides fail to accurately represent the coefficient of thermal expansion and conductivity of concrete containing carbonate aggregate, potentially leading to suboptimal designs and premature distress [14]. Carbonate aggregates exhibit distinct durability challenges, including reduced compressive strength and abrasion resistance in pervious mixes, as well as susceptibility to freeze-thaw damage and reactive degradation that compromise structural integrity [6] [8] [9]. There is a critical need to assess the general performance of carbonate aggregates under varying environmental conditions to ensure material selection supports long-term pavement service life and prevents early failure mechanisms [7].

State of the art — Current practice treats crushed limestone as a viable coarse aggregate for pervious concrete, though its lower unit weight and higher abrasion loss compared to river gravel necessitate careful quality evaluation and the use of admixtures like silica fume or latex to achieve adequate strength [6]. Field performance data indicates that limestone aggregates can deliver durability comparable to blast-furnace slag when construction variables such as moisture conditioning, air-void systems, and water-cement ratios are properly controlled, rather than relying on aggregate type alone [8]. The emerging consensus emphasizes rigorous testing across regions to establish minimum property specifications for carbonate aggregates, focusing on proper moisture management and supplementary cementitious materials to ensure freeze-thaw resistance and long-term durability [6] [8].

Findings from the reports

  • Ground-penetrating radar reliably located aggregate-induced cracking in pavements using crushed limestone, while petrographic analysis confirmed that the observed internal cracks were due to failure of coarse carbonate aggregates [7].
  • Carbonate-based crushed limestone aggregate exhibited lower unit weight and poorer abrasion resistance than river gravel, resulting in pervious concrete mixes with higher void ratios and reduced early-age compressive strength [6].
  • A modified dry-mix cement coating procedure failed to produce a significant strength gain for limestone mixes due to the aggregate’s textured surface, whereas it improved strength in river gravel mixes [6].
  • Adding sand or latex admixtures increased compressive strength and reduced permeability for both limestone and river gravel aggregates, confirming that supplemental materials can mitigate the inherent weakness of carbonate aggregate [6].
  • In freeze-thaw testing, concrete mixes containing crushed limestone outperformed those with non-carbonate gravel by exhibiting significantly higher durability, fewer pop-out failures, and reduced salt accumulation in the interfacial transition zone [9].
  • Some limestone particles located near joint depths developed microcracking and showed susceptibility to cyclic freeze-thaw damage when saturated, despite the overall superior performance of limestone mixes compared to gravel [9].
  • Concrete made with limestone exhibited a relatively low coefficient of thermal expansion that was close to default model values, but its measured thermal conductivity was roughly half the standard default input for Portland cement concrete [14].
  • Crushed carbonate coarse aggregate in several studied pavements was found to be notably hard, sound, and durable, indicating it was not the source of observed distress in those specific cases [8].

Novelty & rigor — Research has introduced a systematic evaluation of how crushed limestone influences the thermal expansion and conductivity of concrete, revealing that these properties deviate significantly from standard pavement design defaults [14]. The methodological approach is distinguished by its direct comparison of carbonate aggregates against siliceous controls under identical mix conditions to isolate the specific impact on freeze-thaw durability and interfacial transition zone characteristics [9] [14]. Replication of these thermal assessments relies on standardized length-change testing procedures applied to saturated field cores alongside controlled thermal conductivity measurements on fabricated specimens [14].

Performance assessments reveal that while some studies identify carbonate aggregates as inferior to river gravel in terms of abrasion resistance and early-age strength for pervious concrete, others report superior freeze-thaw durability compared to non-carbonate gravels. Field investigations further contrast these findings by linking specific carbonate aggregates to long-term pavement distress and cracking, while other core analyses describe the material as hard, sound, and durable without direct causation of observed failures. [6] [7] [8] [9]

Implications — Designers should replace generic thermal expansion coefficients with aggregate-specific values for carbonate-rich mixes to prevent underestimating thermal expansion effects, while retaining default conductivity values until more reliable testing methods are established [14]. Pavement specifications must prioritize low-porosity carbonate aggregates over marginal non-carbonate options to enhance joint durability and resistance to freeze-thaw damage, particularly in environments with aggressive deicing salts [9]. When using crushed limestone in pervious concrete, designers should supplement the material with higher-quality aggregates or admixtures and enforce stricter void content controls to mitigate reduced strength and durability caused by limited cement-paste bonding [6].

Limitations — The reliability of ground-penetrating radar for detecting distress in carbonate aggregates is compromised by environmental variables and operational constraints, preventing its universal application [7]. Conclusions regarding durability and strength are provisional due to narrow sample diversity, unexpected material alterations during testing, and the inability to isolate aggregate effects from other mix design factors [6] [8] [9]. Thermal conductivity measurements lack field representativeness because they rely on limited core samples and test methods designed for homogeneous materials rather than actual pavement conditions [14].
